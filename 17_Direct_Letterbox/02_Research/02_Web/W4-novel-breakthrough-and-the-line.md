# W4 — Novel & Breakthrough Approaches to Mailing Someone Data About What They Own, and Where the Line Is

**Research date:** 2026-08-08
**For:** Fields Real Estate — direct letterbox programme (`17_Direct_Letterbox`)
**Question:** What novel and breakthrough approaches exist, across all industries, for mailing someone personalised data about something they already own — and where has that approach backfired?

## Executive summary — the twelve findings that matter

1. **The canonical case is real, measured, and smaller than its reputation.** Opower's Home Energy Reports cut electricity use **2.0%** in the first ten sites (Allcott 2011, 600,000 households) — but **1.31%** across all 111 sites and 8.6 million households, and the academic proof-of-concept that started it all was **3–6× larger than even the first ten**. Effects shrink at every step away from a hand-picked population.

2. **A one-shot mailer is close to worthless.** A single report's effect peaks ~10 days after arrival and **decays to nothing within about three months**. A two-year programme is **2.5–4.2× more cost effective** than a one-shot. But there is **no habituation even at year five**. Fund a cadence or don't start.

3. **Low opt-out is not consent.** **34%** of Opower recipients had weakly negative willingness to pay — they'd rather not receive it even free — while only **0.08–3.3%** opted out. Any dashboard treating "nobody complained" as validation is measuring inertia.

4. **The comparison is the product, not the irritant.** **49%** of recipients would like the report *less* without the neighbour comparison; only 11% would like it more. **73%** said it was useful. Strip out the comparative data and you remove the thing people value.

5. **Comparative data moves outliers and barely moves anyone else.** Opower top decile **6.3%** vs bottom decile ~0%. HMRC's "you are in the small minority who haven't paid" norm: **+5.1 pp** vs **+1.3 pp** for a generic norm. Ferraro & Price's water comparison: ~94% larger relative effect among high users. Targeting is worth more than creative — profiling raised Opower's effect **74%** and cut cost per outcome **43%**.

6. **And the outliers are the ones who complain.** Opower's opt-outs skewed to *higher-usage* households — the exact segment producing the effect. Costa & Kahn found Republican households **increased** consumption 1% in response to the same mailer that cut liberal households' use 3%, and were more likely to opt out and to report disliking it. Where a recipient rejects your frame, the mailer works against you.

7. **The realistic effect band is 1–8 percentage points, and only where the data reveals a gap.** APRA compelled Australian super trustees to write to ~1 million members saying their fund had officially underperformed: **7% of accounts moved**. FCA's insurance disclosure got **+1.2 pp** in the smallest price-rise quartile and **+4.7 pp** in the largest — and **zero** where prices hadn't risen. Trial effects routinely fail to survive scale-up; FCA's **+3.2 pp** became **−0.8 to −3.0 pp** nationally.

8. **Information alone underperforms; the mechanism is comparison, or removing the next step.** Ferraro & Price's technical-advice-only arm fell *below its own pre-registered minimum detectable effect*. Nine systematic reviews of personalised health-risk letters: "no evidence that risk information had strong or consistent effects." The large effects in that literature come from mailing the **instrument** (FIT kits RR 1.36, HPV kits RR 1.51–1.93), not the number.

9. **Personalisation is an inverted U.** In a Facebook field test, moderate personalisation beat none (0.017% → 0.077% CTR) but **high personalisation — putting the person's own attributes in the ad — cut it back to 0.032%**, a 58% fall, and in a second category landed below the no-personalisation baseline. A photo of the recipient's house sits at the high end of that scale. Do not assume it helps, and do not stack it with every other maximal-personalisation element at once.

10. **The line between welcome and creepy is provenance, not depth.** Acceptability is explained by *where the data came from* and *how it was obtained* — not by how much you know. **Disclosing an inference is worse than saying nothing** (2.52 vs 2.96); disclosing a retrieved fact is free-to-positive. Naming an acceptable source erased the entire covert-collection penalty in one experiment and lifted clicks **50%** in another. Disclosure is a multiplier on provenance, in whichever direction it points.

11. **In every enforcement action found, the violation attached to the comparison or savings claim — never the estimate itself.** Opendoor's $62m FTC order (54,689 homeowners refunded) turned on a net-proceeds chart and overstated agent costs, not a bad AVM. Purplebricks' banned claim failed on the evidence chain for a specific number. Two regulators on two continents independently held that **fine print does not cure a headline**.

12. **The hardest Australian constraint is the list, not the message.** Australia's largest property data business states on the record to the ACCC that Valuer General and land-titles licences **prohibit using owner information for direct marketing**, and that such data cannot be sold, sub-licensed or downloaded, under audit and termination rights. The Spam Act does not cover post — but that is not a licence, it just means the binding constraints are elsewhere (§6).

# 1. The Opower evidence, in depth

Opower (founded 2007, acquired by Oracle for ~$532M in 2016) mailed printed **Home Energy Reports (HERs)** to utility customers. Each report showed the household's own electricity use, compared it to ~100 geographically-nearest neighbours matched on house size and heating type, gave a normative label ("Great" / "Good" / "Below Average"), and listed tailored action steps. By 2012, 6.2 million households across 85 utilities were receiving them; Oracle later claimed deployment to 100 utilities and 60M+ homes.

Scale is confirmed in the peer-reviewed record, not just the marketing: Allcott & Rogers (2014) state "There are now **6.2 million households** receiving home energy reports" [PRIMARY DATA].

This is the closest existing analogue to what Fields is contemplating, and — unusually for a marketing intervention — it was measured by economists across **111 randomised controlled trials covering 8.6 million households**. The evidence is unusually good. It is also more sobering than the folklore.

**Sources for the frame:** [Oracle press release, 2016](https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/economy/oracle-agrees-to-buy-arlington-energy-data-firm-opower-for-532-million/2016/05/02/83739416-107f-11e6-93ae-50921721165d_story.html) [VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]; [Tools of Change Opower case study, 2012](https://toolsofchange.com/userfiles/Opower%20Landmark%20Case%20Study%202012.pdf) [VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]; [CNBC, 2022](https://www.cnbc.com/2022/07/14/how-opower-sold-to-oracle-has-helped-save-3-billion-in-energy-bills.html) [VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM].

## 1.1 The headline effect: 2.0%, and it shrinks as you scale

**Allcott, H. (2011), "Social Norms and Energy Conservation", *Journal of Public Economics* 95(9-10): 1082-1095.** [PRIMARY DATA] — [full text PDF hosted by Oracle](https://www.oracle.com/us/industries/utilities/social-norms-energy-cons-jpe-5225418.pdf)

> "Using data from randomized natural field experiments at 600,000 treatment and control households across the United States, I estimate that the average program reduces energy consumption by 2.0%."

Detail worth having exactly right:

| Finding | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Unweighted mean ATE across experiments | **2.03%** | Allcott 2011, §3.2 |
| Range across individual experiments | **1.37% to 3.32%** | Allcott 2011, Table 4 |
| Absolute size of the 2% effect | **0.62 kWh/day** — "equivalent to turning off an air conditioner that would have been on for 37 min each day" | Allcott 2011, §3.3.1 |
| Equivalent short-run price increase | **11–20%** | Allcott 2011, §3.3.2 (using Reiss & White 60-day elasticity of −0.10 to −0.18) |
| Equivalent *sustained* price increase | **5.2%** | Allcott 2011, §3.3.2 (long-run elasticity −0.39) |
| Cost per report | **"on the order of one dollar"** | Allcott 2011, §3.3.3 |
| Cost effectiveness | **3.31 cents per kWh saved** (unweighted mean) | Allcott 2011, §3.3.3 |
| Effect of mailing frequency | Monthly/bimonthly mean ATE **2.2%** vs quarterly **1.7%**; where frequency was *randomised*, monthly caused a **0.5 percentage point larger ATE** than quarterly | Allcott 2011, §3.2 |

**The shrinkage chain is the most important single fact in this section.** The same intervention got weaker every time it was scaled to a less-selected population:

**Allcott, H. (2015), "Site Selection Bias in Program Evaluation", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 130(3): 1117-1165.** [PRIMARY DATA] — [working paper PDF](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/Allcott_SiteSelectionBias.pdf)

- Academic proof-of-concept (Schultz et al. 2007 / Nolan et al. 2008): ~300 hand-delivered door hangers in **a wealthy California suburb**. Allcott: "the treatment effects are **three to six times larger** than even the first ten Opower programs."
- Opower's **first ten sites**: ATE ≈ 2.0% (this is what Allcott 2011 measured, and what the world quotes).
- **All 111 sites**: mean ATE **1.31 percent**.
- Predicting later sites from the early microdata "over-predict[s] the mean average treatment effect (ATE) by **0.41 to 0.66 percentage points**" — i.e. a **~30–50% overstatement** on a ~1.3% base.
- Mechanism: "utilities in more environmentalist areas are more likely to adopt the program, and their customers are more responsive"; and within utilities, programs were first aimed "at higher-usage consumer subpopulations, [so] efficacy drops as the program is later expanded."

**Transfer to Fields:** any pilot you run on a hand-picked, high-engagement, high-value subset will overstate what a suburb-wide roll-out delivers, probably by a third to a half. Budget for the *later*-site number, not the pilot number.

## 1.2 Persistence and decay: what happens when you stop

**Allcott, H. & Rogers, T. (2014), "The Short-Run and Long-Run Effects of Behavioral Interventions: Experimental Evidence from Energy Conservation", *American Economic Review* 104(10): 3003-3037.** [PRIMARY DATA] — [PDF via J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/899%20Allcott%20and%20Rogers%20AER2014%20The%20Short-Run%20and%20Long-Run%20Effects%20of%20Behavioral%20Interventions.pdf) · [NBER w18492](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18492)

Design: three utility sites, **234,000 households**, daily meter data (225 million observations at one site), with treatment groups randomly assigned to be **discontinued after ~2 years** or **continued indefinitely**. This design is what makes the persistence answer credible.

**(a) "Action and backsliding" — the within-cycle sawtooth.**
- Effects appear **1–3 days** after predicted mail arrival.
- Consumption drops ~**0.2 kWh/day** in the days after arrival.
- "Conservation effort reaches its peak about **10 days after report arrival**, and consumers backslide after that point."
- For the quarterly group, "the treatment effect decays by almost 0.2 kWh/day between 10 days and 80 days after the report arrival" — i.e. **the entire effect of one mailer is gone within ~3 months** if not renewed.
- After the first four reports the sawtooth flattens: "the immediate consumption decreases after report arrivals are about **five times smaller** than they were initially." People stop being surprised.

**(b) Post-discontinuation persistence.**
- For groups discontinued after ~2 years, "the effects decay at about **10 to 20 percent per year** — four to eight times slower than the decay rate between the initial reports."
- Interpretation: repeated treatment builds a "capital stock" — physical (efficient appliances) or habit — that outlives the mail. Utility program-participation data confirmed *some* physical capital change but it "explains only a small share of the effects."
- Prior regulatory practice assumed **zero** persistence. That assumption "understates electricity cost savings over our four to five year samples by **more than a factor of two**, predicting **$41 to $63 per household versus the observed figures of $100 to $149**."

**(c) Habituation — people do NOT tune out.**
- "Treatment effects in the third through fifth years are **50 to 60 percent stronger if the intervention is continued** instead of discontinued." Five years in, the mail was still working.

**(d) Optimal frequency — the operationally decisive result.**
- A **one-shot** intervention: cost effectiveness **4.31 cents/kWh**.
- A **two-year** programme is "**between 2.5 and 4.2 times more cost effective** than the one-shot intervention."
- Reducing frequency after two years (Site 1 dropped to biannual) gave incremental cost effectiveness of **0.69 cents/kWh** — extraordinarily good.
- Overall: "program designers can improve cost effectiveness by a factor of **more than three** relative to a one-shot intervention by initially repeating the intervention and then reducing treatment frequency."

**Transfer to Fields:** a single mailer is close to worthless — it decays to nothing in ~3 months. The economics only work as a *cadence*: dense at first (monthly-ish for the first ~4 items, which is where the surprise value lives), then taper. "Send once and see" is the design most likely to produce a false negative.

## 1.3 The boomerang effect — real, and specifically fixed by design

**Schultz, Nolan, Cialdini, Goldstein & Griskevicius (2007), "The Constructive, Destructive, and Reconstructive Power of Social Norms", *Psychological Science* 18(5): 429-434.** [PRIMARY DATA] — [journal](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01917.x) · [PubMed 17576283](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576283/) · [2018 "Reprise"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1745691617693325)

This is the origin of the "boomerang". A **descriptive** norm alone ("your neighbours use X") made high users conserve **and made low users increase consumption toward the average**. Adding an **injunctive** norm — a hand-drawn smiley face signalling social approval for below-average use — **eliminated the boomerang** while preserving the saving among heavy users. That smiley face is why the Opower report carries "Great / Good / Below Average" labels rather than a bare number.

**Did the boomerang survive at Opower scale? Mostly no — because of the injunctive fix.** Allcott 2011 tested this directly:
- Quantile treatment effects: point estimates at the 2nd and 4th percentiles were positive (i.e. increased use) but "neither is statistically positive with 90% confidence."
- Regression discontinuity at the "Great"/"Good" boundary (20th percentile) and the "Good"/"Below Average" boundary: differential effects **larger than 0.16–0.20 percentage points can be ruled out**.
- Conclusion: "the descriptive norm boomerang effect is not strong enough to induce previously-low usage households to significantly increase usage."

**But it survived in a different cut — ideology.**

**Costa, D. & Kahn, M. (2013), "Energy Conservation 'Nudges' and Environmentalist Ideology: Evidence from a Randomized Residential Electricity Field Experiment", *JEEA* 11(3): 680-702.** [PRIMARY DATA] — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/jeea/article/11/3/680/2300535) · [NBER w15939](https://www.nber.org/papers/w15939)

- The nudge is "**two to four times more effective with political liberals than with conservatives**."
- A Democratic household buying renewable power, donating to environmental groups, in a liberal neighbourhood: **−3%**.
- A Republican household doing none of those: **+1% — consumption went UP.**
- "Political conservatives are more likely than liberals to **opt out** of receiving the home electricity report **and to report disliking** the report."

**Transfer to Fields:** the boomerang is not primarily about the number — it is about whether the recipient accepts the sender's implied standard. Where the recipient rejects the frame, the message actively backfires. A homeowner who reads "your home is positioned below the suburb median" as an insult, or as an agent angling for a listing, is the Republican household in this experiment. Comparative framing needs an injunctive/positive escape hatch for people on the wrong side of the average, exactly as the smiley face provided.

## 1.4 Heterogeneity: nearly all the effect comes from a minority

Allcott 2011, §4 [PRIMARY DATA]:
- Conditional ATEs by decile of baseline usage range from **"almost zero for the bottom decile" to 6.3% in the top decile**.
- Consequently: "if the OPOWER program were to be administered to half of the eligible population, **profiling would increase the ATE by 74%** relative to arbitrary assignment, thereby **reducing the cost per kilowatt-hour conserved by 43%**."

Confirmed and extended in **Knittel & Stolper, "Using Machine Learning to Target Treatment" (NBER w26531)** [PRIMARY DATA] — [PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26531/w26531.pdf).

**Transfer to Fields:** targeting is worth more than creative. Halving the mail volume and choosing the right half is a bigger lever than any headline rewrite. Fields already holds the data to do this (time held, equity position, renovation signals, prior sale price vs current comps).

## 1.5 The finding almost nobody quotes: a third of recipients did not want the mail

**Allcott, H. & Kessler, J. (2019), "The Welfare Effects of Nudges: A Case Study of Energy Use Social Comparisons", *AEJ: Applied Economics* 11(1): 236-276.** [PRIMARY DATA] — [NBER w21671 PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21671/w21671.pdf) · [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fapp.20170328) · RCT registry [AEARCTR-713](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/713)

They ran an incentive-compatible multiple price list on ~10,000 households: how much would you pay (or accept) to get / avoid another year of Home Energy Reports? Findings:

- Mean WTP for another year: **$2.97 unweighted, $2.81 re-weighted**.
- **"34 percent have weakly negative WTP — that is, they prefer not to be nudged even if the nudge is free."** (Most of that group is near-indifferent: 56% of the negative WTPs sit between $0 and −$1.)
- **"almost 60 percent of nudge recipients are not willing to pay the marginal social cost of the nudge."**
- But the top of the distribution is strong: **one in five would pay at least $9**, 4.8× marginal social cost.
- WTP is only **57% of the retail energy cost savings** — "the remaining 43 percent represents net financial, time, comfort, and psychological costs required to generate the energy savings." Standard evaluations ignore that 43%, which is why they overstate welfare gains **by a factor of 3.7** in-sample, and by up to **$620 million** across all HER programs.
- Targeting via a "smart default" ML algorithm would **double** the welfare gains.

**The two numbers to put side by side:**

> **34% had weakly negative willingness to pay. Between 0.08% and 3.3% actually opted out.**

Opt-out sources, both primary:
- Allcott & Kessler (2019), §V: in their experiment "only **0.08 percent** of households opted out."
- Allcott (2011), §2, across 17 randomised experiments: "cumulative opt-out rates range from **0.1% to 3.3%**." An earlier working-paper snapshot: "In the first six months of 2009, **247 households (0.6 percent of the Treatment group) opted out**" ([MIT CEEPR WP 09-014](https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/51712/2009-014.pdf)).

That gap between 34% disliking and ≤3.3% acting is the single most operationally important fact in this entire report. **A near-zero opt-out rate is not evidence that people want your mail.** It is evidence that opting out costs more effort than throwing the letter away. Any Fields dashboard that treats "hardly anyone asked us to stop" as validation is measuring inertia, not consent.

**Two further details from Allcott 2011 that Fields should internalise:**

> "The most common reasons for opting out is the perception that **the comparisons are unfair or inaccurate** or that the reports are a **'waste of resources.'**"

The complaint driver was **not** "how do you know about my house." It was **"your number is wrong."** For Fields — whose valuation carries a measured MAE around 8-10% inside its design envelope and is suppressed entirely outside it — accuracy *is* the reputational risk, not privacy.

> "Customers that opt out tend to have **higher pre-treatment electricity usage** and are also **older and lower-income**."

Read that against §1.4: the high-usage households are simultaneously **where nearly all the effect comes from (6.3% in the top decile)** and **the most likely to opt out**. The people a comparative mailer works on are the people it most annoys. That tension does not resolve — it has to be managed with framing (§1.3) rather than eliminated.

**Counterweight — the comparison itself is the value, not the irritant.** From the same paper's phone survey:
- **49% would like the reports LESS if the neighbour comparisons were removed**, against only **11% who would like them more**.
- **73%** agree or strongly agree the reports provide useful information.
- Affect was mostly neutral (57% felt neither "inspired" nor "pressured"; 63% neither "proud" nor "guilty"), and where affect occurred it skewed **positive**.

So: the comparative, personalised core is what people value. The residual 34% dislike is not caused by the comparison — it is caused by receiving unrequested mail at all.

## 1.6 The criticisms, honestly stated

1. **External validity / site selection bias.** Allcott 2015 above. The famous 2% is an early-adopter number; the population number is 1.31%.
2. **Welfare ≠ behaviour change.** Allcott & Kessler 2019: "'Nudge'-style interventions are often deemed 'successful' if they cause large behavior change, but they are rarely subjected to full social welfare evaluations." A 2% saving that costs recipients 43% of its value in hassle and guilt is a much weaker result than "2% saving".
3. **Distributional/ideological backfire.** Costa & Kahn 2013.
4. **The effect is small in absolute terms.** 0.62 kWh/day. Impressive per dollar spent; trivial per household.
5. **The distribution channel was mandated and pre-existing.** Utilities bought HERs largely to hit **Energy Efficiency Portfolio Standards** — regulatory savings quotas (Allcott & Kessler §I). The sender already had a billing relationship, a legitimate reason to hold the data, and a regulator paying for the mail. **Fields has none of these three.** This is the biggest single caveat on transferring Opower's results.
6. **The data was the utility's own meter data**, not scraped or inferred. The "how do you know that about my house?" question never arose. It will arise for Fields.

# 2. Other "data about the thing you own" mailers — pensions, insurance, cars, water, health
### Evidence from pensions, insurance, automotive, water and health. Non-real-estate, non-energy.

**Label key:** `[PRIMARY DATA]` = peer-reviewed study, RCT, or regulator's own evaluation data · `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]` = company/trade-press assertion, no verifiable method · `[UNSOURCED]` = widely repeated, no source located.

**Headline of the section:** across five unrelated industries, sending someone accurate data about an asset they already own reliably produces a *small* behavioural effect — typically 1–8 percentage points — and only when the data reveals a **gap, loss, or change** the recipient did not already know. Where the number merely confirms what they assumed, the effect is zero. The two largest effects found anywhere in this literature (mailed FIT, mailed HPV kits, both ~9–22pp) are not from *information* at all — they are from mailing the **instrument that removes the next step**.

## 1. Bank / superannuation / pension statements reframed as insight

### 1.1 Retirement income projections — the core RCT evidence

**Goda, Manchester & Sojourner (2014), *Journal of Public Economics* 119:80–92** — the canonical trial. `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- Direct-mail intervention to University of Wisconsin employees (~17,000 in the sampled population) showing the relationship between current contributions and projected retirement income.
- Effect: **+$85 in annual contributions**, = **3.6% of the average contribution level**, = **0.15% of average salary**. ([EconPapers abstract, 2014](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:pubeco:v:119:y:2014:i:c:p:80-92); [NBER w17927, 2012](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17927))
- Among the subset who *did* change their contribution, the change was large — **~$1,150/yr** — i.e. the effect is a **small number of large movers**, not a broad small nudge. ([NBER w17927](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17927)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- **Critical caveat for anyone designing such a mailer:** "the assumptions used to generate the projections influence the saving response" — retirement age, return rate and hypothetical contribution level materially moved the answer. The projection is not a neutral fact; its framing *is* the intervention. ([NBER w17927](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17927)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- Reported in secondary summaries as: projections **combined with** planning materials moved contributions, while **projections alone did not have a significant effect**. I could not open the full ScienceDirect text (403) to verify the arm-level split directly — treat the "projections alone = null" claim as **probable but not independently verified here**. ([ScienceDirect listing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0047272714001819)) `[UNSOURCED — secondary summary only]`
- A second experiment in the same literature reports **+$174 (2.3%)** annual contributions, with larger effects for the financially literate. `[PRIMARY DATA — effect size cited in search-result summary; source paper not opened]`

**Dolls, Doerrenberg, Peichl & Stichnoth (2018), *Journal of Public Economics* 158:168–179 — German *Renteninformation* letter.** `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- Quasi-experimental (regression discontinuity on two age cutoffs for receiving the annual state pension-projection letter), German tax return data.
- Findings: letters **increase private retirement savings**, effects described by the authors as "**fairly sizable and persistent over several years**"; also **increase labour earnings**; and the savings increase **partly crowds out charitable donations**. Mechanism: both *information* and *salience*. ([NBER w22684 / IDEAS abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/22684.html))
- **I could not retrieve the point estimates** (paywalled; IZA DP number mis-resolved). The directional finding is solid and is the strongest evidence that an annual "here is your asset's projected value" letter has multi-year effects — but quote it qualitatively, not numerically.
- The **crowd-out finding is the interesting one and is rarely quoted**: the mailer did not create new money, it **reallocated** it.

### 1.2 The US SECURE Act lifetime income illustrations — regulation shipped, evidence thin

- Mandated by SECURE Act 2019; DOL interim final rule effective **18 September 2021**, requiring annual lifetime-income illustrations on DC plan statements. ([DOL EBSA fact sheet](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/ebsa/about-ebsa/our-activities/resource-center/fact-sheets/pension-benefit-statements-lifetime-income-illustrations)) `[PRIMARY DATA — regulation]`
- **Center for Retirement Research (Boston College) critique:** the rule assumes **no future contributions and no future investment returns**, so for anyone not near retirement it generates "**a meaningless number**"; Munnell concludes the illustrations are "unlikely to be useful to participants who are not on the verge of retirement." DOL itself effectively conceded, encouraging plans to keep offering "interactive, stochastic, and tailored" calculators instead. ([CRR, "DOL Punts on SECURE Act Lifetime Income Illustrations"](https://crr.bc.edu/dol-punts-on-secure-act-lifetime-income-illustrations/)) `[PRIMARY DATA — regulator/academic commentary]`
- **Backlash / harm signal:** PGIM research (2022) on participant reactions to the illustrations reported that **68% of participants' responses were negative**, with the risk that early-career savers seeing a small projected income are demoralised rather than motivated, and near-retirees seeing projections *higher* than actual income are falsely reassured. ([PLANSPONSOR coverage](https://www.plansponsor.com/participants-need-support-understand-lifetime-income-projections/)) `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — asset manager research, method not published]`
- **Notable absence:** I found **no published post-implementation evaluation** measuring whether the mandated illustrations changed contribution rates. A nationwide disclosure mandate went live with no measurement plan attached.

### 1.3 UK "simpler annual benefit statement" — mandate without evaluation

- From **1 October 2022**, trustees of DC auto-enrolment schemes must issue a statement fitting on "**one double-sided sheet of A4**", covering: pot size, projected value at retirement, and how to increase it. ([GOV.UK consultation outcome](https://gov.uk/government/consultations/simpler-annual-benefit-statements-for-workplace-pensions); [DWP draft statutory guidance](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/985787/draft-statutory-guidance-simpler-annual-benefit-statements.pdf)) `[PRIMARY DATA — regulation]`
- **The Regulatory Policy Committee rated the Impact Assessment "fit for purpose" only after revision, and rated the monitoring and evaluation plan "Weak":** "The IA commits to reviewing the policy after three years but does not provide any details on how the policy will be monitored." Cost-benefit analysis rated only "Satisfactory": "based on a number of assumptions that are based on limited evidence." ([RPC-DWP-5097(1), 7 Oct 2021](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/1026842/Full_Committee_RPC-DWP-5097_1__-_Simpler_Annual_Benefit_Statements.pdf)) `[PRIMARY DATA — regulator]`
- Economics: **transitional cost ~£46m over two years**; ongoing **saving** to providers ~£2m/yr; **overall net present value −£28.9m**. The behavioural benefit to savers was **never monetised**. A negative-NPV mandate justified entirely by an unquantified belief that clearer asset data changes behaviour. `[PRIMARY DATA]`

### 1.4 Australian superannuation — the cleanest natural experiment, and the most sobering result

The *Your Future, Your Super* performance test (2021) forced trustees of failing MySuper products to **write to every member** telling them, in plain terms, that the fund holding their retirement savings had officially underperformed, and to consider moving.

- **13 MySuper products across 12 trustees failed the inaugural (Aug 2021) test**; letters were required by **27 September 2021**. ([APRA, inaugural results](https://www.apra.gov.au/news-and-publications/apra-releases-inaugural-your-future-your-super-performance-test-results)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- **Result, APRA's own count (10 November 2021):** of ~**1 million member accounts** in failed products, "**fewer than 68,000 have been closed**" — **7% of total accounts**, and only **4.2% ($2.2 billion) of assets**. ([APRA media release, 10 Nov 2021](https://www.apra.gov.au/news-and-publications/apra-urges-super-members-prioritise-their-own-best-financial-interests)) `[PRIMARY DATA — regulator]`
- This is the single most transferable number in this section: **a legally mandated, personally addressed, unambiguous "your asset is underperforming" letter, with real money at stake, moved 7% of recipients.** ~90%+ did nothing.
- **Documented supplier backlash / gaming:** ASIC reviewed communications from **30 trustees, including all 12** whose products failed, and found trustees were (a) "publishing the MySuper product's failure of the test on a webpage less likely to be visited by persons interested in the product", (b) selectively highlighting favourable metrics, and (c) **delegitimising the test itself**. ([ASIC 22-154MR, 2022](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2022-releases/22-154mr-asic-warns-super-trustees-to-be-transparent-in-their-underperformance-communications-to-members/)) `[PRIMARY DATA — regulator]`
- Where large member movement *did* occur, it was **structural, not behavioural**: nine underperforming MySuper products **exited the market**, moving ~800,000 members and $39bn — i.e. mergers did what letters could not. ([Financial Newswire](https://financialnewswire.com.au/superannuation/all-but-one-of-failed-super-funds-moved/)) `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — trade press]`

### 1.5 Savings-rate reminders — FCA Occasional Paper 7 (the best-designed "your money is losing value" trial)

**FCA OP7, "Stimulating interest: Reminding savers to act when rates decrease" (Jan 2015), Adams, Hunt, Vale & Zaliauskas.** `[PRIMARY DATA — regulator RCT]`
- Field trial with a UK savings provider: reminding customers that the bonus rate on their own account was about to end.
- "Reminders … can increase switching by at least 8% relative to not receiving a reminder. Specifically, switching increased by between **5.6 to 7.9 percentage points** 20 weeks after the rate decrease, relative to a base switching rate of approximately **50% to 70%**."
- **Timing matters more than content:** a reminder **before** the rate decrease increased switching by **7.1pp**; within-firm switching *fell* 2.0pp while switching *away* rose **9.1pp**.
- **Framing effects, small but real:** standard reminder **+5.4pp**; loss-framed **+7.2pp**; gain-framed **+7.9pp**; the best variant ran **+8.8pp** over the standard reminder's comparator.
- Also relevant to fulfilment design: adding a **return form** enabling customers to claim redress raised response **+2.6pp on average**.
([FCA Occasional Paper 7](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-7.pdf))

## 2. Insurance renewal "here's what changed" — the best-evidenced case, and the one that partly backfired

### 2.1 The field trials that justified the rule (FCA Occasional Paper 12, Dec 2015)

**Adams, Baker, Hunt, Kelly & Nava — RCT with 300,000 customers across one home insurer and two motor insurers.** `[PRIMARY DATA — regulator RCT]`

| Treatment | Home (Firm A, combined) | Motor (Firms B/C) |
|---|---|---|
| **Last year's premium shown** | **+3.2pp** switch-or-negotiate (= **11–18% relative** increase) | **no significant effect** on switching |
| Money Advice Service leaflet | no effect | no effect |
| Glossary of terms | no effect | no effect |
| Salient bullet points | **−1.0pp** (i.e. *worse*) | no effect |
| Simplification | — | no effect |
| Reminder letter | — | no effect on switching; **+10.6pp shopped around** |
| Reminder email / SMS | — | no effect |

- **Dose–response:** the effect scales with the size of the price rise being revealed. For customers in the **highest price-increase quartile** the effect was **+4.7pp** (combined) and **+2.9pp** (contents) — "**15% to 30% more customers switching and negotiating**". Lower quartiles: +1.2pp to +2.3pp.
- **No effect for contents insurance** at all on average — the product where price increases were smaller.
- **Motor null explained:** at Firm B, average prices *did not increase* at renewal and the base switching rate was already high (**47% vs under 28%** at the home insurer). Disclosure only works where there is something adverse to disclose.
- Motor customers did **shop around** more (**+7.3pp**, an 11% relative rise) — then chose not to switch. Information changed *search*, not *action*.
- **Age did not moderate the effect**; customers with **9+ renewals were affected less**, contrary to the "protect the loyal elderly" rationale that motivated the policy.
([FCA Occasional Paper 12](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-12.pdf))

### 2.2 What actually happened after the mandate (FCA Evaluation Paper 19/1, Oct 2019)

Rules made Aug 2016, in force **1 April 2017**: renewal notices must show last year's premium, plus shop-around text, plus an extra message at the 4th+ renewal. FCA evaluated using transaction data from **17 firms** and notices from 21. `[PRIMARY DATA — regulator evaluation]`

- **Net consumer savings £39m–£330m/yr, central estimate £185m/yr**, against **~£4–5m/yr ongoing costs** and **~£30m one-off** (70% below the ex-ante CBA estimate).
- **Where the benefit came from is not what the trials predicted.** Most of it came from **firms pre-emptively moderating renewal prices**, not from consumers switching:
  - Home: renewal increases **£2–£9 lower** per policy than counterfactual (£16 actual vs £18–25 counterfactual) = 1–3% of a ~£300 premium.
  - Motor: **£1–£6 lower** (£57 vs £58–63).
  - **Pet insurance: £4 HIGHER** (£71 vs £67) — FCA's own reading is that pet insurers **raised renewal prices pre-emptively** to offset expected switching. A transparency mandate made one market's consumers worse off, −£5m to −£7m/yr.
- **Switching effects were tiny and, in home, negative:**
  - Home: **−0.8 to −3.0 percentage points** switching or negotiating.
  - Motor: **+1.3 to +1.7pp**. Pet: **+1.2pp**.
  - Compare with the **+3.2pp** the pre-mandate RCT predicted for home. **The trial's headline effect did not survive scale-up, and reversed sign.**
- Softer engagement measures did move: "actively renewing" rose **21%→25%** (home) and **27%→30%** (motor) in Ipsos MORI survey data.
([FCA Evaluation Paper 19/1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/corporate/ep19-1.pdf))

### 2.3 The 2021 "loyalty penalty" / price-walking ban — transparency abandoned for prohibition

Because the disclosure remedy delivered ~£185m/yr against a several-billion-pound problem, the FCA moved from *telling people* to *banning the practice*: from **1 January 2022**, renewal price must not exceed the equivalent new-business price (PS21/5).

**FCA Evaluation Paper 25/2 (July 2025)** — the regulator's own scorecard: `[PRIMARY DATA — regulator evaluation]`
- **Ex-ante forecast: £4.2bn consumer savings over 10 years** (£2.5bn motor).
- **Realised:** motor prices fell **£6.63 per policy** on average, → 10-year range **£163m–£3.0bn, central estimate ~£1.6bn**. **Home: no statistically significant price effect at all** — FCA declined to monetise it.
- Price-walking did stop: home renewal-vs-new differential **halved, £95.38 → £49.17**; motor flipped so new customers now pay **£109.19 more** than renewers (was £20.76).
- **Cost overrun:** ongoing compliance costs ran **above** CBA for both small (**£0.8m vs £0.3m**) and large firms (**£1.2m vs £0.7m**); small firms' one-off costs **£5.0m vs £2.2m** forecast.
- Product-quality effects "**inconclusive**" — higher cover limits but higher compulsory excesses in motor.
- Consumer-side backlash during the transition: a **Which? survey of 14,000+ members** found **51%** of home and **43%** of motor policyholders paying **more** in H1 2022 than before the reforms. ([Insurance Times reporting on Which?, 2022](https://www.insurancetimes.co.uk/news/insureds-facing-higher-renewal-prices-despite-fcas-price-walking-ban-which/1442191.article)) `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — consumer-group survey, self-selected membership panel]`
([FCA EP 25/2](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/corporate/ep25-2.pdf))

**Transferable verdict:** the transparency mailer worked, *narrowly* — it changed **firm** behaviour more than consumer behaviour, delivered ~4% of the harm it targeted, and in one market (pet) was **net negative**. The regulator's revealed preference after five years was to stop relying on informing the asset-owner.

## 3. Car dealers: "your car is worth $X" — equity mining

### 3.1 What the industry claims

There is a real, mature industry (AutoAlert/AlertMiner, DealerSocket, VinCue integrations) built on mining the dealer's DMS for customers whose financed vehicle now has positive equity, then mailing them their own car's number.

- **AutoAlert's own product page carries no quantitative performance claims at all** — no response rate, no lift, no ROI, no dealer count. ([autoalert.com/alertminer](https://www.autoalert.com/alertminer-automotive-equity-data-mining/)) — worth noting: the category leader does not publish a number.
- Trade/vendor benchmark table for automotive direct mail: `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — no citations, no methodology given]` ([DirectMail.io playbook](https://directmail.io/blog/automotive-dealer-direct-mail-playbook))

### 3.2 Independent verification: essentially none

- **I found no independent, peer-reviewed or regulator-verified measurement of equity-mining mailer response rates.** Every number above traces to a vendor or an agency selling the service. Treat the 4–8% as marketing.
- The only quasi-independent anchor is the **ANA/DMA Response Rate Report (2023)**: **house lists 5.3%**, **prospect lists 2.9%**, median ROI 29%. ([ANA 2023 Response Rate Report, via secondary coverage](https://html.duckduckgo.com/html/?q=ANA+DMA+response+rate+report+direct+mail)) `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — industry association, self-reported member data]`
- **The critical read:** an equity-mining list *is* a house list. Its claimed 4–8% sits **inside the ordinary house-list benchmark of 5.3%**. There is **no published evidence that personalising with the asset's value outperforms an ordinary house-list mailing**. The uplift narrative is asserted, not demonstrated.

### 3.3 Enforcement and backlash — the deceptive-mailer problem is real and specific to this format

- **FTC v. Traffic Jam Events LLC & David J. Jeansonne II (Aug 2020)** — administrative complaint over mailers that (a) **falsely appeared to direct consumers to federal COVID-19 stimulus benefits**, and (b) carried matching-number flyers implying a prize win, where **small print on the reverse disclosed a 1-in-52,000 chance of actually having won**. Charges under the FTC Act plus **TILA and Regulation Z** for undisclosed credit terms. ([FTC press release, 13 Aug 2020](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2020/08/ftc-adds-charges-against-auto-marketer-deceptive-prize-mailers)) `[PRIMARY DATA — regulator enforcement]`
- **FTC "Operation Steer Clear" (Jan 2014)** — sweep against 10 dealers; **Fowlerville Ford (Michigan)** charged for "mailers that deceptively claimed consumers had won a sweepstakes prize, when, in fact, they had not." Order bars "misrepresenting material terms of any prize, sweepstakes, giveaway, or other incentive." ([FTC press release, Jan 2014](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2014/01/ftc-announces-sweep-against-10-auto-dealers)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- **FTC "Operation Ruse Control" (Mar 2015)** — **252 enforcement actions** (187 US, 65 Canada), 6 new FTC cases, **>$2.6m in judgments** — but on pricing/add-ons/financing, **not** mailers. ([FTC press release, Mar 2015](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2015/03/ftc-multiple-law-enforcement-partners-announce-crackdown-deception-fraud-auto-sales-financing)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- **Regulatory vacuum, current:** the FTC's **CARS Rule** (Combating Auto Retail Scams), which would have banned material misrepresentations across the sale, was **vacated by the US Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit on 27 January 2025** on procedural grounds. ([Federal Register text of the rule, 2024](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/01/04/2023-27997/combating-auto-retail-scams-trade-regulation-rule); vacatur reported by [Mayer Brown, 2025](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/03/a-post-cars-rule-brake-not-so-fast-buckle-up-for-new-regulatory-activity-in-the-motor-vehicle-space)) `[PRIMARY DATA / law-firm reporting]`
- **Enforcement has since shifted back to advertised-price accuracy:** on **13 March 2026** the FTC sent warning letters to **97 auto dealership groups** over six deceptive pricing practices (fees excluded from advertised prices, unavailable rebates, omitted down payments, price conditioned on dealer financing, undisclosed bundles, phantom vehicles). **Mailers and vehicle-value claims are not mentioned.** ([FTC press release, Mar 2026](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-warns-97-auto-dealership-groups-about-deceptive-pricing)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`

**Transferable verdict:** the "your asset is worth $X, act now" mailer has a documented **regulatory failure mode** — it degrades into manufactured urgency (fake prize, fake recall, fake government notice) because the underlying data alone does not produce enough response. Every FTC mailer case in this vertical is about **fabricated urgency layered on top of a legitimate-looking personal notice**.

## 4. Utility comparison reports beyond Opower — the water experiment

**Ferraro & Price (2013), "Using Nonpecuniary Strategies to Influence Behavior: Evidence from a Large-Scale Field Experiment", *Review of Economics and Statistics* 95(1):64–73** (NBER w17189). `[PRIMARY DATA — RCT]`

- **Design:** Cobb County Water System, Georgia, summer 2007. **~106,872 households**: T1 technical advice **n=11,699**; T2 technical advice + prosocial appeal **n=11,695**; T3 technical advice + prosocial appeal + **social comparison** (own use vs county median, plus own percentile, negatively framed) **n=11,699**; **control n=71,779**. Households using <4,000 gal/month excluded. Utility pre-specified a **minimum detectable effect of 2%** at power ≥0.90.
- **Effects (summer 2007, vs control):**
  - **T3 strong social norm: −4.8%, ≈ −1,740 gallons per household.**
  - T2 (prosocial, no comparison): significant but smaller.
  - **T1 technical advice alone: statistically significant but BELOW the 2% minimum detectable effect** — the authors write this "calls into question the economic significance of information-only conservation efforts."
  - Treatment reductions were **7.41% to 53.38% greater** than the control group's own year-on-year decline; rank order T1 < T2 < T3 as predicted; all significant at p<0.05 (ANOVA with Šidák correction; Jonckheere–Terpstra trend test significant).
- **Benchmarking:** the social-comparison effect is equivalent to what a **12–15% price increase** would have achieved, and is "**more than twice**" the effect seen in contemporaneous energy social-comparison programmes.
- **Heterogeneity (the important part):** effects concentrate in **high users** — an approximate **94.1% difference in relative treatment effect** between low- and high-use households under T3. Social comparison works on the people who are visibly *above* the norm; it does little for those already below it.
- **Decay — measured, not assumed:** in the month after the letter, T3 households used **5.62% less** water than control; **four months later that difference had declined by 35.4%**. Waning "is driven almost entirely by the high user group" — i.e. the segment that produced the effect is also the segment that regresses.
([NBER w17189 PDF](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w17189/w17189.pdf); [journal listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/restat/v95y2013i1p64-73.html))

**Persistence follow-up — Ferraro, Miranda & Price (2011), *AER Papers & Proceedings* 101(3):318–22:** on re-measurement, "**only messages augmented with social comparisons have a lasting impact on water demand**" — the prosocial appeal alone decayed to nothing, the comparison arm persisted. ([AEA listing](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.101.3.318)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`

**Energy comparison (for calibration only):** Allcott & Rogers (2014), *AER* 104(10):3003–37 — after Opower reports stop, effects persist but **decay ~10%/yr**; early reports cause "high-frequency action and backsliding cycles" that attenuate over time. ([AER](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.104.10.3003); [NBER w18492](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18492)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`

**Transferable verdict:** *information alone underperforms its own minimum detectable effect.* What produced a real effect was **the comparison to peers**, targeted at the group **on the wrong side of the norm**, and even that lost a third of its power in four months.

## 5. Health "your results" mailings — the largest effect sizes in this entire section

### 5.1 Mailed FIT kits (colorectal cancer)

- **Network meta-analysis, 76 RCTs, 400,638 patients** (2025/2026): patient navigation **RR 1.58 (95% CI 1.23–2.02)**; **mailed FIT outreach RR 1.36 (95% CI 1.07–1.74)**; educational multimedia RR 1.27 (0.91–1.78, **NS**); reminder-only RR 1.24 (0.98–1.57, **NS**); choice-based and colonoscopy outreach **not significantly better than usual care**. Mailed FIT beat colonoscopy outreach **RR 1.35 (1.11–1.63)**. In **low-baseline settings (<30% uptake) mailed FIT was strongest: RR 3.12 (95% CI 1.70–5.71)**. ([PubMed 41932450](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41932450/)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- **SMARTER CRC cluster RCT** (28 rural Oregon clinics, **n=5,614** Medicaid enrollees): mailed FIT + patient navigation vs usual care → **11.8% vs 4.5%**, a **+7.3 percentage point** difference at 6 months (**95% CI 5.3–9.2**). All 1,489 intervention-eligible enrollees were mailed a FIT; 88.5% got advance notification, 78.1% got reminders. ([PMC11915063, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11915063/)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- Add-on components: text messaging **+6.7pp** and mailed reminders **+4.8pp** on FIT return. ([CGH 2025, S1542-3565(25)00835-3](https://www.cghjournal.org/article/S1542-3565(25)00835-3/fulltext) — abstract accessible via search index; full text 403 to me) `[PRIMARY DATA — effect sizes from indexed abstract, full text not verified]`
- The widely quoted "mailed FIT improves screening rates by **22 to 28 percentage points**" appears in review literature but I could not trace it to a specific primary source in this session. `[UNSOURCED]`

### 5.2 Mailed HPV self-sampling kits (cervical cancer)

- **HOME trial (Winer et al., JAMA Network Open 2019), n=19,851 randomised** (9,960 intervention / 9,891 control), women overdue for screening in an integrated delivery system:
  - **Screening uptake 26.3% vs 17.4%, RR 1.51 (95% CI 1.43–1.60)** — an **+8.9pp** absolute effect.
  - **But the clinical endpoint did not move:** CIN2+ **detected** 12 (0.12%) vs 8 (0.08%), **RR 1.49 (95% CI 0.61–3.64)**; CIN2+ **treated** 12 (0.12%) vs 7 (0.07%), **RR 1.70 (95% CI 0.67–4.32)** — both non-significant.
  - Harms: "no unexpected and 5 expected adverse events were self-reported" (2 light bleeding, 3 discomfort).
  ([PMC6865279](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6865279/)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- **MBMT-3 (Lancet Public Health, May 2023)** — under-screened, low-income women in North Carolina: mailed HPV self-collection kit + scheduling assistance vs scheduling assistance alone, **RR 1.93 (95% CI 1.62–2.31)** — roughly a doubling. ([Lancet Public Health 2023](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpub/article/PIIS2468-2667(23)00076-2/fulltext)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- **The documented failure mode** is downstream: the accompanying Lancet comment is titled "**The HPV self-collection paradox: boosting cervical cancer screening, struggling with follow-up care**" — uptake rises, completion of follow-up does not. In SMARTER CRC only **57.9%** of those with abnormal results received navigation to follow-up colonoscopy. ([Lancet Public Health comment, 2023](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanpub/article/PIIS2468-2667(23)00094-4/fulltext); [PMC11915063](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11915063/)) `[PRIMARY DATA]`

### 5.3 Personalised *risk* letters — the null result that matters

**"Can Communicating Personalised Disease Risk Promote Healthy Behaviour Change? A Systematic Review of Systematic Reviews", *Annals of Behavioral Medicine* 51(5):718 (2017)** — **9 systematic reviews, 36 unique primary studies.** `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- "For a broad range of methods of estimating and communicating risk, the reviews found **no evidence that risk information had strong or consistent effects**."
- "Presenting risk information on its own, **even when highly personalised, does not produce strong effects on health-related behaviours**."
- Exceptions were all cases where the personal data was made **physically tangible**, not merely stated: spirometry with "lung age" feedback **RR 2.12 (95% CI 1.24–3.62)**; imaging on smoking **OR 2.81 (95% CI 1.23–6.41)**; short-term (2–6 month) genetic testing effects **RR 1.55 (95% CI 1.09–2.21)**. "These promising short-term results generally did not sustain at longer follow-ups"; "effects on behaviour were not consistent and where changes were observed, they were **not maintained**."
([Annals of Behavioral Medicine](https://academic.oup.com/abm/article/51/5/718/4648590))

**Harm / anxiety findings — mostly reassuring, contra expectation:** `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- Genome-wide polygenic risk disclosure for T2D and CHD (RCT): **no significant difference in anxiety or worry** between intervention and control. ([PMC9189371](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9189371/))
- Coronary artery disease genetic risk score pilot RCT: "can be provided in clinical practice with **no significant adverse psychological effects**." ([Frontiers in Cardiovascular Medicine 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cardiovascular-medicine/articles/10.3389/fcvm.2017.00053/full))
- Breast cancer personalised risk communication: **both general anxiety and cancer worry declined** after risk communication among women with high baseline anxiety. ([PubMed 31461546](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31461546/))
- **Informed decision-making does improve sharply:** 45.2% made an informed choice with personalised risk info vs 20.2% with generic (OR ~3.65–4.48). ([systematic review, via ABM 2017](https://academic.oup.com/abm/article/51/5/718/4648590))
- **The honest summary:** personalised risk data reliably improves *understanding and decision quality*, does **not** reliably change *behaviour*, and does **not** appear to cause psychological harm. Anxiety is the harm people fear; **inaction** is the harm that actually occurs.

## Cross-cutting conclusions

1. **The realistic effect band for "here is data about your asset" is 1–8 percentage points.** APRA's mandated underperformance letter: **7%**. FCA renewal disclosure at trial: **+3.2pp**; at national scale: **−0.8 to −3.0pp** (home). FCA savings-rate reminders: **+5.6 to +7.9pp**. Water social comparison: **−4.8%** consumption. Retirement projections: **+3.6%** of contributions.
2. **Effects appear only where the data reveals an adverse gap the recipient did not know about, and scale with the size of that gap.** FCA OP12's price-change quartiles are the cleanest demonstration: **+1.2pp** bottom quartile → **+4.7pp** top. Where no gap existed (motor insurance, contents insurance, low-use water households), effect = **zero**.
3. **Trial effects do not survive scale-up.** Home insurance: +3.2pp in RCT → *negative* nationally. Price-walking ban: £4.2bn forecast → ~£1.6bn realised, motor only, home not significant. Assume roughly an **order of magnitude of shrinkage** between a controlled test and a market-wide rollout.
4. **The counterparty adapts, and can neutralise or invert the mailer.** Pet insurers raised renewal prices pre-emptively (**−£5m to −£7m/yr consumer harm**). Australian super trustees buried and delegitimised their own failure letters. Anticipate the adaptation before shipping the disclosure.
5. **Information alone underperforms; the mechanism that works is comparison or instrumentation.** Technical advice alone fell **below its own 2% minimum detectable effect** (Ferraro & Price). Personalised risk information alone: "no strong or consistent effects" across 9 systematic reviews. The genuinely large effects — mailed FIT **RR 1.36**, mailed HPV kits **RR 1.51–1.93** — come from **mailing the instrument that removes the next step**, not from mailing the number.
6. **Search ≠ action.** Motor insurance reminders raised shopping-around **+7.3 to +10.6pp** and switching by **zero**. Screening uptake rose **+8.9pp** while CIN2+ detection did not significantly move. Measure the terminal outcome, not the engagement proxy.
7. **Decay is fast and concentrated in the responders.** Water: **−35.4% of the effect within four months**, driven almost entirely by the high-use segment that produced it. Opower: **~10%/yr** post-cessation. Risk communication: short-term effects "not maintained".
8. **The commercial version of this format has a documented integrity failure mode.** Every FTC action found here involves genuine-looking personal notices carrying **fabricated urgency** (prize, stimulus, sweepstakes) — because the honest asset number alone does not generate enough response. Meanwhile the vendor-claimed **4–8%** equity-mining response rate sits **inside the ordinary house-list benchmark of 5.3%**, with **no independent verification anywhere**.

## 1. US iBuyers and portals — what was actually mailed, and what went wrong

### 1.1 Opendoor and the FTC — the single most instructive case

**The enforcement action.** On 1 August 2022 the FTC announced an administrative action and $62m settlement against Opendoor Labs Inc. for misleading homeowners about what they would net by selling to Opendoor.
`[PRIMARY DATA]` https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/08/ftc-takes-action-stop-online-home-buying-firm-opendoor-labs-inc-cheating-potential-sellers (2022)

Final consent order approved October 2022, Commission vote 4–0. The order "prohibits it from making the deceptive, false, and unsubstantiated claims to consumers about how much money they will receive or the costs they will have to pay," and requires Opendoor to "have competent and reliable evidence to support any representations made about the costs, savings, or financial benefits associated with using its service, **and any claims about the costs associated with traditional home sales**."
`[PRIMARY DATA]` https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/10/ftc-approves-final-order-against-opendoor-labs-preventing-company-misleading-consumers-about-cost (2022)

**Redress.** April 2024: FTC sent payments totalling nearly $62m to **54,689 homeowners** who sold to Opendoor before 1 Aug 2022 — roughly **$1,134 average per homeowner**.
`[PRIMARY DATA]` https://www.ftc.gov/enforcement/refunds/opendoor-refunds (2024)

**What the mechanism actually was** (from the FTC's redacted complaint, retrieved and text-extracted — https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/192_3191_-_opendoor_combined_package_public_redacted_not_signed.pdf). This is the part that transfers directly to any homeowner-valuation mailer:

- **The delivery vehicle was an emailed personalised valuation**, not postal mail. ¶22: "Opendoor emails consumers a link with a customized offer within a few days… the emails have stated that Opendoor has *'just finished analyzing your home's market value, and we're excited to make you an offer!'*" `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- **The deceptive artefact was a comparison chart**, not the valuation itself. ¶25: the chart "projected the net proceeds each consumer would receive from accepting Opendoor's offer versus selling on the market. The chart **prepopulated Opendoor's proposed purchase price and the market price as identical**." `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- ¶26: "As of November 2019, over [redacted] percent of these charts used in accepted offers projected that the consumer would realize more net proceeds by selling to Opendoor." Press reporting of the same paragraph gives **>90% of charts** projecting a gain with **average projected gain >$6,000** `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]` — the underlying FTC figures are redacted in the public complaint, so treat the exact numbers as press-attributed, not verified.
- **The fine-print disclaimer did not save them.** ¶28 is the key finding: Opendoor included two disclaimers, but "**Those disclaimers were not conspicuous and were in fine print. Moreover, they did not cure Opendoor's repeated representations**… one merely stated that '[t]hese figures are our best estimates' and the other stated, **in direct contravention to Opendoor's marketing**, that the offer 'does not necessarily represent the "market value" of your home' **because it was not a formal appraisal**." `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- **The AVM was systematically shaded and the shading concealed.** ¶34: "Opendoor has used an automated system to generate expected market values… employees have manually adjusted these values before presenting them to consumers as offers. Opendoor's internal analyses showed that these manually adjusted offers were several percentage points below Opendoor's assessment of market value." ¶35: a June 2019 "risk-based pricing" policy "automatically reduced offers below Opendoor's assessment of market value." ¶37: "Consumers had no reason to know that Opendoor had reduced their offers." `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- **Overstating the counterfactual was an independent violation.** ¶52: the net-proceeds calculator told sellers to expect **9%** in agent commissions + seller concessions + overlap costs, plus **1%** for staging — while "Opendoor's data showed that sellers were likely to pay much less, and the company had no other data supporting its representations about traditional costs." ¶53: it claimed sellers pay 2% in concessions but itself paid [redacted]% when reselling. `[PRIMARY DATA]`
- **Internal data contradicted the marketing.** ¶55: a March 2019 internal analysis of consumers who accepted an offer then withdrew after the repair demand found [redacted]% "made more money selling on the open market." `[PRIMARY DATA]`

**Opendoor's own account:** "Opendoor did not admit any wrongdoing"; it "disagreed with the FTC's allegations, but decided to settle." It dates the conduct to 2017–2019 and says it now shows the service charge "as an exact figure before you accept any offer" plus estimated net proceeds at offer stage.
`[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]` https://www.opendoor.com/articles/opendoor-ftc-settlement

**Direct mail specifically:** the FTC complaint text contains **no** reference to direct mail, mailers, postcards or letters — the channel at issue was web/email/Facebook. Claims that "Opendoor uses direct-mail postcards" appear only in marketing blogs. `[UNSOURCED]` — I could not verify Opendoor postal-mail volumes or response rates from any primary source.

## 2. The Zestimate itself — accuracy, and the "is it an appraisal?" litigation

### 2.1 Published accuracy

Zillow's own current published figures: nationwide median error **1.83% on-market**, **7.01% off-market**.
`[PRIMARY DATA — Zillow's own published figures]` https://www.zillow.com/zestimate/ and https://www.zillow.com/z/zestimate/ (2025/26; both 403 to automated fetch, figures retrieved via domain-restricted search of zillow.com)

Also from Zillow: in major metros "the Zestimate for on-market homes is within 20% of the actual selling price more than 99% of the time." `[PRIMARY DATA — Zillow]`. Note what that sentence does *not* say: nothing about off-market, and ±20% is an enormous band.

**Three sceptical reads you should carry over:**
1. **On-market vs off-market is the whole story.** 1.83% vs 7.01% — a ~3.8× degradation the moment the list price is removed from the input set. Homeowner mail is by definition off-market. The number that applies to a mailer is 7%, not 1.8%.
2. **Median ≠ typical.** A 7% median error means half of homes are worse than 7% off; the tail is unbounded. `[PRIMARY DATA — arithmetic]`
3. **Survivorship in the denominator.** Accuracy is measured only against homes that actually sold, using the most recent Zestimate before sale. Homes that never sold contribute nothing. `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]` — this critique is well-established in trade commentary (e.g. https://homerise.com/zillow-zestimate/) but Zillow's own methodology page (https://www.zillow.com/research/putting-accuracy-in-context-3255/) returned 403 to automated fetch, so I could not confirm Zillow states it in those terms.

### 2.2 Barbara Andersen v Zillow (2017) — the unlicensed-appraisal theory, and why it failed

- Filed 27 April 2017, Cook County Circuit Court, removed to N.D. Ill.; plaintiff a Glenview homeowner and practising attorney. Alleged the Zestimate functioned as an unlicensed appraisal under the Illinois Real Estate Appraiser Licensing Act, plus consumer-fraud counts. `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]` https://www.washingtonpost.com/realestate/zillow-faces-lawsuit-over-zestimate-tool-that-calculates-a-houses-worth/2017/05/09/b22d0318-3410-11e7-b4ee-434b6d506b37_story.html
- **Dismissed August 2017 by Judge Amy J. St. Eve.** The unlicensed-appraisal count was dismissed **with prejudice**; the remaining consumer-protection counts dismissed **without prejudice**. `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]` https://www.inman.com/2017/08/24/zestimates-emerge-from-legal-attack-unscathed/ ; https://www.chicagobusiness.com/article/20170823/CRED0701/170829946/zillow-zestimates-lawsuit-dismissed-in-illinois
  (Note a discrepancy in secondary reporting: Techdirt describes all claims as dismissed without prejudice — https://www.techdirt.com/2017/08/31/court-dumps-lawsuit-against-zillow-over-inaccurate-zestimates/. Inman and Crain's report one count with prejudice. I could not retrieve the docket to resolve this. Flagged as an unresolved conflict.)
- **The dispositive reasoning:** the Illinois appraisal statute "expressly excludes 'the procurement of an automated valuation model.'" The court also treated Zestimates as "**nonactionable statements of opinion**" built on publicly available data, noting "the zestimates are not false, misleading or confusing," and leaned on the fact that Zillow **labels them estimates and publishes their error characteristics**. `[PRIMARY DATA — court reasoning as quoted in]` https://www.techdirt.com/2017/08/31/court-dumps-lawsuit-against-zillow-over-inaccurate-zestimates/

**The transferable rule:** an AVM survives the "you're practising valuation without a licence" attack when (a) a statutory AVM carve-out exists, (b) it is presented as an *estimate* of opinion, and (c) published accuracy limitations are visible. It does **not** protect you from consumer-protection law — which is exactly the door the FTC walked through with Opendoor.

### 2.3 US regulator position on AVMs

The 2024 interagency rule (OCC/Fed/FDIC/NCUA/CFPB/FHFA) sets **quality-control standards for AVMs** used in mortgage credit decisions, and is explicit that it does **not** cover an AVM used by a licensed appraiser in developing an appraisal. Under USPAP "an AVM is a tool that appraisers may use… but not for the completion of an appraisal in determining the appraiser's opinion of value." The rule distinguishes *appraisals* from *evaluations* ("written estimates of market value"), and "USPAP standards and appraiser credentialing are not required for individuals who prepare evaluations."
`[PRIMARY DATA]` https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/08/07/2024-16197/quality-control-standards-for-automated-valuation-models ; https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_automated-valuation-models_final-rule_2024-06.pdf (2024)

Consumer-facing marketing AVMs sit outside this rule entirely. The regulatory exposure for a homeowner mailer is **consumer law, not valuation law**.

## 3. Agent prospecting mail — what is actually measured

### 3.1 The "I have a buyer for your home" letter

Long-standing industry practice; the letter is characterised in consumer-facing guidance as "**thinly disguised solicitation letters from agents seeking listing business. Even if an agent does represent a buyer in the market, it's unlikely that the buyer actually wants the specific home.**"
`[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]` https://www.thebalancemoney.com/i-have-a-buyer-for-your-home-1799002

**Regulatory hook (UK):** estate agents are bound by the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 and the Estate Agents Act 1979; a false statement that a specific buyer exists is a misleading action, enforceable by National Trading Standards Estate & Letting Agency Team, with penalties up to prohibition from estate agency work. Complaint route runs through the mandatory redress schemes.
`[PRIMARY DATA]` https://www.gov.uk/selling-a-home/making-a-complaint ; https://services.bristol.gov.uk/ntselat/consumers/make-a-complaint-about-or-report-a-property-agent

I found **no** case in which a regulator has published a decision specifically on an "I have a buyer" letter. The risk is structural and obvious rather than precedent-backed.

### 3.2 Response-rate benchmarks — read this sceptically

The number everyone quotes is from the **ANA/DMA Response Rate Report 2018**: direct mail response **9.0% to house lists**, **4.9% to prospect lists**, median cost-per-acquisition to house files **$43.90**, oversized envelopes highest ROI at 59%.
`[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — self-reported industry survey]` https://www.ana.net/miccontent/show/id/rr-2018-ana-dma-respose-rate ; figures as quoted at https://www.dunhills.com/2019/03/06/takeaways-from-the-ana-dma-response-rate-report-2018/

**Why you should not use 9% for anything:**
- The house-list figure **jumped from 5.1% to 9.0% in a single year** — a 76% year-on-year move in a mature channel. That is a sampling artefact signature, not a market change. `[PRIMARY DATA — arithmetic on the two published figures]`
- The ANA/DMA itself states the numbers "are based on a **low sample size** and should be used for information purposes only." `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — ANA caveat as reported]`
- It is a **voluntary self-report by marketers**, i.e. maximally exposed to survivorship: campaigns that flopped are not submitted.
- "House list" means people who already have a relationship with you. **A homeowner who has never heard of you is a prospect list.** The relevant benchmark is the 4.9%, and even that is inflated by the above.

**The vendor tier, for contrast:** print/mail suppliers quote **0.5%–2%** for real-estate seller-prospecting postcards, with "1% on a well-targeted list is solid."
`[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]` https://www.iprint360.com/resources/blog/direct-mail-campaigns-for-real-estate-7-high-impact-strategies.html ; https://presortinc.com/how-to-increase-your-conversion-rate/

Note the direction of the bias here is *unusual and worth noting*: the vendors selling mail quote **lower** numbers (0.5–2%) than the industry association's survey (4.9–9%). When the party with the incentive to inflate quotes the lower figure, believe the lower figure. **Plan against 0.5–2% for cold homeowner prospecting, and treat anything above that as unproven.**

### 3.3 NAR data

I was unable to retrieve the NAR *Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers* breakdown of how sellers found their agent — the public page is a summary only and the data sits behind a member PDF. `[UNSOURCED — gap]` https://www.nar.realtor/research-and-statistics/research-reports/highlights-from-the-profile-of-home-buyers-and-sellers (2025 edition, covering Jul 2024–Jun 2025). ## 4. UK — Purplebricks, the ASA, and the £1 sale

### 4.1 ASA ruling, 14 September 2016 — the savings claim

Claim: **£4,158 average customer saving** vs a high-street agent. **Complaint upheld; claim banned.**
Purplebricks' attempted substantiation: a **Which? survey from 2011** for average commission, an email from a third-party conveyancing firm, and third-party website data giving a national average house price of £303,190.
The ASA's findings:
- "The data from a property website which did not show the price at which the properties were sold was not adequate to confirm Purplebricks average sale price."
- "The email from a conveyancing firm and the Which? report were not adequate to confirm that the average commission rate was 1.5% as claimed."
- Purplebricks failed to supply **its own sales data** showing average fees, calculation methodology, or whether London rates were included.
Conclusion: the figure was not "adequately substantiated" and was "likely to mislead."
`[PRIMARY DATA — ASA adjudication, as reported in detail by]` https://marketinglaw.osborneclarke.com/advertising-regulation/purplebricks-saving-claim-banned-substantiation-failings/ (2016); see also https://www.estateagenttoday.co.uk/breaking-news/2016/9/asa-upholds-two-of-three-complaints-made-against-purplebricks

**The transferable point:** the ASA did not dispute that savings existed. It banned the claim because the *evidence chain for the specific number* was third-hand, five years stale, and did not include the advertiser's own transaction data. A precise figure you cannot reconstruct from your own records is the vulnerability — not the direction of the claim.

### 4.2 ASA ruling, 27 June 2018 — all three issues upheld

Complainant: **Hunters Property Group** (a competitor).
Ad: "Instruct us to sell for £849" / "£1,199 in London and surrounding areas", plus a savings comparison tool, plus hover-text stating "Around 40% of our customers pay us a fixed fee of £300 to cover ALL viewings."
- **Issue 1 upheld** — consumers would read the headline price as the total cost; the separate £300 viewings fee "should have been immediately clear" rather than in hover-over text. "Because the ad did not make clear that the viewings service had separate fee… we considered the ad was likely to mislead."
- **Issue 2 upheld** — the savings comparison tool did not account for the extra £300; the qualifying text was "not sufficiently prominent to override the overall impression that the headline savings claim… would be achieved by all consumers."
- **Issue 3 upheld** — the ad failed to make clear the fee was **always payable regardless of whether the home sold**, contrary to the commission-on-completion norm consumers expect.
`[PRIMARY DATA — ASA]` https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/purplebricks-group-plc-a18-442067.html (2018)

**Two rules fall directly out of this:** (i) a personalised savings/gap figure must net off *every* cost the recipient will actually bear, and (ii) hover-text, footnotes and fine print do **not** cure a headline impression — the same finding the FTC made at Opendoor ¶28. Two regulators, two continents, same conclusion.

- **May/June 2023: business and assets sold to Strike for £1**, backed by Sir Charles Dunstone. ~£5.5m residual cash used to settle liabilities and return a residual to shareholders. Share price down >98% from peak.
- Post-takeover, pre-tax losses widened from **£19.4m to £37.8m** in the year to 31 March 2024.
`[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — trade/financial press]` https://newsnreleases.com/2023/05/17/purplebricks-sells-its-business-to-strike-for-1-and-plans-to-liquidate/ ; https://www.onlinemarketplaces.com/articles/a-comprehensive-timeline-of-purplebricks/ ; https://www.cityam.com/huge-losses-revealed-at-purplebricks-after-takeover/

## 5. Australia

### 5.1 PropTrack / REA — and a licence constraint you must know about

PropTrack (formerly Hometrack, acquired by REA 2018, renamed 2021) supplies the AVM behind **realEstimate** on realestate.com.au. Its submission to the ACCC's Digital Platform Services Inquiry (data brokers) is a useful primary source:

- Raw data is "primarily obtained from public sources, including State/Territory land titles offices, State/Territory Valuers General and Geoscape Australia, but also from proprietary sources, including… realestate.com.au."
- **The critical constraint:** "restrictions on access to personal information are a feature of all State and Territory licences. **To the extent PropTrack acquires personal information, PropTrack is prohibited from using that information for direct marketing purposes.**"
- "**PropTrack receives property owner names from some State Valuers General**", but "we do not use it in our AVM or provide access to it as part of our Property Data offering."
- "Valuer General contracts generally strictly limit the disclosure of owner information (e.g. the information **cannot be sold, sub-licensed or downloaded**, and may only be accessed for the limited purposes set out in the licence). Valuer General contracts also contain strict record-keeping and audit requirements… (which often contain strict termination rights for any misuse of the data)."
- REA's forward plan for owner names in its Comparative Market Analysis product is "**only for identity verification purposes** (i.e. so real estate agents can know they are talking to the owner of the property)", with audit-tracked agent access.
`[PRIMARY DATA — PropTrack submission to ACCC]` https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/PropTrack.pdf (2024)

**Read that again in the context of mailing homeowners.** Australia's largest property data business, sitting on owner names, states flatly that it is contractually barred from using them for direct marketing and does not expose them. Any homeowner-name-and-address mailing list in Australia therefore has to come from somewhere other than Valuer General / land titles licensed data — and the provenance of that list is the first thing a regulator or data supplier would ask about.

### 5.2 CoreLogic / RP Data

CoreLogic operates propertyvalue.com.au as the consumer-facing estimate, with the same estimate surfaced on partner REA properties; its commercial AVM (IntelliVal) "delivers a point estimate of market value with a **confidence score** and a high/low price range."
`[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]` https://whichrealestateagent.com.au/value/property-valuation/ ; https://www.ipart.nsw.gov.au/sites/default/files/cm9_documents/Online-Submission-CoreLogic-Asia-Pacific-Name-suppressed-6-Feb-2025-165730539.PDF
No published accuracy figures for the consumer-facing estimate were located. `[UNSOURCED — gap]` Note that both major Australian AVMs ship a **confidence score** alongside the point estimate as standard practice; a bare figure is below market norm here.

### 5.3 ACCC and state enforcement — underquoting is the live risk

- **ACCC v Gary Peer & Associates (Federal Court, 13 April 2005)** — the first ACCC underquoting case. Property at 341 Glen Eira Road, Caulfield, advertised as "price guide $600,000 plus buyers should inspect", later "$650,000 plus". Sundberg J found false representations as to "the price at which the vendors of the property were prepared to sell", the price the vendors had instructed the agent to sell for, and the agent's own belief about the likely selling price — and that the agency "**did not have reasonable grounds to make the representations**." ACCC Chairman: "The Trade Practices Act requires adequate and appropriate disclosure at all times of all relevant price information, so that prospective buyers and the general public can make informed pricing decisions."
  `[PRIMARY DATA — ACCC]` https://www.accc.gov.au/media-release/gary-peer-associates-found-to-have-misled-buyers (2005)
  **The "reasonable grounds" limb is the transferable one.** Under the ACL a price/value representation about a future matter is *deemed misleading* unless the maker had reasonable grounds. The burden is on you to hold the grounds, in advance, in writing.
- **ACCC v Fletchers (Fletcher & Parker Balwyn), 2017** — record **$880,000** penalty for underquoting 22 properties by up to $700,000 below eventual sale price; each instance treated as a separate ACL breach at $40,000 each. `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — legal-sector summary]` https://www.mertonlawyers.com.au/knowledge-bank/underquoting
- **NSW Fair Trading:** >100 penalty notices for underquoting in 2024; Operation Sunlight issued "please explain" notices to 25 agencies with 15 more under investigation; $8.4m Strata and Property Taskforce, 164 audits in 2025; a Central Coast blitz across 64 businesses produced 7 penalty infringement notices. Penalties raised from **$22,000 to $110,000 or three times commission, whichever is greater**.
  `[PRIMARY DATA — NSW Government]` https://www.nsw.gov.au/ministerial-releases/nsw-cracks-down-on-underquoting-tough-new-laws ; https://www.nsw.gov.au/departments-and-agencies/fair-trading/news/fair-trading-inspection-blitz-on-central-coast-real-estate-agents-leads-to-enforcement-against-underquoting

Underquoting law targets *quoting a price you don't believe*. A published valuation that is systematically low — for example one produced by a method that regresses to the middle of its comparable pool — is functionally the same representation, made to a different audience.

### 5.4 Unsolicited approaches — Australian Consumer Law

REIQ guidance on unsolicited approaches to homeowners in Queensland (ACL unsolicited consumer agreement rules):
- No approaches on Sundays or public holidays; "before 9 am or after 6 pm on a weekday"; "before 9 am or after 5 pm on a Saturday."
- Do-not-knock signs are binding; if asked to leave, "you must do so and not return on behalf of the same business for at least 30 days unless invited."
- Mandatory disclosures on contact: purpose of visit, identification, right to ask you to leave.
- Agreements arising from unsolicited contact carry an automatic **10 business day cooling-off period** during which no services may be provided and no fees charged — extending to **approximately six months** if the rules were breached.
- Do Not Call Register applies to phone contact.
`[PRIMARY DATA — REIQ, restating ACL]` https://www.reiq.com/articles/agency-practice/know-the-rules-before-you-knock

**Important boundary:** these unsolicited-consumer-agreement rules bite on *negotiating an agreement* at the door or by phone. **Addressed postal mail that solicits an inbound enquiry is not itself an unsolicited consumer agreement** — but if a mailer leads to a doorstep or phone negotiation, the cooling-off and disclosure regime engages, and a breach converts a 10-day cooling-off into ~6 months of unwind risk.

## 6. Cross-cutting findings

1. **Two regulators on two continents made the identical finding about fine print.** FTC ¶28 (disclaimers "not conspicuous… did not cure" the headline) and ASA 2018 issue 2 (qualifying text "not sufficiently prominent to override the overall impression"). A disclaimer that contradicts the headline is evidence *against* you, not a defence — the FTC specifically noted Opendoor's own disclaimer stood "in direct contravention to Opendoor's marketing."
2. **The valuation figure was never the violation. The comparison was.** Opendoor was not penalised for a bad AVM; it was penalised for a *net-proceeds chart* that prepopulated its offer and market value as identical, and for overstating the counterfactual costs of the alternative. Every enforcement action found here attaches to a **savings/gap claim**, not to an estimate.
3. **A published estimate is defensible; a priced commitment off the same estimate is not.** Andersen v Zillow shows the estimate survives legal attack when labelled as opinion with published error. Zillow Offers shows what happens nine months after you let that same number become an offer.
4. **Off-market is the hard regime and the only one that matters for mail.** Zestimate 1.83% on-market vs 7.01% off-market. Anyone quoting portal accuracy at a homeowner who is not listed is quoting the wrong number.
5. **No credible measured response rate exists for homeowner valuation mail.** The ANA/DMA 9%/4.9% is a small-sample voluntary self-report that moved 76% in one year; mail vendors — who have every incentive to inflate — quote 0.5–2%. Plan against the vendors' number.
6. **Australia has a data-provenance problem the US and UK do not.** PropTrack states on the record that Valuer General/land titles licences bar direct-marketing use of owner information, that such data cannot be sold, sub-licensed or downloaded, and that licences carry audit and termination rights. Owner-addressed mail in Australia needs a list whose provenance survives that question.
7. **The "reasonable grounds" burden (ACL) and the "competent and reliable evidence" burden (FTC final order) are the same obligation.** Both require the substantiation to exist *before* the claim is made, and both place the burden on the maker. The Purplebricks 2016 ruling is the worked example of failing it: the direction of the claim was accepted, the evidence chain for the number was not.

# 4. Breakthrough creative — what actually broke through, and which results survive scrutiny

A warning before the list. Most "famous direct mail case study" content online is trade-press or agency-authored, with no control group, no denominator, and no independent verification. Claims like "25% response rate" or "12,400% ROI" circulating on agency blogs ([uviaus](https://www.uviaus.com/blog/17-examples-of-b2b-direct-mail-campaigns-that-drove-roi), [magneticmarketing](https://magneticmarketing.com/blog/case-studies-in-direct-mail-campaigns-learning-from-real-world-examples), 2024-25) are **[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]** and should not be used for planning. They are self-selected survivors, usually 50–500 piece B2B sends to hand-picked targets. They are not evidence.

Below I separate the campaigns with **real experimental evidence** from the folklore.

---

## 4.1 The strongest evidence in existence: social-pressure voter mail

This is the closest experimental analogue to what Fields wants to do — **mailing people public-record data about themselves, unrequested, alongside a neighbour comparison** — and it is measured in randomised trials with hundreds of thousands of subjects.

**Gerber, A., Green, D. & Larimer, C. (2008), "Social Pressure and Voter Turnout: Evidence from a Large-Scale Field Experiment", *American Political Science Review* 102(1): 33-48**, as summarised in **Gerber & Green (2016), "Field Experiments on Voter Mobilization: An Overview of a Burgeoning Literature"** [PRIMARY DATA] — [PDF](https://isps.yale.edu/sites/default/files/publication/2016/02/Gerber_Green_FieldExperimentsVoterMobilization.pdf)

Four mailers of escalating personal exposure, 20,000 households per treatment arm, 100,000 control:

| Mailer | What it showed the recipient | Turnout effect |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline non-partisan GOTV (typical) | generic appeal | **+0.5 pp** (SE 0.3); meta-analysis of 85 studies: **~+0.75 pp** |
| "Civic Duty" | forceful appeal, no personal data | **+1.8 pp** |
| "Self" | **the recipient's own voting record** from the public voter file | **+4.9 pp** |
| "Neighbors" | the recipient's record **plus their neighbours' records, by name, on their block** | **+8.1 pp** |

"These quantities are all distinguishable from zero **and each other**." Pooling all social-pressure studies gives **+2.3 pp** versus ~+0.75 pp for ordinary mail.

**Three findings that matter enormously for Fields:**

1. **Showing someone their own data roughly 6–10×'d the effect of a generic mailer** (4.9 pp vs 0.5–0.75 pp). Personal data is the mechanism, not the decoration.
2. **Adding the neighbour comparison nearly doubled it again** (8.1 pp). Same structural insight as Opower.
3. **And it caused a backlash.** From the same review: the social pressure mailings, "especially those that confronted voters with their voting record and those of their neighbors (the highly effective 'Neighbors' mailing), **provoked outrage among some recipients** (Matland and Murray 2014), prompting a **search for messaging strategies that produced the turnout effect without as much agitation**."

That search produced the design rule Fields should copy:
- The **"Self"** mailer — *your own data only, no neighbours named* — retained the large majority of the effect (4.9 of 8.1 pp, ~60%) with "**only a modest level of resistance**."
- **Panagopoulos (2013)** replaced scolding with praise — an "honor roll" of perfect voters — keeping the norm mechanism while removing the shaming.
- Versions that "did not scold" had weaker effects but far less agitation.

Also note the ceiling: the "Neighbors" replication in a high-turnout Wisconsin election produced only **+1 pp overall**, rising to **+3 pp among those with a 30% base turnout rate**. The effect is concentrated in the disengaged — again, targeting.

**Transfer:** *your own home's data* is the engine. *Your named neighbours' data* is a bigger engine that also generates complaints. Fields can show comparable sales (already public, already de-identified by convention as an address+price) without ever framing it as "here is how you rank against the Smiths at number 14."

## 4.2 A photograph of the thing you own

**UK Behavioural Insights Team / Cabinet Office (2012), "Applying Behavioural Insights to Reduce Fraud, Error and Debt"** — the DVLA vehicle relicensing trial. BIT tested the standard DVLA letter against a redesigned letter carrying a blunt message ("Pay Your Tax or Lose Your [Make of Vehicle]") **and an image of the owner's own car**.

Reported result: payment rates rose **from 40% to 49%**.

**Verification status: [VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — government self-report, primary PDF now offline].** The trial's *existence* is confirmed in a peer-reviewed source — John, P. (2017), "Policy entrepreneurship in UK central government: The Behavioural Insights Team and the use of randomized controlled trials", [PMC5447898](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5447898/): "BIT worked with DVLA to find out whether a photo of the owner's car would encourage people who are behind with registration to update." But that paper reports **no response rates**, and the original Cabinet Office PDF (`BIT_FraudErrorDebt_accessible.pdf`) now returns 404/403 at both gov.uk and the National Archives. The 40%→49% figure is repeated widely in secondary literature but **I could not verify it against the primary document.** Treat as directional, not as a planning number. Note also it is confounded — the image was bundled with rewritten copy and a loss frame, so the photo's independent contribution is unknown.

**Why it still matters:** it is the only publicly-known large-scale government trial that put a picture of the recipient's own possession on a letter, and the stated rationale was that the image "make[s] the idea of losing their vehicle more salient" — attention plus personalisation. It is the direct precedent for a photo of the recipient's house.

## 4.3 Creative content genuinely moves demand — measured, in mail

**Bertrand, M., Karlan, D., Mullainathan, S., Shafir, E. & Zinman, J. (2010), "What's Advertising Content Worth? Evidence from a Consumer Credit Marketing Field Experiment", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 125(1): 263-306.** [PRIMARY DATA] — [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/125/1/263/1880334) · [Yale ISPS](https://isps.yale.edu/research/publications/isps10-006)

A South African consumer lender randomised **advertising content, loan price and offer deadline simultaneously** across a direct mail campaign. Findings:
- Advertising content significantly affects demand. Including a **photo of an attractive woman** raised loan take-up by about **as much as a 25% reduction in the interest rate** — a genuinely startling equivalence.
- Showing **fewer** example loans (less choice) *increased* take-up.
- Critically: "although it was difficult to predict **ex ante** which specific advertising features would matter most, the features that do matter have **large effects**."

**Transfer:** creative is worth as much as price, but you cannot reason your way to the winning creative — you have to test it. Fields should treat headline/format/photo as a randomised variable from mailing one, not as a thing to be decided in a meeting.

## 4.4 Social norm letters — and why the famous version of this finding is wrong

**⚠ This is a case where the widely-repeated government figure and the peer-reviewed figure disagree by a factor of three. Use the peer-reviewed one.**

**The press version.** [BIT Annual Update 2011-12](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a79565040f0b63d72fc4ed2/Behavioural-Insights-Team-Annual-Update-2011-12_0.pdf) [VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — government self-report] states that telling late taxpayers "most people in their towns had already paid their tax increased payment rates by 15 [percentage points]". Secondary sources render this as: control **67.5%** → national norm **72.5%** → postcode norm **79%** → home-town norm **83%**, with an estimated **£160 million** of tax debt advanced over six weeks. These numbers circulate everywhere.

**The peer-reviewed version.** **Hallsworth, M., List, J., Metcalfe, R. & Vlaev, I. (2017), "The behavioralist as tax collector: Using natural field experiments to enhance tax compliance", *Journal of Public Economics* 148: 14-31** [PRIMARY DATA] — [WRAP open-access PDF](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/88777/1/WRAP-behavioralist-tax-collector-Vlaev-2017.pdf) · [NBER w20007](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20007). Two natural field experiments, >200,000 individuals, HMRC administrative data, measured on payment within the first 23 days:

| Message variant | Effect vs control letter |
|---|---|
| **Basic norm** ("the great majority of people pay their tax on time") | **+1.3 pp** (SE 0.025) |
| **Country norm** (same, scoped to the UK) | **+2.1 pp** (SE 0.040) |
| **Minority norm** ("...you are currently in the very small minority who have not paid") | **+5.1 pp** (SE 0.11) |
| Public services message (gain- or loss-framed) | **+1.6 pp** (SE 0.030) — loss-framing hypothesis **not supported** |
| Financial/interest-cost messages (Experiment 2) | **+3.2 to +3.9 pp** |
| *Any* reminder letter vs no letter | **+6.7 pp** — recipients "nearly four [times]" as likely to pay |

Key results, in the authors' own terms:
- The **minority norm** effect "is much larger… around **70% of the reminder letter effect**", and is significantly greater than both the basic and country norms (both **p<0.001**).
- **"Descriptive norms have a significantly larger effect than injunctive norms on increasing payment rates."** (Note this *contrasts* with the Schultz 2007 energy result, where the injunctive norm's role was to suppress the boomerang rather than to drive the effect.)
- **The country norm was NOT significantly better than the basic norm** (diff = 0.017, p=0.26) — which the authors say "does not support the construal level theory". **This directly contradicts the "tighter geography is better" story in BIT's press summary.**
- **Persistence:** "Many of the effects generated by the treatments remained economically meaningful and statistically significant **seventy days after the letters were issued**" — the authors explicitly link this to Allcott & Rogers.
- **Heterogeneity by stake size:** effects were broadly similar across debt quartiles, except that "for those in the **top 1%** of the [debt] distribution, the loss-framed public service message increased the payment rate by **17.6 percentage points**."
- Revenue: **£4.9 million** accelerated in the first 23 days from the test messages; **>£9 million** overall.

**Transfer, corrected:** the mechanism that carried the effect was **not** geographic tightness — it was telling the recipient they were in a **small minority**, i.e. personally on the wrong side of a descriptive norm. That is a much more specific and more usable finding than "go local", and it is the same mechanism as Opower's high-decile effect and Ferraro & Price's high-user effect: **comparative data moves the people it identifies as outliers, and barely moves anyone else.** It also carries the obvious risk — being told you are an outlier is precisely what generates the boomerang and the complaint.

**Method note for Fields:** this discrepancy is itself the lesson. The government's own press summary of its own trial overstated the headline by roughly 3× versus the peer-reviewed analysis. Do not plan against press-released effect sizes, including your own.

## 4.5 Why physical mail specifically

[JICMAIL](https://www.jicmail.org.uk/data/) is the UK's joint industry currency for mail measurement — panel-based, methodologically published, and the nearest thing to primary measurement in this space (though it is industry-funded; treat as **[PRIMARY DATA, INDUSTRY-FUNDED]**).

- Mail **lifespan in the home: 8.6 days** (Q4 2024) — the item is physically present and re-encountered for over a week.
- **Item reach 1.17 people** — more than one household member sees it.
- **4.86 interactions** with an item across a 28-day window; Business Mail read **4.8 times**, average **186 seconds** of interaction ([JICMAIL Q4 2024 release](https://www.jicmail.org.uk/news/news-q4-2024-results-mail-read-rates-hit-an-all-time-high-as-the-channel-continues-to-assert-its-super-touchpoint-strengths/)).

This maps onto the Allcott & Rogers finding that conservation effort peaks ~10 days after arrival: the physical artefact keeps working for roughly as long as it stays on the kitchen bench.

**Response rate benchmarks — do not plan against these.** The [ANA/DMA Response Rate Report](https://theworldsgreatestmarketing.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/rr-2024-02-ana-response-rate-report-2023.pdf) is the standard citation **[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — voluntary self-report by member marketers]**. Its instability across editions is the most informative thing about it:

| Edition | B2C house list | B2C prospect list |
|---|---|---|
| 2017 | ~5.1% | — |
| 2018 | **9.0%** | **4.9%** |
| 2023 | **5.3%** | **2.9%** |

A **76% jump then a 41% fall** in a mature channel is a sampling artefact signature, not a market movement, and the ANA itself cautions the figures rest on a low sample size. Campaigns that flopped do not get submitted.

Two further cautions:
- The commonly-repeated "direct mail is 36× email" line is **misleading** — it compares a targeted, expensive, opt-out channel against a cheap, high-volume, opt-in one, with "response" defined differently in each.
- **Fields' mail is a prospect list, not a house list** — the recipient has no prior relationship. And note the direction of bias in §2/§3: the print vendors *selling* mail quote **0.5–2%** for cold real-estate prospecting, *below* the association's survey. **When the party with the incentive to inflate quotes the lower number, use the lower number.**

| Campaign | Claim | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Land Rover 3D View-Master mailer | demonstrated off-road capability via nostalgic device | **[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]** — no verifiable response data found |

## 4.7 The synthesis for section 4

The mechanisms with real experimental support, ranked by evidence quality:

1. **Show the recipient their own data.** (Gerber/Green: 4.9 pp vs 0.5 pp. Opower's entire premise.)
2. **Use a descriptive norm that identifies the recipient as an outlier — that is where the effect lives.** (Hallsworth: minority norm **+5.1 pp** vs basic norm +1.3 pp; Opower top decile 6.3% vs bottom ~0%; Ferraro & Price ~94% larger relative effect among high users.) Note the peer-reviewed evidence did **not** support "tighter geography is automatically better" (country norm vs basic norm, p=0.26) — the popular version of that claim comes from a press summary, not the paper.
3. **Pair any comparison with an approving/constructive frame, never a bare rank.** (Schultz 2007 injunctive norm; Panagopoulos honor roll.)
4. **Make it physically salient — including, plausibly, a picture of the thing itself.** (JICMAIL lifespan; DVLA vehicle photo, unverified.)
5. **Randomise the creative — you cannot predict which element wins.** (Bertrand et al.)
6. **Repeat on a cadence, front-loaded then tapered.** (Allcott & Rogers: 2.5–4.2× the one-shot.)
7. **Do not name third parties.** The "Neighbors" variant is the one that generated outrage for a ~60% incremental gain over "Self".

## A1. Target's pregnancy-prediction score (2012)

`[UNSOURCED]` — the father, the store and the date are all anonymous; no corroborating record has ever surfaced. **Eric Siegel** (founder of Predictive Analytics World, i.e. the person closest to Pole's original talk) argues the story is at best unverified and probably not caused by the prediction model at all. ## A3. Home energy reports (Opower) — the closest analogue to a home-data mailer

This is the single best comparator in the record: an **unsolicited physical mailer, sent to a household, containing a quantitative statement about that specific household's property, benchmarked against the neighbours**, at national scale, with randomised measurement. It is what Fields is proposing, in a different vertical.

**Opt-out is the honest measure of creepiness, and it is small.** Hunt Allcott, "Social norms and energy conservation," *Journal of Public Economics* 95(9–10), 2011, evaluating 17 randomised Opower experiments `[PRIMARY DATA]`:

> "cumulative opt-out rates range from **0.1% to 3.3%**. The most common reasons for opting out is the perception that **the comparisons are unfair or inaccurate** or that the reports are a **'waste of resources.'**"

> "Across the different utilities, customers that opt out tend to have **higher pre-treatment electricity usage and are also older and lower-income**."

An earlier working-paper snapshot: "In the first six months of 2009, **247 households (0.6 percent of the Treatment group) opted out**."
Sources: https://www.oracle.com/us/industries/utilities/social-norms-energy-cons-jpe-5225418.pdf · https://dspace.mit.edu/bitstream/handle/1721.1/51712/2009-014.pdf

**And the intervention worked.** "the average program reduces energy consumption by **2.0%**"; per-experiment ATEs "range from **1.4 to 3.3%** of baseline usage." `[PRIMARY DATA]`, same source. ## A5. Backlash to "we know what your house is worth" mail specifically

**Honest finding: there is no documented scandal.** I searched multiple phrasings (homeowner + creepy + home value + direct mail + public records; unsolicited offers; regulator complaints) and found **no press incident, no regulatory action, and no organised complaint corpus** attaching to a mailer that tells a homeowner what their home is worth. `[UNSOURCED — absence of evidence]`

What exists instead is a **volume/nuisance** complaint stream, not a **privacy** complaint stream:
- Consumer/press coverage of unsolicited "we want to buy your house" contact frames it as frustrating, confusing and relentless — not as an invasion. Sources: https://orchard.com/blog/posts/getting-unsolicited-offers-to-buy-my-house · https://www.realtor.com/advice/sell/unsolicited-homebuying-offers-how-to-deal/ `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]`
- Australian coverage of unsolicited agent contact concerns **electronic** channels (SMS/calls after open homes) and routes to the Spam Act / Do Not Call Register / ACMA, not to the content of the message. *The Guardian*, 17 Jun 2026: "Australia has strict rules and regulations around spam, which mean real estate agents can't contact you with marketing messages if you don't want them to." Source: https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2026/jun/17/mobile-number-property-viewing-open-house-data-rights-law-stop-real-estate-agents-messages-spam `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]`

**The binding Australian constraint on a physical mailer is APP 7 (Privacy Act 1988), not any creepiness norm.** OAIC guidance `[PRIMARY DATA]`:
- Where personal information was collected **from a third party rather than from the individual**, direct marketing is permitted only if the individual consented **or it is impracticable to obtain consent** — *and* a **simple means of opting out** is provided, *and* **each** communication carries a **prominent opt-out statement**, *and* the individual has not opted out.
- On request, the organisation must tell the individual **where it got their information**, unless impracticable or unreasonable — and the organisation bears the burden of justifying refusal.
- **Sensitive information** may be used for direct marketing **only with express consent** — no impracticability exemption.
Source: https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-7-app-7-direct-marketing

Read together: **the law already assumes you will be asked "how did you get this?" and requires you to have an answer.** A mailer that pre-answers that question is not merely being polite; it is pre-satisfying a statutory obligation.

## TOPIC B — WHAT THE RESEARCH ACTUALLY FOUND

## B1. The personalisation–privacy paradox: the founding result

Awad & Krishnan, "The Personalization Privacy Paradox: An Empirical Evaluation of Information Transparency and the Willingness to be Profiled Online for Personalization," *MIS Quarterly* 30(1), 2006. `[PRIMARY DATA]`
**Finding, and note its direction — it is counter-intuitive:** consumers who place the *highest* value on information transparency are **less** willing to be profiled, not more. Giving people better visibility into data practices does not convert them; it sorts them. The people who care most about knowing are the people most likely to decline.
Source: https://misq.umn.edu/misq/article/30/1/13/392/The-Personalization-Privacy-Paradox-An-Empirical

**Implication:** "just be transparent" is not a universal solvent. Transparency changes *who* responds, and it is not free.

---

## B2. The single most important result for a home-data mailer — covert vs overt collection

**Aguirre, Mahr, Grewal, de Ruyter & Wetzels (2015), "Unraveling the Personalization Paradox: The Effect of Information Collection and Trust-Building Strategies on Online Advertisement Effectiveness," *Journal of Retailing* 91(1), 34–49.** `[PRIMARY DATA]`
Full text: https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/15747/1/AGUIRRE%20et%20al%20%202015%20(2).pdf

**Pre-study 1 (Facebook field test, 5 days, two brands).** Personalisation is **inverted-U**, not monotonic. More is not better; more is worse past a point.
- Financial services: CTR **0.017%** (no personalisation) → **0.077%** (targeting on stated interests, z = −4.61, p ≤ .001) → **0.032%** (ad personalised with the person's own demographics, z = 3.15, p = .002).
- Dog food: **0.047%** → **0.064%** (p = .084, one-tailed) → **0.033%** (z = 2.60, p = .009).
**High personalisation cut CTR by 58% and 48% respectively versus moderate personalisation — and for dog food, ended up below the no-personalisation baseline.**

**Pre-study 2 (natural experiment, Dutch cookie law, 381 days).** When enforcement began 1 Jan 2013 and users were suddenly *made aware* that data had been collected, CTR on an existing personalised campaign dropped **−11% immediately**; it recovered after **nine weeks**, then **exceeded** the original baseline and kept rising.
**Read: the shock is to the discovery, not to the practice. Disclosure has a short-run cost and a long-run premium.**

**Study 1 (n = 120 after exclusions; 2 × 2, more/less personalised × overt/covert collection).** This is the core result.
- **Overt** collection: more personalisation raised click-through intention from **M = 2.67 → 4.38** (F(1,116) = 11.44, p = .001). A 64% lift.
- **Covert** collection: more personalisation did **nothing** — **M = 2.94 → 3.16**, F(1,116) = .22, **p = .641**.
- Interaction F(1,116) = 4.64, p = .033. Mediated by **feelings of vulnerability**, significant in the covert condition (z = −2.00, p = .045) and absent in the overt (z = .66, p = .510).
- A supplementary study (n = 39) separated *cognitive privacy concern* from *affective vulnerability*: privacy showed **no** difference (p = .298); **vulnerability did** (p = .043). **Creepiness is an emotion, not an evaluation. People do not reason their way to it, and they will not be reasoned out of it.**

**Study 2 (context/trust transfer).** Under covert collection, a highly personalised ad drove vulnerability and depressed click-through **on an untrustworthy site** (indirect effect β = −.20, 95% CI [−.45, −.04]) but **not on a trusted site** (β = −.03, CI [−.18, .10] — includes zero). **Institutional credibility absorbs the vulnerability.**

**Study 3 (n = 121; the disclosure icon).** A small in-ad icon that stated how data was collected, linked to the policy, and offered opt-out **closed the covert penalty entirely**:
- No icon: overt **M = 4.00** vs covert **M = 2.96** (F(1,119) = 5.48, p = .021) — a 26% penalty for covert.
- With icon: overt **3.43** vs covert **3.77** — difference **not significant** (p = .448).
- Within the covert condition, the icon lifted click-through **2.96 → 3.77** (p = .082).
**The cheapest available fix — one line disclosing source and offering opt-out — recovered essentially all of the covertness penalty.**

Authors' own managerial conclusion: "retailers should be **proactive in providing comprehensible, visible notifications of their information collection practices**."

## B3. John, Kim & Barasz — "inferred vs volunteered" and acceptable information flows

The HBR piece — **Leslie K. John, Tami Kim, Kate Barasz, "Ads That Don't Overstep: How to Make Sure You Don't Take Personalization Too Far," *Harvard Business Review* 96(1), Jan–Feb 2018, 62–69** (https://hbr.org/2018/01/ads-that-dont-overstep) `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM]` — is the practitioner summary. The underlying data are in:

**Tami Kim, Kate Barasz, Leslie K. John, "Why Am I Seeing This Ad? The Effect of Ad Transparency on Ad Effectiveness," *Journal of Consumer Research* 45(5), Feb 2019, 906–932.** `[PRIMARY DATA]`
Full text: https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Kim%20et%20al%202019%20-%20Why%20Am%20I%20Seeing%20This%20Ad_c30f31d3-1f7f-4fe3-a167-73e2d35e40b8.pdf · https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/45/5/906/4985191

**Their frame — Nissenbaum-style contextual integrity, made operational.** Acceptability is not about *how much* is known; it is about whether the **information flow** matched a norm. Their two offline analogies:
- using data from another site to target you here is "**akin to talking behind someone's back**";
- stating an inference you drew about someone is "**taboo**" — "making an overt inference about someone can be taboo."

**Study 1 (N = 149; factor analysis of 30 real Google/Facebook targeting practices).** Two dimensions explain most of the variance in acceptability (7-point scale):
- **Where it came from:** within-website **M = 4.58** (SD 1.52) vs cross-website **M = 3.05** (SD 1.80); t(148) = −10.61, **p < .001**.
- **How it was obtained:** stated by the consumer **M = 4.08** (SD 1.88) vs **inferred by the firm M = 3.11** (SD 1.70); t(148) = −7.30, **p < .001**.
**Note the absolute levels: inferred (3.11) and cross-site (3.05) both sit BELOW the 4.0 scale midpoint — i.e. net-unacceptable, not merely less liked.**

**Study 2 (N = 449; disclosing within- vs cross-website).** Ad effectiveness: cross-website **M = 2.83** < within-website **M = 3.56** (p < .001) and < **baseline with no disclosure at all M = 3.87** (p < .001). Within-website vs baseline: **n.s.** (p = .13).

**Study 3 (N = 348; disclosing stated vs inferred attributes) — the directly load-bearing one.** Ad effectiveness:
- **inferred M = 2.52** vs **stated M = 3.10** (t(232) = 2.77, p = .01)
- **inferred M = 2.52** vs **baseline (no disclosure) M = 2.96** (t(230) = 2.22, p = .03)
- stated vs baseline: **n.s.** (p = .53)
Mediated by privacy concern crowding out interest in personalisation (indirect effect b = −.17, SE .08, 95% CI [−.35, −.01]).

**So the answer to "does explaining the source help or hurt?" is: it depends entirely on what the source is.**
- Disclosing an **acceptable** source is **free** (equal to saying nothing) in the lab, and **positive** in the field on a trusted platform.
- Disclosing an **unacceptable** source is **worse than saying nothing** — a 15% hit vs baseline in Study 3, 27% in Study 2. **Transparency about a norm-violating flow does not launder it; it advertises it.**

**Study 4 (N = 462; real ads from participants' own Facebook feeds, coded by them).** Transparency score → ad effectiveness β = .31 (SE .09, p < .001); moderated by platform trust, interaction β = .15 (SE .06, p = .02). Johnson–Neyman: the benefit of disclosing acceptable flows is significant **only where trust > 3.56 on a 7-point scale**. Below that, **no kind of transparency helps.** "even acceptable [transparency] is unlikely to overcome low trust."

**Studies 5a/5b (field, loyalty-program redemption sites, two weeks, n = 9,079 and n = 1,862 users).** Changing a recommendation sidebar's title was the entire manipulation:
- 5a "Recommended based on **your clicks on our site**" vs "Recommended": click propensity **.10 vs .09** (Wald χ² = 100.56, p < .001); dwell **8.52s vs 6.35s** (p < .001); spend on recommended products **.22 vs .16** (p = .04).
- 5b "Recommended based on **what you've shared with us**" vs "Recommended": click propensity **.12 vs .08** — a **50% relative lift** (Wald χ² = 76.06, p < .001); dwell **8.31s vs 5.98s** (p < .001); revenue **n.s.** (p = .35).
**In a trusted context, one clause naming an acceptable source lifted engagement 11–50% at zero media cost.**

## B4. Tucker — control beats concealment

**Catherine Tucker, "Social Networks, Personalized Advertising, and Privacy Controls," *Journal of Marketing Research* 51(5), 2014, 546–562.** `[PRIMARY DATA]`
Working paper full text: http://www.netinst.org/Tucker_10-07.pdf · https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1509/jmr.10.0355

Randomised field experiment: a US non-profit's Facebook campaigns, **1.2 million users**, 5-week window straddling Facebook's mid-2010 privacy-control overhaul. **Facebook did not change how ads were targeted — only how much control users felt they had.**

- After the change, "**users were twice as likely to click on personalized ads**." Estimated CTR rose **+0.024 percentage points on a 0.0232 baseline — i.e. it doubled.**
- **No comparable change** for ads that did not signal use of private information. (Placebo holds.)
- **Before** the change, during heavy privacy press coverage, personalised ads performed **slightly worse** than merely targeted ones (negative, marginally significant `Personalized` coefficient).
- The gain was **largest for ads using less commonly available private information** — i.e. control helps most exactly where the personalisation is most unusual and most exposing.

Tucker frames the risk in the same emotional register as Aguirre: consumers may find personalisation appealing, or "**not only creepy but off-putting**" if they feel privacy was violated. **The identical ad flipped from underperforming to double-performing without a single word of copy changing. What changed was the recipient's sense of agency.**

## B5. Reactance, and when a justification is needed

**White, Zahay, Thorbjørnsen & Shavitt (2008), "Getting too personal: Reactance to highly personalized email solicitations," *Marketing Letters* 19(1), 39–50.** `[PRIMARY DATA]`
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11002-007-9027-9 · https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/getting-too-personal-reactance-to-highly-personalized-email-solic/

- "Consumers experience **personalization reactance** in response to highly personalized messages **when the fit between the offer in the message and consumers' personal characteristics is not explicitly justified** by firms."
- Critical moderator: "**for those consumers with higher perceived utility, justification of personalization is less important** because highly personalized messages are less likely to elicit reactance."

**Read: justification is a substitute for usefulness.** If the recipient can immediately see what the message is *for*, the deep personalisation is forgiven. If they cannot, the personalisation reads as surveillance and they need to be told why you know.

**Tene & Polonetsky, "A Theory of Creepy: Technology, Privacy and Shifting Social Norms," *Yale Journal of Law & Technology* 16, 59.** `[PRIMARY DATA — legal scholarship, not empirical]` https://yjolt.org/theory-creepy-technology-privacy-and-shifting-social-norms
Their contribution is the definition: "creepy" occupies the space where a practice is **lawful and not demonstrably harmful, yet violates a social norm** because the technology has outrun the norm. Their proposed handles are "enhanced transparency," "accessibility to information in usable format," and "the elusive principle of context." Their worked examples turn on *who is doing the looking and why*: checking a nanny's driving record reads as responsible; checking the other parents' reads as sinister. **Same data, same method, different standing.**

## B6. The aggregate picture

A 2025 meta-analytic review of 53 experimental studies reports that **personalised advertising is generally more effective than generic advertising** on persuasion and attitudes.
Source: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00218499.2025.2467763 (403 on direct fetch; abstract and study count verified via index) `[VENDOR OR PRESS CLAIM — meta-analysis, not independently verified here]`

Together with B2–B4 this gives the shape: **personalisation is on average strongly positive; the negative cases are a specific, identifiable subset defined by covertness, inference, and lack of control — not by depth of detail.**

## WHERE THE LINE APPEARS TO SIT

Every result above converges on the same claim, from five different methods: **the offence is not knowing. The offence is the recipient discovering, at the moment of reading, that they cannot account for how you know.**

1. **The line is drawn by provenance, not by depth.** Kim/Barasz/John Study 1 shows acceptability is explained by *where from* and *how obtained* — not by sensitivity or volume. Stated (4.08) and within-context (4.58) sit above the midpoint; inferred (3.11) and cross-context (3.05) sit below it. A very detailed statement from an acceptable source is fine; a thin statement from an unacceptable one is not.

2. **Inference is the specific trigger.** "Making an overt inference about someone can be taboo." Disclosing an inference is measurably *worse than saying nothing* (2.52 vs 2.96, p = .03). A stated fact you retrieved and a conclusion you drew are different objects to a reader, even when both are correct.

3. **Detection is the event, not collection.** The Dutch cookie law cost −11% CTR without changing a single data practice (Aguirre pre-study 2); Facebook's privacy overhaul doubled personalised CTR without changing targeting (Tucker). The mailer's fate is decided by what the recipient concludes about you in the two seconds after they realise you know something.

4. **The reaction is affective, not evaluative.** Aguirre isolated it: vulnerability moved (p = .043) while measured privacy concern did not (p = .298). You cannot argue someone out of creeped-out. You can only avoid inducing it.

5. **Naming the source is the cheapest known fix — conditional on the source being defensible.** Aguirre Study 3: a disclosure icon erased the entire covertness penalty (2.96 → 3.77). Kim 5b: one clause naming an acceptable source lifted clicks 50%. But the same move applied to a bad source amplifies the damage. **Disclosure is a multiplier on the provenance, in whichever direction the provenance points.**

6. **Control substitutes for concealment, and beats it.** Target's answer was camouflage: hide the inference behind lawnmower coupons, and "as long as a pregnant woman thinks she hasn't been spied on, she'll use the coupons." Tucker's natural experiment is the same problem solved the other way: give people control, change nothing else, and effectiveness doubles. **Camouflage is a strategy that only works until someone notices; agency works better and survives being noticed.**

7. **Utility buys latitude.** White et al.: justification matters most when perceived utility is low. Home energy reports — an unsolicited mailer quantifying your own house against your neighbours, at national scale — sustain **0.1–3.3% opt-out** and 1.4–3.3% behaviour change, because the recipient can see the point immediately. **Usefulness is the licence.**

8. **Being wrong is worse than being right.** The dominant complaint about home energy reports is not surveillance but accuracy — "I don't have confidence in the data," "maybe they're vacant homes." Shutterfly's disaster was a false positive. Target's much-repeated anecdote is unverified and probably never happened; the verified harm in that whole literature is OfficeMax's — a data-plumbing accident where an internal free-text note rendered into an address block. **A confidently stated wrong fact about someone's home is the most likely real failure, and the one with no recovery path for the recipient.**

9. **Trust is a gate, not a dial.** Kim Study 4: the benefit of transparency exists only above 3.56/7 platform trust; below it, nothing helps. An unknown sender cannot disclose its way to acceptability. Institutional credibility must exist first, or be established in the same artefact.

10. **The template must not presume valence.** Meyer's case is the purest: the algorithm correctly identified the most significant thing that happened to him that year, then wrapped it in party graphics. **A correct retrieval plus an assumed emotional frame equals cruelty.** Any auto-generated statement about a home — a fall in value, a long time on market, a distressed comparable next door — carries the same risk.

**Net: for a mailer about someone's own home, the line sits at inference-presented-as-fact, from an unnamed source, with no way out.** Everything on the safe side of that line — a retrievable public fact, sourced in plain words, framed as useful, with a stated opt-out and an obvious way to correct it — is not merely tolerated by the evidence; it is the configuration the evidence says outperforms saying less.

## 1. Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) and the Australian Privacy Principles

### 1.1 Does the business fall under the Act at all? — the small business exemption (s 6D)

`[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]` OAIC, *Small business* (undated, current 2026):
https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/small-business

> "A small business is one with an annual turnover of $3 million or less."

Annual turnover for this purpose "includes all income from all sources" and excludes "assets held, capital gains or
proceeds of capital sales" (OAIC, same page; the mechanics are in **s 6DA** *What is the annual turnover of a business?*).
A "small business operator" is carved out of the definition of "organisation" and so is not an APP entity —
**Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) s 6D**, https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03712/latest/text (compilation No. 104,
in force 4 June 2026). *I could not retrieve s 6D verbatim — see sourcing note above.*

**A pre-revenue business is under $3M and therefore prima facie exempt. That is not the end of the analysis.**
OAIC lists the categories of small business that the Act covers **despite** the turnover threshold
`[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]`, same page:

- health service providers;
- **entities that "trade in personal information"**;
- Commonwealth contract service providers;
- residential tenancy database operators;
- credit reporting bodies;
- AML/CTF reporting entities;
- employee associations; protected action ballot agents;
- telecommunications data-retention service providers;
- Consumer Data Right accredited entities;
- **entities related to a body corporate that is itself covered by the Privacy Act**;
- entities prescribed by the Privacy Regulation 2013;
- entities that have voluntarily opted in (**s 6EA**).

OAIC defines the trading exception `[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]`:

> "provide[s] a benefit, service or advantage to collect personal information, or disclose[s] personal information
> for a benefit, service or advantage."

**⚠ This is the single most consequential clause for this business and it cuts both ways.** The exception is not
limited to *selling* lists. On OAIC's own wording it is also engaged where the business **provides a benefit,
service or advantage in order to collect personal information about another individual from someone else** —
which is what paying a third-party data supplier, a paid property-data API, or a paid scraping/enrichment service
for owner- or address-linked records looks like on its face. It is also engaged if leads (address + owner-linked
data) are ever passed to an agent, a partner, or a buyer for money or for any "benefit, service or advantage".

**NEEDS A LAWYER.** Whether paid ingestion of property/owner data, or any future onward supply of homeowner
leads, makes this business a "small business operator that trades in personal information" — and therefore a full
APP entity with **all** of APP 1–13 applying — is the threshold question for everything in section 1 of this
document. Do not assume the $3M exemption applies. A closely related open point: whether the exemption is lost
prospectively only, or whether prior collection is re-characterised.

`[SECONDARY — COMMENTARY]` Note also that the exemption never covered everything. The **statutory tort for serious
invasions of privacy**, inserted by the *Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024* (Cth), is
described in commentary as applying irrespective of the small business exemption — a sole trader can be sued under
it "as readily as a bank": Scalesuite, *Privacy Act Changes for SMEs* (2026),
https://www.scalesuite.com.au/resources/privacy-act-changes-for-smes ; MinterEllison, *Privacy and other
Legislation Amendment Act 2024 now in effect* (2025), https://www.minterellison.com/articles/privacy-and-other-legislation-amendment-act-2024-now-in-effect
**I have not verified the tort's commencement date or its elements against the statute — NEEDS A LAWYER.**

### 1.3 Is a residential address "personal information"?

`[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]` OAIC, *APP Guidelines Chapter B — Key concepts*,
https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-b-key-concepts

Section 6(1) definition, as reproduced by OAIC:

> "information or an opinion about an identified individual, or an individual who is reasonably identifiable:
> whether the information or opinion is true or not; and whether the information or opinion is recorded in a
> material form or not"

Three elements matter here:

1. **Address is listed as a common example of personal information** — OAIC Ch B, **[B.89]**.
2. **"Reasonably identifiable" is an objective, contextual test** — **[B.94]–[B.96]**: the nature and amount of the
   information, the circumstances of receipt, access restrictions, **other information the entity holds**, and the
   practicability of identification. [B.96] states that identification that is technically possible but so
   impracticable as to be unlikely does not qualify.
3. **"About an individual"** — [B.91]: information "about" an individual may extend beyond bare identifiers, and an
   *opinion* qualifies.

**Applied to this fact pattern (description of the risk, not a conclusion):** a bare street address in isolation
may or may not be "about an individual". But the mail piece proposed is not a bare address. It is (a) an address,
(b) linked in the sender's own systems to that dwelling's attributes and sale history, (c) carrying an **opinion**
about the monetary value of an asset, (d) posted to the person who lives there. Once the business also holds or can
readily obtain owner names (Queensland title/valuation/rates data, scraped listing history, agent records), the
"reasonably identifiable" limb becomes much easier to satisfy because [B.94] expressly counts **other information
the entity holds**. A value estimate is an opinion about that individual's asset. **NEEDS A LAWYER** on whether the
particular dataset as held is personal information; but treating it as *not* personal information is the fragile
position, not the safe one.

**Note the seldom-quoted trap:** OAIC Ch B **[B.26]** — an entity "collects" personal information only where it is
collected "for inclusion in a record or generally available publication" (s 6(1)). Scraping into a database is
squarely inclusion in a record.

### 1.4 APP 3 — collection of solicited personal information

`[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]` OAIC, *Read the Australian Privacy Principles*,
https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/read-the-australian-privacy-principles

Load-bearing text:

> **APP 3.5** — "An APP entity must collect personal information only by lawful and fair means."

> **APP 3.6** — an organisation "must collect personal information about an individual only from the individual
> unless … it is unreasonable or impracticable to do so" (agencies) / unless the individual consents to collection
> from someone other than the individual.

**Two obligations therefore attach to the *sourcing* step, before any mail is printed:** collection must be by
**fair means**, and collection from a third party rather than from the individual must be justified.

### 1.5 APP 5 — notification of collection

`[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]` OAIC, *Read the APPs* (as above):

> **APP 5.1** — "At or before the time or, if that is not practicable, as soon as practicable after, an APP entity
> collects personal information about an individual, the entity must take such steps (if any) as are reasonable in
> the circumstances: to notify the individual of such matters referred to in subclause 5.2 as are reasonable in the
> circumstances; or to otherwise ensure that the individual is aware of any such matters."

APP 5.2 matters include: the entity's identity and contact details; **the fact that and circumstances in which the
information was collected from someone other than the individual**; whether collection was required by law; the
purposes of collection; the main consequences if the information were not collected; usual disclosure recipients;
how to access, correct and complain; and overseas disclosure.

**The "reasonable steps (if any)" wording is the carve-out, and it is narrower than it looks.** See §1.6.

### 1.6 Scraping and "publicly available" — the Clearview AI determination

`[PRIMARY — DETERMINATION]` *Commissioner initiated investigation into Clearview AI, Inc. (Privacy)*
**[2021] AICmr 54**, 14 October 2021, Australian Information Commissioner:
https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0016/11284/Commissioner-initiated-investigation-into-Clearview-AI,-Inc.-Privacy-2021-AICmr-54-14-October-2021.pdf
(OAIC media statement: https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/clearview-ai-breached-australians-privacy)

Verbatim, from the determination:

> **[168]** "An APP entity must collect personal information by fair means. A 'fair means' of collecting
> information is one that does not involve intimidation or deception, and is not unreasonably intrusive. Collection
> may also be unfair where an entity misrepresents the purpose or effect of collection."

> **[169]** "When assessing whether a collection is 'unfair' for the purposes of APP 3.5, all the circumstances must
> be considered. For example, it would usually be unfair to collect personal information covertly without the
> knowledge of the individual."

> **[173]** "In these circumstances and in the absence of specific and timely information about the respondent's
> collection practices, I am satisfied that the respondent's collection of Scraped images and vectors constituted
> **covert collection**."

> **[179]** "Having regard to the kind of information collected by the respondent, the respondent's **commercial
> purposes**, and the **covert and indiscriminate method of collection**, I consider that the covert collection of
> Scraped images and vectors was unreasonably intrusive."

> **[180]** "I find that the respondent interfered with the privacy of individuals by collecting Australians'
> Scraped Images and vectors derived from these images, **by unfair means in breach of APP 3.5**."

And on APP 5 — the paragraph most directly transferable to a mail-out business:

> **[188]** "…a privacy policy is a transparency mechanism that, in accordance with APP 1.4, must include
> information about an entity's personal information handling practices… **It is not generally a way of providing
> notice under APP 5** or obtaining consent."

> **[189]** "Even if the respondent's Privacy Policy and/or Data Policy had included all of the information listed
> at APP 5.2, I am not satisfied that this would have constituted reasonable steps under APP 5 in circumstances
> where: **The respondent's business model involves covertly collecting personal information from third party
> sources, rather than directly collecting personal information from individuals. It is unlikely that individuals
> … would have been aware of the respondent's Privacy Policy or would have sought it out, as most of these
> individuals would have had no direct dealings with the respondent.**"

**What this determination does and does not establish.** It concerned **sensitive information** (biometric facial
images), which attracts APP 3.3 and a higher bar; property data is not sensitive information. So Clearview is **not**
authority that all scraping of public data is unfair. What it *does* establish, and what is directly transferable:

- "publicly available" is **not** a defence to APP 3.5 — the Commissioner considered and rejected that framing at
  [170]–[179];
- collection that is **covert** (the individual has no idea it happened), **indiscriminate** (whole populations, not
  targeted), and for **private commercial purposes** is the combination that drove the unfairness finding at [179];
- **a website privacy policy is not, by itself, "reasonable steps" under APP 5 where the individual has never dealt
  with you** [189]. A business whose entire model is third-party collection of people who have never heard of it
  sits precisely in the [189] fact pattern.

`[SECONDARY — COMMENTARY]` The determination was affirmed on the jurisdictional question by the AAT:
Gadens, *The AAT confirms that the Privacy Act applies to Clearview AI's conduct*,
https://www.gadens.com/legal-insights/the-decision-is-clear-the-aat-confirms-that-the-privacy-act-applies-to-clearview-ais-conduct/

**NEEDS A LAWYER** on whether the Clearview reasoning extends to non-sensitive, property-linked personal
information collected from public listing sites and public registers, and on what "reasonable steps" under APP 5
would look like for a business whose *first* contact with the individual is the mail piece itself. One structural
observation worth putting to a lawyer: the mail piece is a candidate vehicle for APP 5 notification, because it is
the first practicable moment of contact — APP 5.1 expressly contemplates notification "as soon as practicable
after" collection.

### 1.7 APP 7 — direct marketing (the operative provision for post)

`[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]` OAIC, *APP Guidelines Chapter 7 — APP 7 Direct marketing*,
https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-7-app-7-direct-marketing
(PDF version: https://www.oaic.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0011/1235/app-guidelines-chapter-7-v1.1.pdf)

**Direct marketing expressly includes post.** OAIC **[7.9]**: direct marketing involves the "use and/or disclosure
of personal information to communicate directly with an individual to promote goods and services", across telephone,
SMS, **mail**, email and online advertising. **[7.11]** gives as an example "sending an individual a catalogue in the
mail addressed to them by name".

The provisions, as reproduced by OAIC:

> **APP 7.1** — "If an organisation holds personal information about an individual, the organisation must not use or
> disclose the information for the purpose of direct marketing."

> **APP 7.2** — permits use where **all** of: "the organisation collected the information from the individual; and
> the individual would reasonably expect the organisation to use or disclose the information for that purpose; and
> the organisation provides a simple means by which the individual may easily request not to receive direct
> marketing communications" (and the individual has not made such a request).

> **APP 7.3** — applies where "the organisation collected the information from: **the individual and the individual
> would not reasonably expect** the organisation to use or disclose the information for that purpose; **or someone
> other than the individual**; and either: **the individual has consented** to the use or disclosure of the
> information for that purpose; **or it is impracticable to obtain that consent**" — **plus** a simple opt-out means
> **plus**, in **each** direct marketing communication, "a prominent statement" drawing the individual's attention to
> the right to opt out (or otherwise drawing their attention to it).

> **APP 7.4** — sensitive information requires consent, full stop.

> **APP 7.6–7.7** — an individual may request (a) not to receive direct marketing communications, (b) that the
> organisation not use or disclose the information for that purpose, and (c) **that the organisation provide its
> source of the information**. The organisation must comply "within a reasonable period after the request is made"
> and **must not charge** the individual.

**Which limb applies here: APP 7.3, not 7.2.** Where the address was obtained from public/scraped sources rather
than from the individual, the entity is in **APP 7.3(a)(ii)** ("someone other than the individual"). Consent will
not exist, so the entity is relying on **"impracticable to obtain that consent"**, and OAIC **[7.24]** sets the bar:

> "Whether it is 'impracticable' for an organisation to obtain consent will depend on a number of factors, including
> the time and cost involved."

with the gloss that **inconvenience alone is insufficient** — the burden must be "excessive in all the
circumstances". *(Note the circularity a lawyer will want to address: it is not obviously "impracticable" to seek
consent from someone whose postal address you have and are about to post to.)*

**Three concrete obligations follow if APP 7 applies:**

1. A **simple opt-out mechanism** — for postal marketing this means something the recipient can actually use without
   going online or paying (OAIC **[7.28]** requires opt-out statements "written in plain English", "positioned
   prominently", with a font size "easy to read").
2. A **prominent opt-out statement in EVERY mail piece** — APP 7.3(d)/(e). This is not a one-off; it attaches to each
   communication.
3. A **source-disclosure capability** — APP 7.6(c)/(d) with OAIC **[7.45]–[7.47]**: on request, the organisation must
   tell the individual **where it got their information**, generally within 30 days, free of charge, unless doing so
   is "impracticable or unreasonable". *Operationally this means per-record provenance must be retained at ingestion
   time — it cannot be reconstructed later.*

**APP 7.8 — the carve-out that does NOT help here.** OAIC **[7.8]**:

> "APP 7 does not apply to the extent that the *Do Not Call Register Act 2006*, the *Spam Act 2003* or any other
> legislation prescribed by the regulations apply."

Post is covered by neither of those Acts (§2, §3 below). **Therefore APP 7 is the live, unexcluded regime for
physical direct mail** — the opposite of the intuition that "the spam rules don't apply to post, so post is
unregulated".

`[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]` OAIC, *Direct marketing* (organisations guidance):
https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/direct-marketing

### 1.8 Other APPs that bite on this specific product

- **APP 1** — open and transparent management; a compliant, current privacy policy (APP 1.3–1.4). Clearview [188]
  confirms the policy is an APP 1 instrument, **not** an APP 5 substitute.
- **APP 6** — use/disclosure for the primary purpose or a related purpose within reasonable expectations.
- **APP 10 — quality.** An APP entity must take reasonable steps to ensure the personal information it **uses or
  discloses** is "accurate, up-to-date, complete and relevant". `[UNSOURCED — not retrieved verbatim this session;
  cited from the APP list structure.]` **This is directly engaged by mailing a value estimate.** A statistical
  estimate with a known error distribution is not self-evidently "accurate" in the APP 10 sense, and the internal
  documentation for this business's own valuation model records a ±12% band containing the actual sale price about
  61% of the time and a design envelope outside which the figure is unreliable. **NEEDS A LAWYER** on how APP 10
  applies to a modelled opinion of value as distinct from a factual attribute.
- **APP 11** — security; **APP 12/13** — access and correction. A homeowner who receives the mail piece may request
  access to, and correction of, the record you hold about their home.

## 2. Spam Act 2003 (Cth) — electronic only; it does not reach post

`[PRIMARY — LEGISLATION]` *Spam Act 2003* (Cth): https://www.legislation.gov.au/Details/C2016C00614 (compilation
page) — see s 5 (*What is an electronic message?*) and s 6 (*What is a commercial electronic message?*).
*Verbatim text not retrieved this session — see sourcing note.*

`[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]` ACMA, *Avoid sending spam* (last updated 29 November 2024):
https://www.acma.gov.au/avoid-sending-spam

> "If you send or have someone else send your marketing **emails or messages**, you need to know about spam laws.
> The *Spam Act 2003* and the Spam Regulations set out your responsibilities under Australian law."

ACMA's three rules, verbatim in substance: you must **get consent** (express or inferred) before sending; the
message must **identify you as the sender** with correct contact details ("Use the correct legal name of your
business, or your name and Australian Business Number (ABN)… must remain correct for at least 30 days"); and you
must **make it easy to unsubscribe** (honoured "within 5 working days", no fee, functional for at least 30 days, no
requirement to create an account or supply extra personal information).

ACMA also records two prohibitions worth noting because they touch data sourcing:

> "Under the Spam Act, you cannot: **use or supply a list that has been created with address-harvesting software**;
> use or supply address-harvesting software."

and, on bought lists:

> "Take care when you buy or use a marketing list. **You are still responsible for making sure you have consent for
> any addresses you use.**"

**Plain statement of scope.** The Act regulates **commercial *electronic* messages** — email, SMS, MMS, instant
messaging — sent to an *electronic address*. **A letter in a letterbox is not an electronic message, so the Spam
Act does not apply to physical post.** `[PRIMARY — REGULATOR: ACMA page frames the entire regime around "emails or
messages" and electronic addresses; no ACMA page located that states the postal exclusion in terms — the exclusion
follows from the statutory definitions in ss 5–6, which I have not read verbatim. Flagged for verification.]`

**What that does NOT excuse.** Four things survive the Spam Act's inapplicability:

1. **APP 7 still applies to post** — and, per APP 7.8, applies *precisely because* the Spam Act does not (§1.7).
2. The address-harvesting prohibition is about *electronic* addresses, but the **APP 3.5 "fair means"** test applies
   to *all* personal information however collected.
3. Any **email or SMS follow-up** to a postal recipient — a reply-by-email flow, an SMS QR-code landing, a
   "text us your address" mechanic — moves the communication squarely **into** the Spam Act, where consent is
   required *first* and cannot be sought by sending an electronic message: ACMA, "**You cannot send an electronic
   message to ask for consent, because this is a marketing message.**"
4. **Inferred consent will not cover a cold recipient.** ACMA: inferred consent "is usually when a person has a
   provable, ongoing relationship with your business… **It does not cover sending messages after someone has just
   bought something from your business.**"

> **s 105 What is advertising material**
> "(1) **Advertising material** means any of the following, if it includes any form of advertising for a commercial
> purpose— (a) a circular, a flyer, promotional matter, information or **a letter**; (b) a newspaper, magazine or
> other publication distributed without charge to intended readers."

> **s 106 What is unsolicited advertising material for premises**
> "(1) Advertising material is **unsolicited advertising material** for premises **if it is not addressed by name to
> an owner or occupier of the premises** or to a person who is otherwise lawfully at the premises from time to time.
> *Examples of unsolicited advertising material—* advertising material addressed only as 'To the householder' or
> 'To the occupier'"

> **s 107 Unlawful delivery provision**
> "(1) A person **must not deliver to premises any unsolicited advertising material** for the premises if—
> (a) on an exterior receptacle or other place for receiving mail for the premises, or on a boundary fence or gate
> adjoining the footpath or other public access point for the premises, there is a legible sign or marking; and
> (b) the sign or marking states **'No Advertising Material', 'No Junk Mail', 'Australia Post Mail Only'** or words
> to similar effect or otherwise contains any words in English indicating, expressly or by implication, that
> unsolicited advertising material for the premises is not to be delivered to the premises; and
> (c) the sign or marking is clearly visible to a person delivering the advertising material to the premises."

> **s 108 Secure delivery provision** — a person who delivers unsolicited advertising material "must ensure that the
> material is placed securely— (a) in a receptacle, slot or other place used for the delivery of mail to the
> premises; or (b) in a receptacle or slot that is used for the delivery of newspapers…; or (c) under a door".

> **s 112 Avoiding accumulations of waste**
> "(1) This section applies if a person (the **responsible entity**) authorises, or arranges for, the distribution of
> advertising material to premises in an area.
> (2) The responsible entity **must take reasonable steps to ensure that the advertising material does not… become
> waste**. **Maximum penalty—100 penalty units.**
> *Example of reasonable steps…* The advertising material is delivered in a way that is consistent with the
> **Distribution Standards Board's code of practice** for the delivery of advertising material.
> (3) If, in the delivery… a person contravenes the unlawful delivery provision or the secure delivery provision,
> the responsible entity must, **if directed by the chief executive**, collect the material from the premises…
> **Maximum penalty—100 penalty units.**"

> **s 111** — the chief executive may require the person who "**authorised or arranged for the distribution**" (or
> for the **printing**) of the material to name the deliverers, within 7 days. **Maximum penalty—40 penalty units.**

**The operative distinction is name-addressing.** Under **s 106(1)**, material addressed **by name to an owner or
occupier** is *not* "unsolicited advertising material", and s 107 therefore does not reach it. Material addressed
"To the Householder", "The Owner", or to the address alone **is** unsolicited advertising material, and delivering
it to a letterbox bearing a "No Junk Mail" sign contravenes s 107.

**This has a direct, uncomfortable design consequence for an address-first business model.** A business that
deliberately does **not** hold owner names — precisely to reduce privacy exposure — and therefore addresses mail to
the dwelling rather than to a person, lands **inside** the s 106 definition and **inside** the s 107 prohibition.
Conversely, obtaining and using the owner's **name** takes the mail out of s 107 but strengthens the case that the
business holds **personal information** and is doing **APP 7 direct marketing** (§1.3, §1.7). **These two regimes
push in opposite directions. NEEDS A LAWYER to resolve the trade-off; it cannot be optimised away.**

**Open point on s 107's penalty.** In the reprint current as at 16 June 2026, **s 107 states the prohibition but
carries no maximum penalty on its face**; ss 111 and 112 carry the stated penalties (40 and 100 penalty units) and
the general littering provision **s 103** carries 30–40 penalty units. Whether s 107 is enforced as an offence via
the infringement-notice regime, via s 112, or otherwise, **I could not determine and it NEEDS A LAWYER.** The dollar
value of a Queensland penalty unit (set under the *Penalties and Sentences Act 1992* (Qld) and indexed) was **not
verified this session — `[UNSOURCED]`.**

## 6. Queensland real estate law

### 6.1 Does providing a "property market appraisal" require a real estate agent licence?

`[PRIMARY — LEGISLATION]` *Property Occupations Act 2014* (Qld), authorised reprint **current as at 1 August 2025**:
https://www.legislation.qld.gov.au/view/pdf/inforce/current/act-2014-022

The licence is defined by reference to **activities performed as an agent for others for reward**:

> **s 26 What a real estate agent licence authorises**
> "(1) A real estate agent licence authorises the holder of the licence to perform the following activities **as an
> agent for others for reward**— (a) to buy, sell (other than by auction), exchange or let real property or
> interests in real property; (b) to buy, sell (other than by auction), exchange, or let businesses or interests in
> businesses; (c) **to negotiate for** the buying, selling, exchanging, or letting of something mentioned in
> paragraph (a) or (b); (d) to collect rents."

And the offence:

> **s 97 Acting as property agent**
> "(1) A person must not, **as an agent for someone else for reward**, perform an activity that may be done under the
> authority of a property agent licence unless the person— (a) holds a property agent licence…; or (b) is otherwise
> permitted… **Maximum penalty—200 penalty units or 2 years imprisonment.**
> (2) A person must not act as a property agent unless— (a) the person holds a property agent licence…; or (b) the
> act is otherwise permitted… **Maximum penalty—200 penalty units or 2 years imprisonment.**
> (3) **Without limiting the ways a person may act as a property agent, a person acts as a property agent if the
> person**— (a) performs an activity mentioned in section 25(1) or (2) or 26(1); or (b) **advertises, notifies or
> states** that the person— (i) performs an activity mentioned in section 25(1) or (2) or 26(1); or (ii) **is willing
> to perform** an activity mentioned in section 25(1) or (2) or 26(1); or (c) **in any way holds out as being ready
> to perform** an activity mentioned in section 25(1) or (2) or 26(1)."

**Observation (description, not conclusion).** *Giving an opinion of value* is **not** one of the activities listed
in s 26(1). On the face of the text, the licensed activities are buying, selling, exchanging, letting, negotiating
and collecting rents **as an agent for others for reward**. A pure data/appraisal product, supplied to the
homeowner as principal rather than as their agent, does not obviously perform an s 26(1) activity.

**But s 97(3)(b)–(c) is broad and is where a mail-out is exposed.** "Advertises, notifies or states that the person
… **is willing to perform**" or "**in any way holds out as being ready to perform**" an s 26(1) activity is enough.
A mail piece that offers to sell the home, to introduce buyers, to negotiate, to "get you the best price", or that
invites the owner to list, moves from data supply toward holding out. **NEEDS A LAWYER** on where the line sits for
copy that stops short of an explicit offer to act — this is the single most important drafting constraint on the
mail piece.

`[PRIMARY — INDUSTRY BODY]` REIQ, *The implications of not holding an appropriate licence*,
https://www.reiq.com/articles/agency-practice/the-implications-of-not-holding-an-appropriate-licence — notes that an
unlicensed person "is not entitled to sue for, recover or keep a reward or expense for the performance of an

### 6.2 ⚠ s 222 "Prohibited practices" — supplying particulars of properties for sale, for reward

**This provision was not in the brief and is arguably the most surprising find in this pass.**

`[PRIMARY — LEGISLATION]` *Property Occupations Act 2014* (Qld), s 222 (reprint current as at 1 August 2025):

> **222 Prohibited practices**
> "(1) A person must not, **for reward**, **supply or undertake to supply, advertise, or hold out, that the person
> will supply to another person addresses or other particulars of**— (a) places of residence that are to let; or
> (b) **places of residence or land or interests in places of residence or land that are for sale**.
> **Maximum penalty—200 penalty units or 1 year's imprisonment.**
> (2) Subsection (1) does not apply to a property agent that has been appointed by the landlord or seller… and has
> the landlord's or seller's consent to supply the particulars."

**Why this matters to this business.** The prohibition is on **supplying, for reward, addresses or particulars of
residences that are for sale**, and the only exception in s 222(2) is for **the appointed agent with the
seller's consent**. A property-intelligence platform that publishes for-sale listings, address-level particulars and
comparables, and that monetises in any way (paid reports, lead sales, advertising, subscriptions), is on the face of
the text within the conduct s 222(1) describes — and cannot use the s 222(2) exception because it is not the
appointed agent.

**NEEDS A LAWYER — urgently and specifically.** Open questions: what "for reward" requires (direct payment for the
particulars, or any commercial benefit including advertising or lead revenue?); whether the provision is confined in
practice to the historical "accommodation/listing bureau" mischief it was drafted for; whether publication to the
world at large is "supply to another person"; and whether the **sold-property and off-market** content (properties
*not* for sale) sits outside it while the **for-sale** content sits inside it. **This exposure attaches to the
existing website, not merely to the proposed mail-out.**

### 6.3 s 215 — comparative market analysis (applies to agents, and sets the benchmark)

`[PRIMARY — LEGISLATION]` POA 2014 s 215:

> **215 Representation of price of property—real estate agent**
> "(1) This section applies if a person wanting to sell residential property asks **a real estate agent** for
> information about the price at which residential property that is to be, or may be, offered for sale… is likely
> to be sold.
> (2) If the real estate agent decides to give the person the information, the real estate agent must, when giving
> the person the information, give the person— (a) **a copy of a comparative market analysis** for the offered
> property; or (b) if a comparative market analysis can not be prepared…, **a written explanation showing how the
> real estate agent decided the market value** of the property. **Maximum penalty—540 penalty units.**"

> **Schedule 2 (Dictionary)** — "**comparative market analysis**, for an offered property, means a document comparing
> the offered property with **at least 3 properties sold within the previous 6 months** that are of a **similar
> standard or condition** to the offered property and are **within 5km** of the offered property."

**s 215 binds "a real estate agent", so an unlicensed data business is not directly caught by it.** Its significance
is as a **benchmark**: Queensland's Parliament has legislated what a defensible price representation to a
prospective seller looks like — ≥3 comparables, sold within 6 months, similar standard/condition, within 5 km. A
value estimate posted to a homeowner that is materially weaker than that standard (older comparables, dissimilar
properties, greater distance, or fewer than three) is harder to defend as having "reasonable grounds" under the ACL
(§7). Queensland Revenue Office applies a comparable benchmark for accepting market appraisals: "at least 3 recent
comparable sales in the last 12 months" — https://qro.qld.gov.au/definition/market-appraisal/ `[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]`

### 6.4 s 22 — "unsolicited invitation" and property information sessions

`[PRIMARY — LEGISLATION]` POA 2014 s 22 and s 222(3):

> **s 22 Meaning of unsolicited invitation**
> "(1) An **unsolicited invitation** to a person to attend a property information session includes an invitation—
> (a) **addressed personally to the person and sent to the person's residential, business, postal or email
> address**; or (b) made by telephoning the person, or by another personal approach to the person.
> (2) An invitation… is **not** an unsolicited invitation if it is— (a) made at the person's request…; or (b) made
> to the public generally or a section of the public by media advertisement; or (c) **addressed non-specifically**
> and sent to the person's residential, business, postal or email address. *Examples of invitations addressed
> non-specifically—* • 'The owner' • 'The householder'"

> **s 222(3)** "A person (the first person) must not make an unsolicited invitation to another person to attend a
> **property information session** unless the first person is a property developer, a property agent or someone
> acting for the developer or agent. **Maximum penalty—200 penalty units or 2 years imprisonment.**"

**Directly relevant in two ways.** First, if any mail-out invites homeowners to a seminar, webinar, briefing or
"information session" about property, s 222(3) is engaged and the sender must be a property developer, property
agent, or acting for one. Second — and note the **exact inversion of the WRRA position in §5.1** — POA s 22 treats
**personally addressed** mail as the *unsolicited* form and "The owner"/"The householder" as *non-specific* and
therefore **not** unsolicited, whereas WRRA s 106 treats **name-addressed** mail as *not* unsolicited advertising
material and "To the householder" as *unsolicited*. The two Queensland Acts use the same word to mean opposite
things. **Any addressing decision must be tested against both.** NEEDS A LAWYER.

### 6.5 "Market appraisal" vs "valuation" — and the Valuers Registration Act

**⚠ CORRECTION TO A PREMISE IN THE BRIEF: Queensland has NOT repealed the Valuers Registration Act. It is in
force.**

`[PRIMARY — LEGISLATION]` *Valuers Registration Act 1992* (Qld), authorised reprint **current as at 1 March 2023**,
in force: https://www.legislation.qld.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/act-1992-002

> **s 3 (Dictionary)** — "**valuer** means a person who, **in any capacity, holds himself or herself out as ready to
> make a valuation of land**."

> **s 63 Practice of unregistered valuer prohibited**
> "(1) Subject to subsections (2) and (3), a person who is not a registered valuer must not— (a) **hold himself or
> herself out as being a registered valuer**; or (b) **carry on or attempt to carry on the business of a registered
> valuer**; or (c) take, use or exhibit a name, letter, word, title, description or symbol that, either alone or in
> the circumstances in which it is taken— (i) is capable of being reasonably understood to indicate; or (ii) is
> intended by the person to indicate; that the person is a registered valuer or is entitled to carry on the business
> of a registered valuer. **Maximum penalty—100 penalty units.**
> (2) **If the person is a body corporate, an offence against subsection (1) is not committed if at least 1 director
> or member of the board of management of the body corporate or an employee of the body corporate is a registered
> valuer.**
> (3) It is not an offence against subsection (1) for a person who is an architect, engineer or quantity surveyor to
> make cost assessments or cost estimates in relation to a building, structure or improvement in the course of the
> person's professional duties."

The regulator is the **Valuers Registration Board of Queensland** — https://vrbq.qld.gov.au/

`[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]` VRBQ, *Difference between a Valuation and an Appraisal*,
https://vrbq.qld.gov.au/valuation-v-appraisal/ :

> "A **valuation** provides an unbiased and independent assessment of value that can be relied on for **financial
> decisions, legal or statutory requirements**."

> "An **appraisal**… provides a broad estimate of the potential sale price or rent applicable to a property" and
> "**cannot be relied upon as a basis for a financial decision, legal or statutory requirement**."

> "It is standard practice for a real estate agent to provide an estimate of the sale price or rent applicable to a
> property… **This is not a property valuation.**"

> "**Where a valuation is undertaken for a financial decision, a legal or statutory requirement by any person,
> company, or enterprise not registered as a valuer this will be in contravention of the VRA.**"

**There is a real tension here that must be flagged rather than smoothed over.** The **statutory** offence in s 63
is framed around holding out as a **registered** valuer, carrying on the **business of a registered valuer**, or
using titles indicating registration — it does not, on its face, prohibit an unregistered person from giving an
estimate of value. The **regulator's published position** is broader: it asserts that performing a *valuation* for a
financial/legal/statutory purpose while unregistered contravenes the Act. Note also the s 3 definition — "valuer"
means anyone who "in any capacity, holds himself or herself out as ready to make a **valuation of land**" — which
does not carry the word "registered". **NEEDS A LAWYER** on: (i) whether the s 3 definition feeds an operative
prohibition beyond s 63; (ii) whether an automated, model-generated figure sent to a homeowner is an "appraisal"
(permissible framing) or a "valuation" (regulated); and (iii) whether it changes character if the recipient could
foreseeably use it for a financial decision (refinance, offer negotiation), which is precisely the VRBQ dividing
line. **The vocabulary used in the mail piece — "valuation", "valued at", "we value your home at" — is itself a
legal exposure, independent of the number's accuracy.** The s 63(2) body-corporate carve-out (one registered valuer
as director or employee) is worth noting as a structural option to raise with a lawyer.

(Certified Practising Valuer); registration is a **state** matter and Queensland's regime is the VRA 1992 above.

## 7. Australian Consumer Law — misleading conduct and representations about value

`[PRIMARY — LEGISLATION]` *Competition and Consumer Act 2010* (Cth) **Schedule 2 — the Australian Consumer Law**:
https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A00109/latest/text (confirmed as the official location by Treasury:
https://consumerlaw.gov.au/australian-consumer-law/legislation). *Verbatim text of ss 18, 29, 39–43 not retrieved —
see sourcing note. Section numbers below are cited from the brief and regulator guidance, not read verbatim.*

- **s 18** — a person must not, in trade or commerce, engage in conduct that is misleading or deceptive or is likely
  to mislead or deceive.
- **s 29(1)(g)** — false or misleading representations that goods/services have performance characteristics, uses or
  benefits they do not have; **s 29(1)(i)** — false or misleading representations "with respect to the price of
  goods or services".
- **s 4** — representations as to **future matters** are taken to be misleading unless the maker had **reasonable
  grounds** for making them.

`[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]` ACCC, *False or misleading claims*,
https://www.accc.gov.au/business/advertising-and-promotions/false-or-misleading-claims :

> "**It makes no difference whether a business intends to mislead or not.**"

> "Any information or claim that a business provides about its products or services must be **accurate, truthful and
> based on reasonable grounds**."

> "**Businesses should take extra care not to make misleading statements about price.**"

> "A business that makes a claim about future matters must have **reasonable grounds for making the claim at the
> time**."

**Application to a posted value estimate (risk description, not a conclusion).** Several features of this specific
product raise s 18 / s 29 exposure and should be examined by a lawyer:

1. **The estimate is a representation about price/value made in trade or commerce.** Intent is irrelevant. The
   question is what an ordinary reasonable member of the target class would take from it.
2. **"Reasonable grounds" is the operative test, and it is testable against the business's own measured accuracy.**
   Where internal documentation records that the published range is a flat ±12% that contains the actual sale price
   about **61%** of the time, that the confidence tiers are **not monotonic** across bands, and that the method has a
   **design envelope** outside which it cannot produce a correct answer, then labelling the output a "confidence
   range" or "90% range" — or presenting a figure for a property outside the envelope without disclosure — is a
   candidate misleading representation *even though every underlying number is honestly computed*. The misleading
   element would be the **implied precision and the implied reliability**, not the arithmetic.
3. **Silence and framing can mislead.** Omitting the sample limitations, the data-capture rate of the underlying
   sold-sales source, or the fact that the figure is a model output rather than an inspection-based assessment, are
   candidate misleading omissions where the impression conveyed is of an authoritative valuation.
4. **A "comparable range" framing is materially safer than a single figure** — but only if the range is honestly
   characterised (empirical spread, not a statistical confidence interval) and the comparables genuinely support it.
5. **Any claim of comparative accuracy** ("more accurate than an agent's appraisal", "more accurate than a portal
   estimate") is a s 29 representation requiring substantiation held **at the time of making it**.

`[UNSOURCED]` **Underquoting enforcement.** The brief asked for real-estate enforcement precedent. I did **not**

**Note the Queensland overlay.** POA 2014 **s 216** restricts a *real estate agent* from disclosing a reserve price

## 8. Unsolicited supply — ACL Part 3-1 Div 2 (ss 39–43)

`[PRIMARY — REGULATOR]` ACCC, *Supplying unrequested products or services*,
https://www.accc.gov.au/business/selling-products-and-services/supplying-unrequested-products-or-services :

> "**Consumers and businesses do not need to pay for products, services or advertisements that they didn't ask
> for.**"

> Unsolicited supply occurs "when a consumer or business receives products or services they didn't request, or a
> business claims to have supplied services that a consumer or business didn't receive or request."

Rules recorded by the ACCC:

- no payment obligation exists for unrequested items;
- **if the business has contacted the consumer, it has one month to retrieve the items; if it has not, three
  months** — after which the consumer may keep them without paying;
- **any document that looks like a bill must display prominently: "This is not a bill. You are not required to pay
  money"** unless the business reasonably believes it has a right to payment.

**Application (risk description, not a conclusion).** The core prohibition in this Division is on **asserting a
right to payment** for unsolicited goods or services (ACL s 40; the "not a bill" wording sits in s 40's document
requirements). **Posting a genuinely free report, with no invoice, no price, no "value $X", and no assertion that
anything is owed, is not the mischief the Division targets.** The exposures to raise with a lawyer are narrower and
specific:

1. **Any "normally $495 — yours free" framing on the mail piece.** Stating a notional price for something the
   recipient did not request moves the document toward looking like an assertion of value/entitlement. The ACCC's
   "This is not a bill" requirement exists precisely for documents that read as bills.
2. **Follow-up invoicing.** If a free report is followed by any charge for a "full report", "update", or
   "continuation" that the recipient did not affirmatively request, the Division is directly engaged.
3. **Unsolicited services.** The Division covers *services* as well as goods — a "we've prepared an appraisal for
   you" framing supplies a service the recipient did not request.

**⚠ A separate and probably larger ACL issue the brief did not raise: unsolicited consumer agreements (ACL Part 3-2
Div 2, ss 69–95).** These rules apply to agreements negotiated **at a place other than the supplier's business
premises** — including by telephone — and impose disclosure requirements, permitted calling hours, a **10 business
day cooling-off period**, and a prohibition on supplying or accepting payment during the cooling-off period.

| A16 | **ADMA's Code and Do Not Mail register bind members only**, do not cover unaddressed material, and take ≥45 days to process a registration. | ADMA `[PRIMARY — INDUSTRY BODY]` |

## B. The two structural traps

**B1 — The addressing paradox.** Queensland's two relevant Acts use "unsolicited" in **opposite** senses:
- **WRRA s 106**: name-addressed = **NOT** unsolicited advertising material → s 107 sticker prohibition does **not**
  apply. "The owner"/"The householder" = **IS** unsolicited → sticker prohibition **applies**.
- **POA s 22**: name-addressed = **IS** an unsolicited invitation → s 222(3) restriction applies.
  "The owner"/"The householder" = **NOT** unsolicited.

Simultaneously, using owner names strengthens the case that you hold **personal information** and are conducting
**APP 7 direct marketing**, while avoiding names pushes you into WRRA s 107. **There is no addressing choice that
minimises all three at once.** This must be decided with a lawyer, not by preference.

**B2 — The exemption you are relying on may not exist.** The whole APP analysis turns on whether the small business
exemption survives. It is defeated by **trading in personal information**, which OAIC defines to include
**providing a benefit, service or advantage in order to collect personal information about another individual from
someone else** — i.e. potentially **paying for property/owner data**. And separately, the exemption is a stated
target of Commonwealth reform with an unresolved timetable.

# 7. What this means for a mailer carrying a photo of the recipient's house

This section is synthesis. Every underlying claim is cited in sections 1-6.

## 7.1 The three things the evidence says will work

**1. The recipient's own data is the engine — not the market commentary around it.**
Gerber/Green/Larimer: a generic mailer moved turnout **+0.5 pp**; the same mailer showing the recipient's **own record** moved it **+4.9 pp**. Allcott & Kessler: **49% of Opower recipients would like the report LESS if the comparison to their own neighbours were removed**, against 11% who would like it more, and **73%** said it provided useful information. Fields' asset is the address-specific comparable set. Generic suburb commentary is the thing to cut, not the thing to lead with.

**2. The effect lives in identifying the recipient as an outlier — not in geographic tightness.**
This is the correction I'd most want carried forward, because the popular version of this finding is wrong. The peer-reviewed HMRC trials (Hallsworth et al. 2017, >200,000 people) found the **"minority norm"** — telling the recipient they were in the small minority who had *not* acted — worth **+5.1 pp**, versus **+1.3 pp** for a generic descriptive norm, while narrowing the norm from "people" to "people in the UK" was **not significant (p=0.26)**. The widely-quoted "national 72.5% → home town 83%" ladder comes from a government press summary, not the paper.

The same shape appears everywhere in this literature: Opower's top usage decile **6.3%** vs bottom decile ~**0%**; Ferraro & Price's water comparison ~**94%** larger relative effect among high users; Gerber/Green's "Neighbors" effect collapsing to **+1 pp** in a high-turnout population but holding at **+3 pp** among the disengaged. **Comparative data moves the people it identifies as outliers and barely moves anyone else.**

For Fields this cuts both ways, and the second half is the important half: the homes where the mailer will work are the ones where the data says something the owner does not already believe. Those are also the homes most likely to generate a complaint. Suburb-level granularity is still Fields' asset — but because it *locates outliers precisely*, not because "local" is inherently persuasive.

**3. A picture of the thing you own is a legitimate, evidence-adjacent device — with caveats.**
The DVLA vehicle-photo trial (payment **40% → 49%**) is the only known large-scale government test of putting an image of the recipient's own possession on a letter. **But**: I could not verify that figure against the primary Cabinet Office document (now offline), the peer-reviewed source confirming the trial's existence reports no rates, and the photo was bundled with rewritten loss-framed copy — so its independent contribution is unknown. Treat the house photo as *plausible and worth randomising*, not as a proven multiplier.

There is a second, more important reason to be careful with the photo, below.

## 7.2 Print retrieved facts. Never print inferences.

This is the sharpest line the research draws, and it is the one most likely to be crossed by an AI-generated mailer.

**Kim, Barasz & John (JCR 2019)** measured acceptability across 30 real targeting practices and found two dimensions explain it: **where the data came from** and **how it was obtained**. Information *stated by the consumer* scored **4.08/7**; information *inferred by the firm* scored **3.11** — below the scale midpoint, i.e. net-unacceptable. And in their Study 3 (N=348), **disclosing an inferred attribute was worse than saying nothing at all**: ad effectiveness **2.52 (inferred) vs 2.96 (no disclosure) vs 3.10 (stated)**. Their framing: "making an overt inference about someone can be **taboo**."

Applied to a Fields mailer:

| Safe — a retrievable public fact | Taboo — an inference about the person |
|---|---|
| "Sold March 2019 for $1,240,000 (Queensland titles data)" | "You've owned this home 7 years — most people move around now" |
| "Three comparable homes on your street sold between $1.42M and $1.61M this year" | "Your home looks like it hasn't been renovated" |
| "Your block is 612 m²; the suburb median is 590 m²" | "You may be thinking about selling" |
| "Comparable evidence puts homes like yours in the range $X–$Y" | "You're probably sitting on more equity than you realise" |

The right-hand column is exactly what a generative editorial pipeline produces if left unconstrained, and every item in it is a statement about the *person*, not the *property*. **The mailer should be able to survive the question "where did each sentence on this page come from?" with a public-record answer for every sentence.**

**And never presume valence.** Eric Meyer's Facebook "Year in Review" case is the cleanest illustration: the algorithm *correctly* identified the most significant event of his year — his daughter's death — and wrapped it in party graphics. A correct retrieval plus an assumed emotional frame equals cruelty. A Fields template must not auto-celebrate a falling value, a long-vacant neighbouring property, or a distressed comparable sale next door.

**Audit every merge field before every drop.** The single verified catastrophe in this whole literature was not a targeting strategy — it was plumbing. OfficeMax mailed Mike Seay an envelope addressed:

> Mike Seay
> **Daughter Killed in Car Crash**
> Or Current Business

His daughter had died in a car crash the previous year. Nobody at OfficeMax targeted grief; an internal free-text sensitivity note in a broker's file rendered into the address block. **That is precisely the failure mode a templated, database-driven mailer is exposed to**, and Fields' database contains exactly the kind of free-text and internal-flag fields that can escape into a rendered document — enrichment notes, data-quality flags, editorial drafts, `confidence_reason` strings, `directional_only` markers.

Concrete requirement: every field that can reach the page must be **allow-listed, typed, and length-bounded**, with a hard build-time failure on any unrecognised or free-text field reaching a template. Proof-read a physical sample of the actual print run — not a preview of a template — before every drop.

## 7.3 Name the source, in plain words, on the page

The cheapest fix in the entire literature, with two independent measurements:

- **Aguirre et al. (Journal of Retailing 2015), Study 3 (n=121):** a small disclosure notice stating how the data was collected and offering opt-out **erased the entire covert-collection penalty**. Without it, covert scored **2.96** vs overt **4.00** (p=.021); with it, **3.77 vs 3.43** — no significant difference. And in Study 1, personalisation only worked at all when collection was **overt** (2.67 → 4.38, p=.001); under **covert** collection more personalisation did **nothing** (2.94 → 3.16, p=.641).
- **Kim et al. (2019), Study 5b (field, n=1,862):** changing a sidebar title to "Recommended based on **what you've shared with us**" lifted click propensity **.08 → .12 — a 50% relative lift** — with no other change.

**But this is strictly conditional on the source being defensible.** Disclosure is a *multiplier on provenance*, in whichever direction the provenance points: naming an acceptable source is free-to-positive; naming an unacceptable one is **worse than saying nothing**. Fields' advantage here is real — comparable sales and titles data are public record, and that is a sentence a homeowner can accept. Fields' *exposure* is the address list: if the provenance of the mailing list cannot be stated in one plain line, disclosure will hurt rather than help (§6, and the PropTrack licence constraint in §3).

Note this is also a **statutory obligation, not just good practice**: APP 7.6 requires an entity to tell an individual where it got their information on request (§6).

**Trust is a gate, not a dial.** Kim et al. Study 4 found the benefit of transparency exists only where platform trust exceeds **3.56 on a 7-point scale**; below that, "even acceptable [transparency] is unlikely to overcome low trust." An unknown sender cannot disclose its way to acceptability. Fields is an unknown sender to most recipients — so the artefact itself has to establish credibility (method shown, sources named, accuracy stated) in the same piece of paper that makes the claim.

## 7.4 Control beats camouflage

Target's documented response to the pregnancy-targeting problem was to **hide** the targeting — "we started mixing in all these ads for things we knew pregnant women would never buy, so the baby ads looked random… as long as a pregnant woman thinks she hasn't been spied on, she'll use the coupons."

**Tucker (JMR 2014)** is the same problem solved the opposite way, with better results: a randomised field study across **1.2 million users** spanning Facebook's 2010 privacy-control overhaul found that after users were given more control — **with no change whatsoever to how ads were targeted** — "users were **twice as likely to click on personalized ads**." The gain was largest for ads using *less commonly available* private information, i.e. control helps most exactly where the personalisation is most exposing.

Camouflage works only until someone notices. Agency survives being noticed. For Fields that means a prominent, genuinely easy opt-out and an obvious correction route — not a mailer engineered to look less targeted than it is.

## 7.5 The photo is the single highest-variance element on the page

A photo of the recipient's house is simultaneously the strongest attention device available and the clearest possible signal of *"we have been looking at your home specifically."* It converts an abstract database into something visibly, personally surveillant. Section 5's research is unanimous that the trigger is not the *possession* of data but the **visible demonstration of inference and collection** — and a photo is the most vivid possible demonstration.

**And personalisation is an inverted U, not a ladder.** This is the finding that should make Fields genuinely cautious about maximal personalisation. Aguirre et al.'s Facebook field test found:

| Personalisation level | Financial services CTR | Dog food CTR |
|---|---|---|
| None | 0.017% | 0.047% |
| **Moderate** (targeted on stated interests) | **0.077%** | **0.064%** |
| **High** (ad carried the person's own demographics) | **0.032%** | **0.033%** |

High personalisation **cut click-through 58% and 48%** versus moderate — and in the dog food case landed **below the no-personalisation baseline**. More personal is not more effective past a point; it inverts.

A photograph of the recipient's own house is unambiguously at the "high" end of that scale. It is exactly the move that tipped those campaigns over the peak. That does not mean don't do it — it means **do not assume it helps, and do not stack it with every other maximal-personalisation element at once**. A mailer carrying the house photo *and* the owner's name *and* a street ranking *and* an inferred motive is four steps past the peak.

Practical consequences:

- **Source it from something the homeowner already knows is public.** A photo the owner recognises as their own past listing photo, or a street-level image they know is publicly mapped, reads very differently from a photo they cannot account for. If Fields cannot name the source in one line on the page, do not use the image.
- **Never use an image that implies presence.** Anything that could read as "someone drove to my house and photographed it" — a recent, unmatched, close-range or angled shot — is a different message from a listing photo or a standard street-level image, regardless of how it was actually obtained.
- **Never show the interior.** Interior photos from a past listing are public but reveal what is *inside* the home. That crosses from "data about the asset" to "data about your life."
- **Randomise it.** Bertrand et al. is unambiguous that you cannot predict ex ante which creative element carries the effect, and that the ones that matter have large effects. Run photo / no-photo as an arm from mailing one.

## 7.6 Do not name the neighbours

This is the clearest single design instruction in the whole report, and it comes from two independent literatures agreeing.

- **Gerber/Green/Larimer:** the "Neighbors" mailer (naming neighbours and their records) got **+8.1 pp** — and "**provoked outrage among some recipients**," prompting a documented search for versions that kept the effect without the agitation. The "Self" mailer — **own data only** — retained **4.9 of 8.1 pp (~60%)** with "only a modest level of resistance."
- **Opower:** the same structure at scale, but *de-identified* — 100 nearest comparable homes, aggregated, never named. That is why it survived 6 million households without becoming a scandal.

Fields sits in an unusual position here: **comparable sales are already published as address + price**, so a comps table is not "naming a neighbour's private data" in the way a voting record is. But the *framing* choice is still live, and the rule follows: present comparables as **evidence about the recipient's home**, never as a **ranking against identified households**. "Three homes on your street sold between $1.42M and $1.61M this year" is evidence. "You are the 7th most valuable house on your street" is a ranking, and rankings are what generate complaints.

## 7.7 Structure the number the way the regulators have already told you to

Sections 3 and 6 converge on the same finding from two continents:

- **FTC v Opendoor ¶28:** disclaimers "were not conspicuous and were in fine print… they did not cure" the headline, and one stood "**in direct contravention to Opendoor's marketing.**"
- **ASA v Purplebricks (2018):** qualifying text was "**not sufficiently prominent to override the overall impression.**"
- **ACCC v Gary Peer (2005):** the agency "did not have **reasonable grounds** to make the representations." Under the ACL, a representation about value is deemed misleading unless the maker held reasonable grounds — and the burden sits on the maker, in advance.

And critically: **in every enforcement action found, the violation attached to a comparison or savings claim, not to the estimate itself.** Opendoor was not punished for a bad AVM; it was punished for a net-proceeds chart. Purplebricks was not punished for claiming savings existed; it was punished because the evidence chain for the *specific number* was third-hand and stale.

Applied to Fields:

- **Lead with a range, not a point.** This is already Fields' documented policy (CLAUDE.md: no single valuation in headlines; the valuation memory notes the single figure is deprecated in favour of a comparables range). The research supports it independently.
- **Do not print a figure outside the design envelope.** Fields' own measured constraint — detached houses $1M–$2M, `directional_only` outside it — is not a technicality. Printing a ceiling-pinned number on paper, posted to the owner of a $3M house, is exactly the "no reasonable grounds" fact pattern.
- **Never print a gap/savings claim you cannot reconstruct from your own records.** "$98K hiding in plain sight" is the Purplebricks and Opendoor failure mode in one line. If it goes on paper it needs a written, dated, reproducible basis held before the mailing.
- **Do not net off costs you have not evidenced.** Opendoor's independent violation was overstating what the *alternative* costs (9% agent fees, 1% staging) without data.
- **The disclaimer must not contradict the headline.** If the fine print says "this is not a valuation" while the headline behaves like one, the fine print is evidence against you. Two regulators said so explicitly.
- **Ship a confidence indicator.** Both major Australian AVMs (PropTrack, CoreLogic IntelliVal) publish a confidence score with the estimate as standard. A bare figure is below the local market norm — and Andersen v Zillow suggests published error characteristics are part of what makes an estimate legally defensible as opinion.
- **Use the off-market accuracy number, not the on-market one.** Zillow: **1.83% on-market vs 7.01% off-market**. Every home Fields mails is off-market by definition. Fields' own equivalent (envelope-scoped MAE ~10.5%, median 8.2%) is the honest figure and should govern how wide the range is.

## 7.7b The Queensland legal constraints that change the artefact itself

Full detail and caveats in §6 — **that section is research, not legal advice**. But five findings there change the design of the mailer, not just the paperwork around it.

**1. Queensland has legislated what a defensible comparable set looks like. Meet it or exceed it.**
POA 2014 s 215 + Schedule 2 define a "comparative market analysis" as comparing the property with **at least 3 properties sold within the previous 6 months, of similar standard or condition, within 5 km**. That provision binds *licensed agents*, not Fields — but it is the benchmark the state has set for a price representation to a prospective seller, and a weaker comparable set is correspondingly harder to defend as having "reasonable grounds" under the ACL. Queensland Revenue Office applies a similar test (≥3 comparable sales within 12 months). **Fields should treat "≥3 comps, ≤6 months, similar condition, ≤5 km" as a hard gate: if a property cannot meet it, it does not get mailed.**

**2. The word "valuation" is itself an exposure, independent of the number.**
The brief I was given assumed Queensland had repealed valuer registration. **It has not** — the *Valuers Registration Act 1992* (Qld) is in force, s 63 carries a maximum of 100 penalty units, and the Valuers Registration Board of Queensland's published position is that performing a *valuation* for a financial, legal or statutory purpose while unregistered contravenes the Act. VRBQ draws the line at reliance: an **appraisal** "cannot be relied upon as a basis for a financial decision, legal or statutory requirement"; a **valuation** can. A homeowner could foreseeably use a mailed figure to negotiate or refinance — which is exactly the dividing line. **Vocabulary discipline is a legal control, not a style preference: "appraisal", "estimate", "comparable evidence suggests" — never "valuation", "valued at", "we value your home at".** (Note s 63(2): a body corporate does not offend if one director or employee is a registered valuer — a structural option worth raising with a lawyer.)

**3. You can breach the licensing offence with copy alone.**
POA 2014 s 97(3)(b)–(c): a person acts as a property agent if they merely "**advertise, notify or state**" that they are "**willing to perform**", or "**in any way hold out as being ready to perform**", an activity such as selling, letting or **negotiating**. Maximum 200 penalty units or 2 years. Giving an opinion of value is *not* itself a listed activity — but a mail piece that offers to introduce buyers, to negotiate, to "get you the best price", or that invites the owner to list, drifts from data supply into holding out. **This is the single most important drafting constraint on the copy.**

**4. There is no addressing choice that is safe on every axis.**
Two Queensland Acts use "unsolicited" in **opposite** senses:
- *Waste Reduction and Recycling Act 2011* s 106: **name-addressed = NOT** unsolicited advertising material, so the s 107 "No Junk Mail" sticker prohibition does not apply. "To the householder"/"The owner" **is** unsolicited, and delivering it to a stickered letterbox is prohibited.
- *Property Occupations Act 2014* s 22: **name-addressed = IS** an unsolicited invitation (engaging s 222(3) if it invites the recipient to a property information session). "The owner"/"The householder" is **not**.

And separately, using owner names strengthens the case that Fields holds **personal information** and is conducting **APP 7 direct marketing**. Address-only avoids that but lands inside WRRA s 107. **This trade-off cannot be optimised away and must be decided with a lawyer.** Note the practical consequence for §7.5: a "we don't hold names, only addresses" design — which looks privacy-protective — is the one that breaches the sticker rule.

**5. Provenance must be captured at ingestion; it cannot be retrofitted.**
If APP 7 applies, APP 7.6 gives the individual a right to be told **where you got their information**, free, within a reasonable period. That is only answerable if **per-record source provenance is stored at the moment of collection**. This is an engineering requirement with a deadline that precedes the first mailing, and it happens to be the same capability §7.3 says makes the mailer work better. **Build it once, satisfy both.**

**And the exposure nobody asked about.** The legal research surfaced **POA 2014 s 222(1)** — a prohibition on supplying, **for reward**, "addresses or other particulars of… places of residence or land… **that are for sale**", max 200 penalty units or 1 year, with an exception only for the appointed agent with the seller's consent. On the face of the text this may reach Fields' **existing for-sale pages**, not just the mail-out. It is flagged in §6 as needing a lawyer urgently. I am not in a position to assess how it is applied in practice, and it may well be confined to the historical listing-bureau mischief — but it should not sit undiscovered.

## 7.8 Fund a cadence or don't start

Allcott & Rogers is unambiguous and it is the finding most likely to be ignored:

- A one-shot mailer's effect **decays to nothing within ~3 months** (quarterly group lost the whole effect between day 10 and day 80).
- A **two-year programme is 2.5–4.2× more cost effective than a one-shot intervention.**
- Effects **peak ~10 days after arrival** — measure at 10-21 days, not 48 hours.
- The novelty sawtooth flattens after **4 reports**, but there is **no habituation even at year 5** (continued groups ran 50-60% stronger than discontinued ones in years 3-5).

So: front-load frequency, then taper. And do not conclude anything from a single test drop — a one-shot pilot is the design most likely to produce a false negative and kill a programme that would have worked.

## 7.9 Instrument for annoyance, because opt-out will not show it

The most important operational warning in this report:

> **34% of Opower recipients had weakly negative willingness to pay — they would rather not receive it even for free. Only 0.08% opted out.**

If Fields tracks opt-outs as its harm metric, it will read ~0% and conclude the mail is welcome. It will be measuring inertia. Concretely:

- Put a **genuinely easy** opt-out on every piece (this is also an APP 7 obligation if the Privacy Act applies — see §6) and treat a *rising* opt-out rate as a serious signal, not a nuisance.
- Add a channel that captures irritation *without* requiring an opt-out — a QR/short-URL feedback route, and log inbound complaints by phone/email as a first-class metric with the same seriousness as leads.
- Expect the distribution to be bimodal: Opower's top quintile would have paid **$9+** while a third would have paid to make it stop. Fields will get enthusiastic inbound enquiries and angry calls **from the same campaign**. Neither one alone tells you whether it is working.
- Remember Costa & Kahn: the people who reject your frame don't just ignore you — they **move against you (+1%)** and are the ones most likely to opt out and to tell others they disliked it.

## 7.10 Targeting beats creative

- Allcott 2011: top decile **6.3%** effect vs bottom decile ~**0%**; profiling half the population raised the ATE **74%** and cut cost per unit **43%**.
- Allcott & Kessler: ML "smart default" targeting **doubles** welfare gains.
- Gerber/Green: the "Neighbors" effect collapsed to **+1 pp** in a high-turnout population but held at **+3 pp** among low-propensity households.

Fields' database already holds the variables that would drive this (tenure, equity position, comps drift versus last sale, listing-adjacency signals). **Mailing 500 well-chosen homes on a cadence will beat mailing 5,000 once**, on every dimension the research measures — effect, cost per outcome, and complaint volume.

## 7.11 The honest expectation to plan against

**The Australian benchmark, and it is brutal.** Under *Your Future, Your Super*, APRA compelled the trustees of 13 failing MySuper products to write to every member — a legally mandated, personally addressed, unambiguous letter saying *the fund holding your retirement savings has officially underperformed*, with real money at stake and a named alternative. APRA's own count six weeks later: of ~**1 million member accounts**, "fewer than 68,000 have been closed" — **7% of accounts, 4.2% of assets** ([APRA, Nov 2021](https://www.apra.gov.au/news-and-publications/apra-urges-super-members-prioritise-their-own-best-financial-interests)).

That is the ceiling case: maximum stakes, maximum authority, zero ambiguity, government mandate. It moved 7%. A Fields mailer has none of those advantages.

**The realistic band across five unrelated industries is 1–8 percentage points**, and only where the data reveals an adverse gap the recipient did not already know about (§2). Where no gap existed — motor insurance where prices had not risen, contents insurance, low-use water households — the effect was **exactly zero**.

Other calibration points:
- **No credible measured response rate exists for homeowner valuation mail.** The ANA/DMA benchmark (9% house / 4.9% prospect) is a small-sample voluntary self-report that moved 76% in a single year. Mail vendors — who profit from optimism — quote **0.5–2%**. When the party with the incentive to inflate quotes the lower number, use the lower number.
- Fields' mail is a **prospect list**, not a house list. The recipient has no relationship with Fields.
- Apply Allcott's site-selection discount: whatever a hand-picked pilot returns, expect **30–50% less** at suburb-wide scale. The insurance case is worse than that — FCA's **+3.2 pp** RCT effect became **−0.8 to −3.0 pp** at national scale.
- **Search is not action.** FCA: motor insurance reminders raised shopping-around **+7.3 to +10.6 pp** and switching by **zero**. Fields must measure inbound enquiry, not page views on a mini-site.
- **Information alone underperforms.** Ferraro & Price's technical-advice-only arm fell *below its own pre-registered 2% minimum detectable effect*. Nine systematic reviews of personalised health risk letters: "no evidence that risk information had strong or consistent effects." **The number alone is not the product.**
- **What produced the large effects was mailing the instrument, not the number** — mailed FIT kits RR **1.36**, mailed HPV self-sampling kits RR **1.51–1.93**. The equivalent for Fields is not a bigger figure on the page; it is removing the next step (a pre-filled request, a QR straight into the property's own page, a named time).

**And the integrity warning that comes with the format.** Every FTC enforcement action found in the automotive "your car is worth $X" vertical involves **fabricated urgency bolted onto a genuine-looking personal notice** — fake prize, fake stimulus payment, fake sweepstakes. The pattern exists because the honest asset number alone does not generate enough response, and the temptation to manufacture the gap is structural. Meanwhile the industry's claimed **4–8%** equity-mining response rate sits *inside* the ordinary house-list benchmark of 5.3%, with no independent verification anywhere. Fields will feel this same pressure when the first drop underperforms. The answer is better targeting (§7.10) and cadence (§7.8), not manufactured urgency.

## 7.12 The one-page rule set

**Content**
1. Lead with the recipient's **own** home's data; cut generic market commentary.
2. **Every sentence must be a retrieved public fact, not an inference about the person.** If it can't survive "where did this come from?", it doesn't go on the page (Kim/Barasz/John: inferred **2.52** < no disclosure **2.96** < stated **3.10**).
3. **Never presume valence.** No auto-celebration of a fall in value, a stale listing, or a distressed neighbouring sale (Meyer).
3b. **Allow-list every merge field; hard-fail the build on any unrecognised or free-text field; proof a physical sample of the real run before every drop** (OfficeMax).
4. **Range, never a point figure, in the headline.** Never print outside the valuation design envelope.
5. **De-identified comparables as evidence. No ranking against named households** (Gerber/Green: "Neighbors" +8.1 pp *and* outrage; "Self" +4.9 pp with modest resistance).
6. Pair any comparative statement with a **constructive/injunctive frame** — never a bare rank (Schultz 2007; Panagopoulos honour-roll).
7. Use a **descriptive norm that locates the recipient**, which is where the effect lives — but expect the outliers you identify to be the ones most likely to complain (Allcott 2011: opt-outs skew to high users).

**Provenance and consent**
8. **State the source of the data and of the address in one plain line, on the page.** Overt beats covert (Aguirre: covert penalty erased by one disclosure line; Kim 5b: +50% clicks).
9. **Only disclose if the provenance is defensible** — disclosure multiplies whatever the source is, in both directions. Resolve list provenance *first* (§3: PropTrack states Valuer General/titles licences bar direct-marketing use of owner information).
10. **Prominent, genuinely easy opt-out on every piece**, plus an obvious correction route. Control beats camouflage (Tucker: 2× CTR from control alone).
11. Be able to answer **"where did you get my details?"** — this is an APP 7.6 obligation, not just courtesy (§6).

**Claims**
12. **No savings/gap claim without written, dated, reproducible grounds held in advance** (ACL "reasonable grounds"; FTC "competent and reliable evidence").
13. **Do not net off costs you have not evidenced** (Opendoor's independent violation).
14. **Ship a confidence/accuracy statement**; use the **off-market** error figure.
15. **The disclaimer must never contradict the headline** — FTC ¶28 and ASA both treated that as evidence *against* the advertiser.
16. **Never the word "valuation".** Use "appraisal", "estimate", "comparable evidence suggests". The vocabulary is a legal control (VRA 1992 (Qld) s 63; VRBQ guidance) — §6.
17. **Hard gate on comparable quality: ≥3 sold, ≤6 months, similar standard/condition, ≤5 km.** If a property can't meet Queensland's own legislated CMA standard (POA s 215 + Sch 2), it doesn't get mailed.
18. **No copy that offers to sell, introduce buyers, negotiate, or invites a listing** — POA s 97(3)(b)-(c) "holds out as being ready" is enough, at 200 penalty units or 2 years.
19. **No invitation to a seminar / briefing / "information session"** without licensed standing — POA s 222(3).
20. **No notional price ("normally $495 — free")** on an unrequested item (ACL Pt 3-1 Div 2).

**Design and format**
21. **Photo of the house: exterior only, sourced from something visibly public, never interior, never implying presence — and randomise it.** Do not stack it with every other maximal-personalisation element (inverted-U).
22. **Randomise creative from mailing one.** You cannot predict which element carries the effect (Bertrand et al.).

**Operations**
23. **Cadence, front-loaded then tapered.** Never a one-shot (2.5–4.2× cost effectiveness).
24. Measure at **10–21 days**, not 48 hours.
25. **Instrument annoyance separately from opt-out.** 34% negative WTP vs ≤3.3% opt-out.
26. **Target hard; mail fewer.** Profiling: +74% ATE, −43% cost per outcome.
27. Plan against **0.5–2%** response and discount any pilot by **30–50%** for scale.

**Before the first drop — lawyer, not preference**
28. **List provenance**, and whether paying for property/owner data destroys the s 6D small-business exemption.
29. **The addressing decision** — WRRA s 106 and POA s 22 define "unsolicited" in opposite directions; no choice is safe on every axis.
30. **POA s 222(1)** — supplying, for reward, particulars of residences *for sale*. This may reach the existing website, not just the mailer.
