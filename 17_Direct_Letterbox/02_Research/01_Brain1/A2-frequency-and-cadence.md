## Part 1 — What the corpus actually says about cadence (the direct answer)

### 1.1 There is a clear modal recommendation: quarterly calls + weekly-to-monthly passive touch, sustained over years

The corpus converges — within RealEstate_Gym — on a specific baseline for homeowners who have *not* asked to hear from you:

- **Quarterly phone contact** is the most frequently named call cadence. **u2562**: "Every single one of them does a weekly email. Every single one of them does a call every quarter." **u0955** applies it at scale: "there's going to be more than one more look at but the great thing is I've got 3,000 3,200 people that I'm calling every quarter." **u0645** operationalises it as "four phone calls a year" wrapped around "four quarter market reviews."
- **Weekly email** rides alongside the quarterly call. **u2562** and **u1511** both state it as the shared habit of top producers: **u1511** — "Do you know of our 10 agents that ride over 600 a year? Every single one of them does a weekly email." **u2726** repeats it verbatim.
- **Weekly letterbox / farm touch** is the physical-mail equivalent. **u0199**: "you are better off doing 5,000 letter box drops a week to the same people each week than just doing one person once in a month." **u1174**: "Three and a half thousand every week." **u0722**: "Once a week." **u0660** claims durability: "I've been dropping those letter boxes twice a week every week without fail for the last seven or eight years."

So the RealEstate_Gym baseline for a cold homeowner is roughly: **a weekly passive touch (email or letterbox) + a personal call once a quarter, continued for years.** That is the doctrine.

### 1.2 The trust ladder is quantified in *number of touches*, not calendar time

The single most useful cross-source finding: the corpus gives an explicit touch-count ladder, and it appears in **both** RealEstate_Gym **and** Agent School — the closest thing to consensus in this set.

- **u0495** (RealEstate_Gym): "9 to 12 contacts, people start to become your friend. 18 to 24 contacts, you need to almost start to become extended family."
- **u1202** corroborates verbatim: "9 to 12 contacts, people start to become your friend. 18 to 24 contacts, you need to, you almost start to become extended family."
- **u0073** (Agent School / Steinwede) independently states the same ladder: "9 to 12 contacts people start to become your friend, 18 to 24 I want to accelerate that." **This is a genuine two-source corroboration** — Panos's doctrine and Steinwede's doctrine agree on the 9–12 / 18–24 thresholds.

Serhant supplies the conversion-side number: **u1229** — "80% of sales are made from the fifth to the 12th contact with a potential client." This aligns with the marketing-book claim in **u3034** (Serhant/Sell It): "80% of sales calls require five follow-ups to get to the close. And most sales people quit after three."

**Synthesis:** friendship begins around touch 9–12; the sale statistically lands between touch 5 and 12. If your cadence is quarterly calls only, touch 9 is over two years away — which is exactly why the doctrine pairs quarterly calls with weekly passive touches: the passive stream accumulates the touch count fast while the calls carry the relationship.

### 1.3 The time horizon is measured in years, and the corpus is emphatic about it

Multiple units, across sources, put the conversion window at **6 months to 15 years**:

- **u1229** (Serhant): "people will research you six to 18 months online and in the market before they actually introduce themselves."
- **u1568**: "after three to six months to nine months to two years, and they've been getting every quarter called from me."
- **u2782**: "I've been chasing him for about two years."
- **u1165**: "I've had properties that have come on the market just recently and I've been in contact with them for five years."
- **u0780**: "that six years is paramount to my success today."
- **u1100 / u0968** (Adrian Bo, RealEstate_Gym): "you've been sending me information and keeping in contact for the last 15 years. I've now decided that I want to list my property."
- **u2854 / u3032** (Serhant): a **five-year** weekly follow-up that closed a "$17 million" sale — "Just because someone isn't responding to you doesn't mean they don't like you."

The marketing-book corpus gives the same shape for B2B: **k01185** — "Your quest, over the next 12 to 18 months, is to meet and/or chat with each of the key contacts"; **k01711** — "about half of those sites eventually, inside of six quarters, become clients" (six quarters = 18 months).

**This is corroborated across all four source families** — RealEstate_Gym, Agent School (u0076: "secure the listing in two years time"), Serhant, and the marketing books all independently describe a multi-month-to-multi-year window. This is the strongest consensus finding in the brief.

### 1.4 The annual "health update" is a distinct, named cadence for the coldest tier

For homeowners with *zero* stated intent, the corpus names a specific low-frequency ritual: the **annual appraisal / health update**.

- **u0475**: "For the life of while you live in this property, I will be providing you with a health update on the real estate market and the value of your home each year. It will be February each year."
- **u1883**: "every 12 months I'm going to come back and do an appraisal for you."
- **u2246**: "By around year 10, all you're doing is appraisal updates to warm people."
- **u0370** gives the fullest annual system: "nine points of contact per year and then at the end of the year I give them all a gift."

Note **u0370**'s "nine points of contact per year" ≈ a touch every ~6 weeks, which sits between the quarterly-call and weekly-email poles and is the most concrete *total* annual cadence figure in the corpus for a nurtured-but-cold contact.

## Part 3 — Where does drop-off or annoyance begin?

This is the weakest-evidenced part of your question, and honesty requires stating that plainly.

### 3.1 The corpus does not provide an annoyance threshold, unsubscribe rate, or complaint metric

There is **no unit** giving an opt-out rate, a "touches before annoyance" number, or a measured drop-off curve for unsolicited contact. The pre-extraction flagged this and it is correct across the full set. Any specific "annoyance begins at N touches" figure would be invented — I will not supply one.

### 3.2 What the corpus offers instead: qualitative guardrails against annoyance

The doctrine's defence against annoyance is **not** a frequency ceiling — it is **making each touch value-first and relevant** so that high frequency reads as service, not pestering:

- **u0139** (Agent School): "I touched them 50 times **not in a creepy way**" — 50 touches is fine *if* framed right. The corpus's implicit position is that annoyance is a function of *content*, not *count*.
- **u2144** (Serhant): "People remember all the little moments where you called them and you reached out to them and **you didn't ask for anything**."
- **u2325**: "Sometimes the best conversation... I've got nothing for you, but I just wanted to see if you're doing well."
- **u2029** (Sell It) gives the ratio: "jab, jab, jab, jab, jab, and then hook... give, give, give, give, give" — roughly 5 value touches per 1 ask. **k01114** (marketing book) corroborates with the 4-1-1 rule (4 shares : 1 self-promo : 1 repost), and **k00009** gives the only explicit warning in the corpus: "Set an email cadence! Don't flood your prospects with too many emails" — though notably **without a number**.
- **u2590** identifies the actual failure mode — *irrelevance*, not frequency: "if they randomly get a bottle of wine but haven't spoken to you in two years they're going to be like what where the hell this come from." The annoyance here comes from a touch that is *disconnected*, not from too many touches.

### 3.3 The one anecdote of an explicit "stop"

The single corpus instance of a prospect actually asking to be left alone is **u2872** (Serhant): a developer returned after "many, many, many, many, books later" *with a deal* and only then said "stop sending me books now." Read carefully: the stop request came **after conversion**, not as a rejection of the cadence. The corpus's lone data point on "annoyance" is a prospect who tolerated years of unsolicited books, converted, and *then* opted out. This is thin evidence, and I present it as a single anecdote, not a pattern.

### 3.4 The counter-doctrine: the corpus explicitly tells agents to fear *under*-contact, not over-contact

Several units frame the risk as entirely on the too-*little* side:

- **u2195**: "When you're selling, a lot of people stop prospecting. You should ramp up your prospecting when you've got a heap of listings."
- **u2228**: "you were sending those contacts to the graveyard. That's where contacts went to die."
- **u0968**: "Unless it's top of mind to start with, it's just not going to happen."
- **u3078** (Serhant): "the fortune is in the follow up... Not following up with leads immediately is one of the biggest fatal mistakes."

**Honest conclusion for Part 3:** The corpus has a strong, single-source-dominated bias *toward* frequency and *against* worrying about annoyance. It offers no measured ceiling. Its only defence against over-contact is qualitative (value-first, relevant, no-ask). **A data-first agency should treat the absence of an annoyance threshold as a gap to measure, not a green light** — the corpus is coaching material selling persistence, and has structural reason to under-report the downside. I flag this as my interpretation, not a corpus claim.
