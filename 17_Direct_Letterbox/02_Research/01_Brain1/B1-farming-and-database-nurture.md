## PART 1 — DIRECT ANSWERS TO THE QUESTION

### 1a. How do you choose the area or list?

Three selection criteria recur, and they are **corroborated across sources**:

**Size cap — the single most-cited number.** The corpus is emphatic that the farm must be small enough to saturate. But note the numbers **disagree**, which matters:
- **u0506** (Tom Panos): "You need to select somewhere between 1,000 and 1,500 homes and say this is the area that I'm going to become a rock star in it"
- **u0915**: "it is far easier to become a rock star and an attraction agent and the agent and specialist of choice in a defined area of up to 2,000 homes"
- **u1168** (Zali Reynolds) sets a *lower* ceiling: "you should choose between 1,500 homes. I think if you start with more than 1,500 homes, you really can't cover the farm area in an appropriate way"
- **u0074**: "You want to become a household name. If it's too big, you don't really create this thing called saturation"

The convergent range is **1,000–2,000 homes**, and the *failure mode of picking too big* is documented as lived experience, not theory — see 3a below (u1955, u2202).

**Economic quality of the patch — turnover and price.** This is where the corpus is most disciplined:
- **u0714** (Zali Reynolds): "You need to make sure that it has got a really good average sale price, you love the homes, and you've got a passion for the area"
- **u1168** (Zali Reynolds): "A patch with better turnover and more appropriate competition, if I can upskill myself and become a better Lister and a better prospector, then I'll make my money"
- **u2555** stresses pre-work: "doing your homework before you even start" — commission rates, market turnover, GCI — as foundational analysis.

Sell It corroborates the *choose-your-market* logic from a niche angle rather than a geographic one — **u3053**: "When you try to speak to everyone, you really speak to no one," and **u2869**: "Identifying the perfect customer starts with what you offer and the problems that you solve." So the two doctrines agree that **narrowing is mandatory**; they differ on whether the boundary is a postcode (RealEstate_Gym) or a customer profile (Sell It).

**Competitive entry analysis — is the patch winnable?**
- **u0714** (Zali Reynolds): "I would never enter that patch because the likelihood of me being able to get into that area... would just take so much longer"
- **u1440**: "Become the biggest fish in the smallest pond. That's the secret."

**→ Fields bridge:** Fields already has this data. The corpus's "turnover + average sale price + winnable competition" checklist is exactly what Fields's union-median pipeline and coverage data can compute *automatically* across Robina, Varsity Lakes, and Burleigh Waters — the three target suburbs. The human agent picks a farm by intuition and homework; Fields can rank micro-pockets by turnover-to-competition ratio from the database. **This is a genuine Fields advantage the corpus cannot describe** — the operators had to guess at patch quality; Fields can measure it.

### 1b. Saturation vs. targeting the warm few

The corpus holds **both positions simultaneously**, and the resolution is a sequence, not a contradiction.

**The case for saturation:**
- **u2516** (the cleanest statement): "I'd rather get called into 100% of the listings that come on the market in my marketplace and have a shit conversion... at least you're going to get one in three"
- **u0660** (Stuart Bourne): "you can buy market share with just flooding the area with marketing" — saturation as a *capital* strategy vs. a labour one.
- **u0199**: "you are better off doing 5,000 letter box drops a week to the same people each week than just doing one person once in a month and doing 20,000 in a whole area... frequency builds trust."

**The case for the warm few:**
- **u0727**: "I focused 80% of my energy on my top 20%... it created relationships with people that trust me now" — and the celebrated line: "you've got a fit database, not a fat database"
- **u1775**: an operator who **cut** his database from 790 to 390 and "my business took off" — "the sacrifice of letting go before I was going to get paid back"
- **u1116**: "One quality call in an hour with an A class person could be worth more than 50 short conversations with people that aren't quality."

**The resolution (the corpus's actual answer):** Saturate the *awareness* layer broadly (letterbox/signboard/social — cheap, passive, top-of-mind), and target the *conversation* layer narrowly (calls, face-to-face — expensive, high-conversion). The two are different channels doing different jobs:
- **u0986**: touchpoints "work in combination, not in isolation... consistency, predictable, repeatable. That's when they seem to work."
- **u2036**: "Touch points work in combination, not in isolation."

**→ Fields bridge:** This maps almost perfectly onto Fields's existing stack. The **saturation layer** is Fields's indexed off-market decks, property pages, and SEO — 14.6k+ indexed pages already act as passive, always-on top-of-mind coverage. The **warm-few layer** is the address-capture → direct-mail strategy the memory files describe. The corpus validates running both at once. **Where Fields is weak vs. the corpus:** the human operators own the warm-conversation layer (they *call*); Fields's memory notes the mail step "has NEVER run" and inbound is "UNFALSIFIABLE" ([lead_funnel_audit_2026-08]). The corpus is unambiguous that the warm layer is where listings actually convert — Fields has built the saturation layer and skipped the conversion layer.

### 1c. How long before it pays back?

This is the most consistently-answered question in the entire corpus, and the numbers cluster tightly. **Two distinct payback clocks:**

After about three months, the three to six months you start to get called in on appraisals." And: "From about six months onwards the conversation changed to all I see you is in my little us."
- **u0660** (same operator): "After about three months, the three to six months you start to get called in on appraisals and you start to at least be on a shopping list"

**Clock 2 — Relationship/database nurture → conversion: years, not months.**
- **u0968** / **u1100** (Adrian): "I've had people literally call me... you've been sending me information for 15 years"

- **u1165**: "I've had properties that have come on the market just recently and I've been in contact with them for five years."

- **u0076**: "The door knock today isn't about finding the lead today... It's about the connection... to secure the listing in two years time"

**Digital/content payback (the closest analogue to Fields's model) — 6–24 months.** This is where the **Sell It and Channel Junkies (YouTube) material is essential**, because it's the only source describing *content-driven* farming rather than physical farming:
- **k01476**: "You'd be lucky to close anything in the first three months, it's more than six to nine."

**→ Fields bridge:** Fields's engine is SEO + content, so **u3073's 6–24 month SEO horizon is the payback clock Fields should actually plan against** — not the 3-month letterbox clock. This is important: the memory file [editorial_py_migration_scoping] notes "Only 2.7% listings indexable; bottleneck is DATA coverage." The corpus says the content farm pays back in 6–24 months *if you sustain it* — Fields is early in that clock, which is the correct and expected place to be, not a failure signal.

### 1d. Cost per listing won

The corpus rarely states cost-per-listing cleanly, but there are hard numbers:

**Farm marketing spend → revenue (the one clean ROI figure):**
- **u2377**: "Last year I wrote $245,000... I invested $15,000 in marketing" — a **~6% marketing-cost ratio**, i.e. roughly $15K farm spend to produce a year's listings in a mature farm.
- **u0707**: "I dropped about a thousand and I did, I got quite a few appraisals but three listings out of it" — 1,000 drops → 3 listings, one campaign.

**Digital cost-per-lead (Sell It / Channel Junkies — directly relevant to Fields's paid channels):**
- **u2973** (Sell It): "$5 per lead is a good cap to start with" — cost-per-lead, not cost-per-listing.
- **u2957** (Sell It): "$5 cost per lead is Reasonable and conservative... we could get closer to a three dollar cost per lead"
- **k01410** (Channel Junkies): "If we convert 1%, I can get these leads down to a dollar or 50 cents... If we close 1% of that... we're talking 10 deals a month" — this is the key formula: **cost-per-listing = cost-per-lead ÷ conversion rate**, so listing cost is dominated by conversion, not ad price.
- **k00640** sets the floor: "look to generate at least $2 in revenue if not more for every dollar spent on marketing."

**The activity-math version of cost (RealEstate_Gym's preferred framing — effort, not dollars):**
- **u2560**: appraisal-to-listing runs "one in five basically" (18.5%); another operator at 22% (9 of 41).
- **u2691** / **u2561**: "one in six come onto market" from desktop appraisals (~15.7%).
- **u2560**: to write $45,000/month "you need two and a half sales a month," back-calculated to a required appraisal count.
- **u2561**: "All our million dollar performers are sitting between 20 and 25 appraisals per month. That's the magic number."

**→ Fields bridge:** The corpus gives Fields two costing models. RealEstate_Gym costs listings in **appraisals** (a warm human tactic Fields doesn't do). Sell It/Channel Junkies cost them in **dollars-per-lead ÷ conversion** — and that is exactly the model Fields's Facebook/Google ad stack already runs on. The **k01410 insight is the one to internalise**: Fields's cost-per-listing will be set by *conversion of the address-capture funnel*, not by ad CPL. Since the memory notes the mail/conversion step has never run, Fields currently has **cost-per-lead data but no cost-per-listing data at all** — the denominator is missing.

### 1e. How the best operators sustain it

The word that appears more than any other across all three sources is **consistency**, and the mechanism cited for sustaining it is **habit replacing motivation**:
- **u1813** / **u1165** / **u0760** / **u1660** / **u1913** (five separate operators, verbatim identical): "the motivation is what got me started, but habit is what keeps me going"
- **u2536**: "Consistency is what I'm most proud of in the last decade of my life."
- **u1009** (David Ding): "I've been... doing this for like 13 years... the same photo in 2011. So the same photo, same David Ding logo, everything's the same" — 13-year visual consistency.
- **u0660** (Stuart Bourne): "I've been dropping those letter boxes twice a week every week without fail for the last seven or eight years"
- **u2547**: "Consistency over intensity... I'll pick a number that's really comfortable and I'll do that"

**Systems and delegation sustain it past solo capacity:**
- **u0714** (Zali Reynolds): "When you start, you need to have higher saturation. Whereas now, I've got my systems in place, my farm area's bigger" — systems let you *expand* the farm.
- **u0893**: "A good product is going to make you money, but a good system is going to make you a fortune."
- **u2309**: "I've got 164 calls per week. I've grown that a lot through outsourcing."
- **u0755**: "It's been an important thing to get my team right. Probably is the most important part."

**Energy management is named as the real sustainability lever:**
- **u0240**: "The girl or guy who's got the most energy prospects the most... energy management is prospecting management"
- **u2575**: "energy is my currency. That energy, things just fall into place when I'm not running at capacity"
- **u2185**: "if you're fit you've got more energy... you can handle rejection better and you can make more calls"

**→ Fields bridge:** This is the corpus's strongest structural gift to Fields. A human agent's consistency is limited by human energy — which is why five operators had to reframe it as habit and one had to outsource. **Fields's saturation layer is software: it never gets tired, never skips a week, never loses motivation.** The nightly editorial pipeline, the incremental off-market deck builds, the automated content engine — these *are* the "twice a week every week without fail for seven years" that u0660 had to grind out by hand. The corpus proves consistency is the #1 sustainability factor and the #1 thing humans fail at; Fields's architecture removes the human failure point from the saturation layer entirely. **But** — the warm-conversation layer (the part that actually converts) still requires a human or a very good agent, and that's the layer Fields hasn't built.

## PART 2 — FAILURE MODES & THE PEOPLE WHO QUIT TOO EARLY

You asked specifically for these, and the corpus is rich here.

### 2a. The dropout statistics (mostly Sell It / external, which is useful — it's not RealEstate_Gym doctrine)
- **k01421**: "87% agents leave the profession within 5 years" and "80% dropout rate around 2 years."
- **u0672** (RealEstate_Gym, on a high-volume protocol): "if we had 100 agents brand new in the industry, by about week two, about 80 of them would not do it" — 80% quit in **two weeks** at 200 calls/200 drops a day.
- **u0952**: "a lot of them will say man this is too hard right now I'm just going to give up which is great for the people who survive right?"

### 2b. The specific quit-point: the first loss, and the 90-day blind window
- **u0655**: "I thought I knew everything... until I lost my first listing" — the first loss is named as the attrition trigger.
- **u0359**: "when you lose your first listing or at the moment that you want to give up... those kinds of mistakes" — quit point *is* the first loss.
- **u0302134162** / **u2262** / **u1036**: "You cannot look at your results for the first 90 days. It's not long enough." Combined with u0302134162: "What I do in this 30 days affects the next 90 days" — there is a structural **90-day lag between work and reward**, and people quit inside it because they can't see the pipeline forming.

### 2c. The dominant failure mode is psychological, not methodological
This is stated repeatedly and unusually bluntly:
- **i2912241956**: "Getting the first listing is a moderate problem that is dramatically amplified by psychology and dramatically reduced by activity volume" — "failure mode is internal (rejection tolerance, delayed gratification) not external."
- **i0302134162**: "financial survival pressure and rejection psychology are #1 failure mode, not method."
- **u2142**: "the technical side of real estate is not as important as the psychological part."
- **u2569** / **u2536**: "Make rejection your best friend."
- **u2321**: "If you're not being rejected enough, you're not trying hard enough."

### 2d. The comparison trap (a discrete, named failure mode)
- **i7912053563** / **u0143** / **u2297**: "Don't compare your chapter one to somebody's chapter 10" (three separate operators) — comparing early results to established agents' timelines is named as a specific quit-driver.

### 2e. Structural farming failure modes (the ones Fields can actually design against)

These are the failures that aren't about willpower — they're about system design, and they map directly onto Fields's Rule 7 "no silent failures" doctrine:

1. **Picking too big an area.** The most-documented structural failure:
   - **u1955**: "I was trying to dominate too big of an area... four suburbs in Brisbane, and there was 20,000 properties."
   - **u2202**: "I went from 20,000 down to 780... I took a quarter of one suburb, rather than four suburbs" — the fix was radical narrowing.
   - **u2520**: an operator noticed rivals "doing two, three million GCI with a fraction of the homes, rather than 20,000, they were doing 1,200 homes."

2. **Dirty database — leads sent "to the graveyard."**
   - **u2228** / **u2726**: "you were sending those contacts to the graveyard. That's where contacts went to die."
   - **u0137**: "Not having a clean database... What's the point of having a name and phone number and address of someone in your database and they don't live there."

3. **Dropping prospecting the moment you get busy** — the slump cycle:
   - **u0080**: "You must not drop your prospecting. Let me repeat that... that's what got you busy."
   - **u0346**: "This is how every real estate sale slump develops. Firstly, generally starts when you've been busy."
   - **u0426**: "the death of a real estate agent is the death of the pipeline."

4. **Single-channel spam** (directly relevant — the corpus says the *old* farming method is dead):
   - **u0037**: "The idea of just constantly sending DLs doesn't work anymore, but saturation marketing does" — i.e. one channel repeated = dead; coordinated multi-channel = alive.
   - **u0096**: "Saturation is a must, but over saturation can push people away" — there is an over-saturation ceiling.
   - **u0980**: "Digital saturation will create relationship dilution" — **the single most Fields-relevant warning in the corpus**: at scale, automated digital touch loses the trust that frequency was supposed to build.

**→ Fields bridge on failure modes:** Fields's Rule 7/7b (heartbeat-must-assert-an-outcome) is *the exact institutional answer* to the corpus's structural failure modes. The human operators fail silently — the busy agent stops prospecting and doesn't notice until the pipeline dies 90 days later (u0346, u0426); the dirty database sends leads "to the graveyard" (u2228) invisibly. These are literally "processes that fail silently and go unnoticed for days-to-weeks." Fields's mandate that every ongoing process must raise on its zero-output path is the codified, automated cure for the human slump cycle. **The u0980 warning — "digital saturation creates relationship dilution" — is the one Fields cannot engineer away**, and the corpus gives no digital-specific solution to it, so I flag it as an open risk, not a solved one.
