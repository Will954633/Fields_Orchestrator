Illumination ladder

Will, on version 1: "this is the correct render I want, I just want more of the coat showing or illuminated, too much of the dog is black." Under inversion the pale coat carries very little ink, so at the default gamma only the stroke edges light up. Gamma below 1 raises the faint ink — which is exactly the coat — without touching the shadows or the character of the render. Both sources, five steps each.

Measured on Source A: mean brightness of the lit pixels goes 84 → 94 → 105 → 119 → 134 across the ladder, while the share of the frame that is lit barely moves (25% → 34%). That is the point — it is lighting the coat that is already there, not spreading the dog.

Chosen: Source A inverted at γ0.5 — version 1's treatment, coat lifted (see the ladder above). Earlier in the session this was v3 inverted at γ1.06. It is a tonal negative — the coat reads dark and the eyes come up bright — and that is the look, taken deliberately over the tonally-correct 12. Everything below still holds; it explains what the choice costs, not that it was the wrong one.
The one finding worth keeping. Numbers 1, 4, 5 and 9 all fail the same way, across two different source drawings and both polarities. That is not a drawing problem — it is the subject. A golden retriever has a tonal arrangement everyone recognises: pale coat, dark eyes, black nose, light ball. Inverting any honest drawing of that produces a dark coat, white eyes, white nose and a vanished ball. And a source drawn so that it inverts correctly would be a photographic negative — which, inverted, returns the same picture as simply not inverting a normal drawing. The two routes are the same image by definition. So the retriever cannot be inverted, and no drawing technique changes that. Plants and flags carry no such expectation, which is why the banksia and the flag invert beautifully.
