
## [ARTICLE-EXCERPT-TRUNCATION] Article subtitles cut mid-word at 300 chars — 15:24 AEST
**Symptom:** The subtitle (dek) on the draft article "Sold in 35 Days: How a Renovated Burleigh Waters Duplex Reached $1,100,000" ended mid-word — "...this home landed above the g". `custom_excerpt` was exactly 300 chars. A sweep of `system_monitor.content_articles` found 13 more articles cut mid-word (8 at 300, 5 at 280) plus 4 carrying raw `**` markdown at the tail — 17 of 92 total, 12 of them published and live.
**Root cause:** Every excerpt write path used a raw character slice with no word-boundary awareness: `scripts/push_to_ghost.py` sliced `stripped[:300]` when deriving the excerpt from the first paragraph and re-sliced `custom_excerpt[:300]` on both the create and update writes to Mongo; `pipeline/run_market_strength.py`, `run_state_of_market.py` and `run_market_insight.py` each did `line[:280]`. The 280-char pipeline cut then hit the 300-char cut downstream, so a long lead paragraph was guaranteed to land mid-word. Bold lead paragraphs also leaked their `**` markers because the excerpt was taken from raw markdown.
**Fix:** Added `scripts/excerpt_utils.py` with `clip_excerpt(text, limit)` — strips markdown emphasis, returns the text unchanged when it fits, otherwise prefers the last complete sentence inside the limit (when it retains ≥60% of the allowance) and falls back to the last whole word plus an ellipsis so a trim is visible rather than silent. Guards a degenerate no-word-boundary input by hard-cutting, and is limit-inclusive of the ellipsis. Wired into all five write sites; `push_to_ghost.py` is the last gate before Mongo, so nothing truncated can reach the DB regardless of which pipeline produced it. Backfilled all 17 damaged excerpts by re-deriving from the article's first HTML paragraph and re-clipping; re-verified zero remaining.

## [HOWITSOLD-GAIN-AND-SEGMENT] Capital gain always 0% + sales benchmarked against the wrong median — 17:05 AEST
**Symptom:** Two defects in the How It Sold individual-home article pipeline. (1) Capital gain read +0.0% on 321 of 600 candidate properties, so the pipeline discarded gains like +260%, +441% and +4,459% and silently omitted the strongest fact in the article. (2) A 2-bedroom duplex at 2/5 Bottlewood Court was reported as "42.9% below the Burleigh Waters median" — it was measured against a houses-only median of $1,925,000. 214 of 600 candidates (36%) were non-house types benchmarked this way.
**Root cause:** (1) `_extract_price_history` returns every Sale event including the sale being written about, and the capital-gain loop took the first entry — the current sale — as the "last purchase", comparing the sale to itself. A date cutoff alone does not fix it: the timeline stores the CONTRACT date while `sold_date` is settlement, so the subject sale usually appears days-to-weeks BEFORE `sold_date`. (2) `median_sale_price` comes from `precomputed_indexed_prices`, which is houses-only, but selection only excludes the two exact strings "Apartment / Unit / Flat" and "Studio" — every Townhouse, Villa, Duplex and untyped record was compared to it. `property_type` is itself unreliable: 56 sold records are typed "House" while carrying a unit-prefixed address, including the Bottlewood duplex.
**Fix:** Added `_prior_sales()` to exclude the subject sale by price (the reliable discriminator) and by date. Added `segment_for()` + `MIN_SEGMENT_N=10` to `mongodb_client`, classifying by address unit-prefix first and `property_type` second; `get_suburb_context_and_price_history` now accumulates house and attached prices in the same single pass (no extra RU) and returns `segment_medians` plus per-bedroom sample counts. `assemble()` picks the segment-matched median and suppresses the comparison entirely below MIN_SEGMENT_N rather than borrowing the house median; thin bedroom buckets (some n=1) are suppressed too, which also gates the ±20% outlier flag that spends a GPT-4o call. The prompt now names what the median covers and states its transaction count, making the sample-size rule in `editorial_rules.md` satisfiable.
**Result (600-property candidate pool):** capital gain computed 92 → 412, falsely-zero 321 → 0. Median comparison: 366 house, 229 attached, 5 suppressed. 2/5 Bottlewood Court now reads "1.3% above the Burleigh Waters townhouse and villa median (based on 12 sales)" with a +44.7% capital gain that was previously dropped.

## [BROWSE-RAIL-IMG-BYTES] Off-screen rail photos ate 68% of the bytes before LCP — 08:50 AEST
**Symptom:** Home page (`/`, the browse surface — the logo's destination) measured mobile LCP
~3.7-3.9s on a throttled profile (1.6Mbps 4G, 4x CPU). CLS 0, TTFB 41-57ms, FCP ~0.95s — server
and layout were both fine.
**Root cause:** the page is bandwidth-bound, and it was spending its bandwidth on images nobody
could see. 18 property images (527KB) were fetched **before** LCP with only 2 of them on screen —
68% of the 776KB that transferred pre-LCP. 776KB / 200KB/s ≈ 3.9s, matching measured LCP almost
exactly. The cause is that a `Rail` scrolls **horizontally**, so every card in it shares the
vertical offset of the visible ones. Chrome's native lazy-load threshold is measured vertically,
so `loading="lazy"` cards parked off to the RIGHT read as "near the viewport" and were fetched
immediately. 125 cards / 107 `<img>` were in the SSR HTML. On a 3x phone Chrome also picks the 2x
srcSet entry, so each wasted card cost ~51-63KB rather than ~26KB.
**Fix:** added `useNearViewport()` in `RailCard.tsx` — cards past the first few hold their photo
until an IntersectionObserver sees them. IO against the viewport DOES account for clipping by an
ancestor overflow container, so it gets the horizontal axis right where native lazy loading
cannot. Rails 1-2 keep `VISIBLE_PER_RAIL = 4` photos in SSR; rails 3+ are below the fold at every
viewport size and defer all of them. The `<a>` and all card text stay in the SSR HTML, so crawl
discovery is untouched (**verified: 81 unique `/property/` links before and after**; SSR `<img>`
107 -> 11). `.shot` already had `aspect-ratio` + a background, so the deferred state reserves
identical space — CLS stayed 0.
**Measured (median of 4, throttled 4G + 4x CPU):** LCP **3.76s -> 2.97s**; property images before
LCP **18 -> 7**; image bytes before LCP **527KB -> 188KB**; all bytes before LCP **776KB -> 439KB**;
DOM `<img>` 125 -> 13. Unthrottled mobile LCP is ~330ms. Still above the 2.5s "good" threshold on
the throttled profile — the remaining pre-LCP cost is the 4 SSR'd photos per rail on rails 1-2,
of which a 390px phone shows ~1.5.
**Also fixed:** the whale preload guard checked `inWhaleSeason()` but never `alreadySeen()`,
despite its own comment claiming it cost nothing "after they have had their whale" — so every
repeat visit through Jul-Sep re-warmed ~390KB for a moment that could not play again.
`alreadySeen()` is now exported and part of the guard.
**Two measurement traps hit on the way (both cost real time):**
  1. `fetchPriority` is emitted by React 19 SSR in **camelCase** (`fetchPriority="high"`), so a
     case-sensitive `grep 'fetchpriority='` finds nothing and looks like a missing priority hint.
     The LCP image was already `eager` + `fetchPriority=high`; that was never the bug.
  2. Sampling "the first N above-fold images" found only the blurred `LockedCard`s, which are
     lazy **by design**. Read the actual LCP element's attributes via the PerformanceObserver
     entry, not a positional guess.
  Also: blocking the whale appeared to cut LCP by 1.7s on a single run; across 4 runs each way the
  medians were 3.76s vs 3.87s — pure noise. Single-run A/B on this VM is not trustworthy.

`src/pages/BrowsePage/BrowsePage.tsx`, `src/components/WhaleMoment/WhaleMoment.tsx`,

## [PROPERTY-PAGE-UNCACHED] Property pages ran 3 uncached server calls per view — 09:30 AEST
**Symptom:** `/property/:slug` — the pages that carry the Google referral traffic — measured LCP
**5.5-5.8s unthrottled** and ~9.9s on throttled mobile. The initial paint hid it: FCP was ~1.7s
because the SSR shell (address, beds, price) renders fine; the actual analysis arrived seconds
later.
**Root cause:** three separate server round-trips, none of them cached, stacked in series.
  1. `routes/property.$id.tsx` had **no `headers()` export**, so the document served
     `cache-control: no-cache` with `cache-status: "Netlify Edge"; fwd=miss` — every visitor ran
     the loader live. Document TTFB 0.85-1.86s, against 41ms on `/`, which already had the export.
  2. `/api/v1/properties/for-sale/:slug` — also `no-cache`, TTFB a consistent **3.2-3.4s**.
  3. `/api/valuation/current_listings_comps` — also `no-cache`, **234KB** body at **2.4s**, and it
     only surfaced as the bottleneck once (1) and (2) were fixed.
  The **`[[headers]]` block in netlify.toml declaring `max-age=300` for `/api/v1/*` does not apply
  to Function responses** — those blocks cover static assets. Caching has to be set on the
  `Response` itself. That is why the intent was there but no endpoint honoured it.
**Not the cause (checked first, ruled out by measurement):** the single-property handler loops
serially over suburb collections doing `findOne({url_slug})`, which looks like the obvious
culprit. But `url_slug` **is** indexed on every suburb collection and each lookup is 1-6ms;
timing every DB operation the function performs totals **32ms**. The remaining ~3.15s is
`getFreshClient()` in `db.mjs` opening a **new Cosmos connection per invocation** — the deliberate
workaround for the Netlify/Mongo event-loop hang. Caching makes that handshake a per-window cost
instead of a per-request one; the workaround itself was left alone.
**Fix:** added the `/`-style `headers()` export to the property route
(`s-maxage=300, stale-while-revalidate=600`); set the same policy on the single-property and
valuation-comps responses. `jsonResponse()` in `db.mjs` gained an optional third `extraHeaders`
argument, defaulting to `{}` so all 55 existing callers are unchanged. Not-found responses cache
60s so a retired URL cannot reopen a connection on every hit.
**Measured (warm, median):** document TTFB **1.39s -> 0.03s**; single-property API **3.2s -> 0.037s**;
comps API **2.4s -> ~0.46s**; **unthrottled LCP 5.6s -> ~1.07s**; throttled-mobile LCP **2.5s (with
content at ~9.9s) -> ~1.18s**. CLS stayed 0. Rendered page carries 19,697 chars incl. the full
editorial; 0 console messages.
**Honest caveat — the first visitor still pays.** On a genuine cache miss the document is
**2.6-2.75s** (warm: 0.077s). Caching moves the cost, it does not remove it; the underlying
per-invocation connection setup is still there. A 5-minute window on a long-tail property page
means a fair share of organic entries will be cold. Removing that floor means either revisiting
`getFreshClient` or lengthening the TTL.
**Still open (deliberately not done here):** the analysis is **not in the SSR HTML** — only 1,090
chars of visible text are server-rendered, and the editorial arrives only via the client fetch.
Googlebot renders JS on a deferred pass, so this is an indexing-quality risk, not just a speed
one. Folding the payload into the SSR loader is the real fix and is its own change.

## [PYTHON-REPLACE-EMPTY-BOMB] str.replace('') inflated a 43KB README to 142MB — 13:12 AEST
**Symptom:** GitHub `contents` PUT began returning `409 Repository rule violations found / Timed out validating rule` for one file, repeatedly, across 4 retries. Read as a transient GitHub fault. It was not.

## [FRIDGE-HUM-EDGE-FADE] Hum still faded in/out — a 22.7dB fade was baked INTO the loop file — 14:15 AEST
**Symptom (Will, 2nd report):** "sound is still fading in and out" — after [FRIDGE-HUM-DUCKING] had already made the playback gain constant and verified it (0.17 shut, 0.17 open).
**Why the first fix missed:** I fixed the gain because that was the mechanism I had built. The gain was provably constant, so the fade had to be in the audio itself — but I only re-measured what I had changed. Measuring the DECODED buffer in the browser found it immediately: RMS 0.001 at the head vs 0.122 in the middle, a **22.7 dB** swing, envelope `..:-=+*###@@@@@@@@#@@@@##@#*+-:..` — i.e. the 4s loop faded up and back down every single cycle.
**Root cause:** `dynaudnorm` normalises over a gaussian window (f=250ms x g=7 = 1.75s) and until that window is full it has nothing to work from, so it **ramps in over ~0.9s at the start of whatever you give it and out at the end**. `build_audio.sh` applied it to an ALREADY-SLICED 4.8s segment, so both ends of the loop were faded to near-silence.
**Second, smaller bug found alongside:** the loop join used `acrossfade c1=tri:c2=tri`, which is equal-GAIN. Two different bits of a noise recording sum incoherently, so equal-gain loses ~3dB through the middle of the fade. Equal-POWER (`qsin`) is correct for uncorrelated material.
**Fix:** (1) flatten the WHOLE recording first so the ramps land on the file's own unused edges; (2) `acrossfade ... c1=qsin:c2=qsin`; (3) dynaudnorm alone still left 3.6-3.9dB of drift (peak-based, and it smooths its own gain curve), so flattening is now **deterministic**: measure the RMS envelope, divide it out with ~1s smoothing (slow drift goes, short-term texture stays), normalise to a **target RMS, not to peak** — normalising to peak first dropped the bed 23dB (RMS 3671 -> 250) and would have made it inaudible; (4) the flattest 6.6s window is now SEARCHED for, not chosen by eye.
**Gate added:** `build_audio.sh` now decodes its own output and **fails the build** above 2.5dB total swing or 1.5dB edge-vs-middle. This class of defect was only caught by a human listening on a phone, twice.
**Verified live:** 6s loop, head **-0.1dB** and tail **-0.4dB** vs middle, 2.0dB total swing (was 22.7dB).
**Lesson:** when a fix doesn't work, re-measure the OUTPUT, not the thing you changed. And any filter with a lookahead/window ramps at the edges of its input — normalise before slicing, never after.
