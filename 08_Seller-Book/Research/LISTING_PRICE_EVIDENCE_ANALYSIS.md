# The Listing Price: What the Evidence Actually Says

**A research analysis for Fields Estate — compiled 11 August 2026**

Assembled to test the campaign line *"The listing price is a buyer attraction tool — not your expected selling price"*, and the strategic angle behind it: that a listing price should be set to reach every buyer who can afford the home, to signal the value we believe it holds, and to bring multiple buyers into competition.

**Evidence base:** 60+ peer-reviewed papers verified to primary source where obtainable; portal and industry data from REA, PropTrack, Rightmove, Redfin, Zillow, Zoopla, NAR, Cotality and PropRadar; Australian legislation read directly; plus Fields' own measured data. Roughly 4.5 million Australian transactions and 15 million international transactions sit behind the numbers cited here.

Supporting detail: [SYNOPSIS_higher_price_evidence.md](SYNOPSIS_higher_price_evidence.md) · [SYNOPSIS_listing_price_is_attraction_tool.md](SYNOPSIS_listing_price_is_attraction_tool.md)

# Part 1 — Conclusions first

## 1.1 The single most important finding

**The entire industry argument about listing price is conducted on the wrong axis.**

Both sides argue about price. One camp says list high to anchor the buyer upward. The other says list low to start a bidding war. When you measure them, **both effects are trivially small**:

| Channel | Best available estimate | On a $1,400,000 home |
|---|---|---|
| Anchoring upward (overprice 10–20%) | **+0.05% to +0.07%** | **+$700 to $980** |
| Competition downward (underprice 10%) | **+0.08% to +1.8%** | **+$1,100 to $25,000** |

Meanwhile the effects on **whether you sell at all** are enormous and unanimous across every study, every country and every decade:

| Effect | Estimate | Source |
|---|---|---|
| +1% on the list price | **−5.6%** relative probability of sale within 13 weeks | Guren (2018), 664k listings, IV-identified |
| +5% on the list price | **−45%** relative probability of sale | Guren (2018) |
| ≥12% above estimated value | **~50%** less likely to sell within 60 days | Zillow (2015) |
| Needed a price reduction | **63% sold vs 32%**; 21 days vs 47 to find a buyer | Rightmove, 300,000+ UK listings |
| Never sold at all | **~30% of US listings**, priced **8.1% above** fair value | Seiler & Siebert (2025), *Real Estate Economics* |

**Conclusion:** the listing price is not a lever that moves your sale price much in either direction. It is the lever that determines whether a sale happens, how fast, and to how many competing buyers. That is a far bigger claim than the one the industry argues about, and it is the one the evidence actually supports.

## 1.2 The campaign line is defensible — with one correction

The line **"the listing price is a buyer attraction tool"** is well supported. The listing price is the single most important determinant of who ever sees the home.

The phrase **"not your expected selling price"** needs care, because it is empirically ambiguous:

- **True** if it means *not your aspiration, not your hoped-for number, not a cushion to negotiate down from.* That reading is strongly supported.
- **False** if it implies the listing price and the sale price are only loosely related. In Fields' own data the median first-ask-to-sale gap is **−2.5%** (n=54) and **48% of sold homes sold below their first asking price** (n=142). In private treaty they are *very* close. The listing price is close to the best realistic outcome, not a distant marker.

**The correction:** the listing price is not your *hoped-for* price. It is approximately your *achievable* price — which is precisely why setting it wrong is expensive.

## 1.3 The third pillar of the angle does not survive

The strategic angle assumed that a listing price set to attract creates multiple bidders and that competition then lifts the price above it. **Three large peer-reviewed studies in three countries test exactly this and all reach the same answer: deliberate underpricing lowers the final price, even when it successfully creates the bidding war.**

| Study | Sample | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Hungria-Gunnelin, Kopsch & Enegren (2021) | **11,658** Gothenburg condos, 2012–16 | Hypothesis rejected. 1% of underpricing costs **0.85%** of sale price. **86% of these sold above list, averaging ~10% above** — the bidding war happened, and the price was still lower |
| Kopsch, Helgason, Hansson & Johansson (2021) | **31,671** Reykjavík-region sales, 2014–20 | *"a property under-priced with 10% will result in a sales price reduction of 9.047%"* |
| Hammervold, Sønstebø, Olaussen & Oust (2025), *Real Estate Economics* | **15,288** Norwegian sales, 2013–19 | Structural equation model measures the full chain: underpricing **raises** bid count, bidder count and auction temperature — **and still lowers the selling price** |

Han & Strange, authors of the field's standard survey, call the underprice-to-bidding-war idea **"folklore"**.

**And in Australia it is illegal.** Victoria's Estate Agents Act s47C: *"You must not market a property for sale at a lower price than your estimated selling price"* — 200 penalty units, $41,820. In Queensland the concept is **bait advertising**: an "offers over" figure must reflect the vendor's genuine acceptance threshold, and setting one below it is false advertising (POA 2014 s212, 540 penalty units). Australian Consumer Law adds corporate penalties to $100m.

**Conclusion:** the campaign can say the listing price attracts buyers. It cannot say, or imply, that the listing price should sit below what the seller expects to achieve.

## 1.4 What the campaign *can* say — five claims, ranked

1. **In a private-treaty market, a home only sells above its asking price when two or more buyers bid at the same time.** Merlo & Ortalo-Magné tracked 780 complete London transactions with every offer recorded: *"we detect the occurrence of about 30 de facto auctions out of 780 transactions… such de facto auctions account for **all** instances in the data where the sale price is above the listing price."* All of them. This is the cleanest, most quotable finding in the entire literature and it is exactly the mechanism Fields wants to describe.
2. **Competition, not negotiation, sets the price.** Bulow & Klemperer (1996), *American Economic Review*: *"No amount of bargaining power is as valuable to the seller as attracting one extra bidder."* Corroborated for Australia by Genesove & Hansen (2025), *Review of Economics and Statistics*, on a census of ~4 million Sydney and Melbourne sales: **auction prices reflect buyers' values, negotiated prices split the difference, and list prices reflect only the seller's.**
3. **Your listing price decides who ever sees the home.** Merlo & Ortalo-Magné's bracket test is the peer-reviewed proof; Queensland's own legislation concedes the mechanism; REA's own research says 72% of buyers skip a listing without a price.
4. **Overpricing costs you buyers, and you only get one launch.** Guren's −5.6% per 1%; Redfin's 3.4× day-one views, where a price cut buys back exactly one day.
5. **The real cost of overpricing is not a lower price — it is not selling.** ~30% of listings never sell, and the unsold ones are priced 8.1% above fair value.

# Part 2 — The chain, tested link by link

The strategic angle is a three-link chain. Each link holds differently.

## Link 1 — Does the number of competing buyers drive the price?

### The theory is exact

**Bulow, J. & Klemperer, P. (1996), "Auctions Versus Negotiations," *American Economic Review* 86(1):180–194.**

Their corollary, verbatim (p.189): *"With independent signals and risk-neutral bidders, an absolute English auction with N + 1 bidders is more profitable in expectation than any negotiation with N bidders."*

The worked example is the cleanest number available. Seller value 0, buyer values uniform on [0,1]:

- **One bidder, plus a perfectly optimal take-it-or-leave-it price → expected revenue 0.25**
- **Two bidders, open auction, no reserve → expected revenue 0.333**
- **One extra serious bidder is worth +33% against perfect monopoly bargaining power**

The authors' own strategic conclusion (p.182): *"a seller should generally focus on maximizing the number of bidders, and should refuse to bargain with bidders who wish to preempt the auction process."*

**The conditions matter and are routinely dropped when this is quoted.** Bidders must be "serious" — every extra bidder must value the asset at least as much as the seller does, so a tyre-kicker does not count. Values and strategies must be symmetric. Bidders risk-neutral. No reserve in the (N+1) auction. And the seller in the comparison case is assumed to have *full* bargaining power and commitment, which the authors note makes the result conservative. It is a theorem about asset sales, not a housing measurement. **Cite it as the principle, never as a magnitude.**

### The measured magnitudes are small

| Study | Sample | Effect of more bidders |
|---|---|---|
| **Hungria-Gunnelin (2013)**, *Int. Real Estate Review* 16(3) | 512 Stockholm condos, 2010 | **~+4%** going from one to two bidders, decelerating thereafter. ⚠ No controls for building age or condition — high-quality apartments may simply attract more bidders |
| **Genesove & Han (2012b)**, working paper | Large North American metro, 2005–09 | **Doubling the number of bidders raises sale price 2.4%.** Spread of bidders' valuations for the same house is 4–5% of value. ⚠ Not peer-reviewed |
| **Merlo & Ortalo-Magné (2004)**, *J. Urban Economics* 56(2) | 780 London transactions with every offer recorded | **+£430 per additional bidder** on a mean £66,964 = **+0.64% per competing buyer** |

**Analysis.** There is a real gap between the theory and the measurement. Bulow–Klemperer says one extra bidder beats any amount of negotiating skill; the housing estimates say an extra bidder is worth well under 1%, and doubling the field is worth 2.4%. Both can be true: the theorem compares an auction against *perfect* monopoly bargaining, a benchmark no real seller achieves, and it assumes every additional bidder is serious. In housing, the marginal extra "bidder" is often someone who was never going to stretch.

The practical read: competition is directionally the right thing to maximise, but it is not a lever that produces double-digit premiums. Anyone promising that is not describing measured evidence.

### The finding that matters most for Fields

**Merlo & Ortalo-Magné (2004)**, p.6, verbatim:

> *"we detect the occurrence of about 30 de facto auctions out of 780 transactions… such de facto auctions account for **all** instances in the data where the sale price is above the listing price."*

This is the strongest single result in this whole body of work for Fields' purposes. It comes from a private-treaty market with no auctions, it records every offer on every transaction, and it establishes a categorical fact: **selling above the asking price only ever happened when two or more buyers bid simultaneously.** 30 out of 780 — about 3.8% of sales.

That does two jobs. It proves competition is the *only* route above list. And it sets an honest expectation about how often that happens.

### The Australian gap

**Bidder count has never been estimated against price in Australia.** Genesove & Hansen, working with a census of ~4 million Sydney and Melbourne transactions, state outright: *"We have no data on the number of bidders at individual auctions."* If the best-resourced study of Australian housing could not obtain bidder counts, no one else has them either.

What Australia does have is bidder *trend* data from Ray White (>64,000 campaigns since 2022, roughly one in four of all Australian auctions): registered/active bidders fell from **8.4 / 4.1** at the September 2021 peak to **4.7 / 3.1** by June 2025 and **~4.0 / 2.7** across 2025. Domain reported Brisbane at ~2.5 registered bidders in May 2026, down from 5 earlier that year, with open-home attendance at 2.1 groups against 3.5.

**Analysis.** Competition in the current market is thin and thinning. Any campaign built on "we will create a bidding war" is promising something the market is delivering less of, in a state where 95% of sales are private treaty and the mechanism Merlo & Ortalo-Magné identified fires in under 4% of transactions. The defensible framing is that competition is *the* route to a premium — not that it is a likely outcome.

**Verdict on Link 1: supported in principle, small in magnitude, unmeasured in Australia.**

## Link 2 — Does the listing price determine how many buyers see the home?

**This is the strongest link in the chain, and the best-evidenced part of the campaign.**

### The peer-reviewed core

**Merlo & Ortalo-Magné (2004)** remains the key paper. On a sub-sample of 199 London properties with viewing records (mean 9.54 viewings per sale, 1.74 per week on market):

- **Listing price coefficient on weekly viewing arrivals: −0.395 (SE 0.154)**, significant at 5%.
- Authors' conclusion: *"a lower listing price increases the arrival rate of viewings, which in turn increases the arrival rate of offers. Thus, **the listing price affects the arrival of offers indirectly, by affecting the arrival of viewings**."*
- **Viewing arrival decays monotonically from listing with no discrete drop after weeks 1–2.** There is no stock of waiting buyers. **Interest is a flow, not a stock** — which is the analytical basis for every "first week matters" claim, and a better one than the unsourced figures currently circulating internally.

**And the bracket test — the single most important piece of evidence for the campaign.** Merlo & Ortalo-Magné split price reductions into those that move a property into a *lower* £5,000 segment and those that leave it in the *same* segment:

> **A price cut that moves the property into a lower price segment significantly raises the probability of a first offer arriving. A cut that leaves it in the same segment has no effect at all.**

Robust to controlling for the size of the reduction, which is itself insignificant. It holds for offers and for viewings. And about **half of all initial listing prices in their sample sat within £50 of a £5,000 segment boundary** — sellers and agents already behave as though the boundaries are real.

Their stated mechanism: *"potential buyers restrict their search to properties whose listing price is below a certain amount… The marginal cost (in terms of a decrease of the arrival rate of potential buyers) of raising her listing price up to the nearest mass point is zero."*

**That last clause is the whole campaign in one sentence.** Between two bracket boundaries, raising your price costs you nothing in reach. Crossing one costs you an entire audience. This is precisely the argument the book makes about $1,495,000 versus $1,510,000 — except here it is measured, published and peer-reviewed rather than asserted.

### Supporting measurements

| Study | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Guren (2018)**, *Journal of Political Economy* 126(3) | 663,976 listings, SF Bay/LA/San Diego, 2008–13, IV-identified, first-stage F = 206 | **+1% list price → −2.7pp probability of sale within 13 weeks** (base 48pp) = **−5.6% relative**. **+5% → −21.5pp = −45%.** Demand is strongly concave: overpricing destroys sale probability fast, underpricing barely helps. Guren's own first-listed mechanism: *"buyers may avoid visiting homes that appear to be overpriced"* |
| **Han & Strange (2016)**, *J. Urban Economics* 93 | 3,193 buyer interviews matched to 89,891 MLS transactions, 2006–09 | The only direct measurement of asking price → bidder count. **10% lower ask → +2.2% bidders** (+4% with quality controls; +2.4% normal markets, +4% in busts; up to **+69%** for highly atypical homes) |
| **Vandenbergh (2024)**, *J. Housing Economics* 64 | Immoweb.be daily ad tracking, 2022–23, property fixed effects | **+1% list price → −6.7% search activity.** The penalty is *worse* in a well-supplied market |
| **Zillow (2015)** | All US listings Q4 2014 sold by Mar 2015 | First-week page views are the strongest single predictor of sale: **<100 views → 12%** chance of selling in 60 days; **≥280 views → ~36%** |
| **Redfin (2019)** | 1.2m+ US listings | Newly listed homes get **3.4× more views on day 1 than on the day of a price drop**. 100 views day 1 → ~50 day 2 → ~25 after a week → 17/day at 30 days. **A price cut lifts it to 29 views for a single day, then back to 18** |

### Two honesty problems the campaign must respect

**First: Han & Strange's raw relationship is positive.** Without location controls, their regressions return **+0.08, +0.09, +0.14** — higher asking price, *more* bidders. The sign only flips negative once district fixed effects are added, because expensive suburbs have both higher prices and more buyers. The authors treat their controlled estimate as a lower bound, but anyone can run the naive version and get the opposite answer. Do not present the price-to-bidders relationship as self-evident.

**Second: there is a live conflict in the literature on elasticity.** Vandenbergh finds −6.7% search per +1% price. **Merlo, Ortalo-Magné & Rust (2015)**, *International Economic Review* 56(2), find the opposite: *"the estimated relationship between the list price and the expected rate of arrival of potential buyers is **relatively inelastic**."* They calculate that a menu cost below **0.006% of house value** (about £12 on £200,000) is enough to explain observed list-price stickiness — precisely because the traffic response is weak. Different countries, decades and outcome variables (offers versus portal clicks), so both may be locally right. But the reach elasticity is not settled, and Fields should not quote a single number for it.

### The Australian evidence — and the best asset Fields has

**Queensland's own legislation concedes the mechanism.** Property Occupations Regulation 2014 (Qld) s10 prescribes the exact wording an agent must use on a no-price listing:

> *"This property is being sold by auction or without a price and therefore a price guide can not be provided. **The website may have filtered the property into a price bracket for website functionality purposes.**"*

**The Queensland Parliament wrote into regulation that price brackets exist and that portals use them to decide who sees a listing.** For an Australian audience this is stronger than any portal statistic, any overseas study and any agent's opinion. It is the law conceding the point.

Alongside it:

- **REA Property Seeker (published 26 Oct 2023, >6,000 Australians): 72% of buyers say they would skip over properties that don't include a price.** REA's own Chief Product & Audience Officer describes missing prices as the number one source of consumer dissatisfaction on realestate.com.au. **54% of sellers said they would choose another agent if pricing were withheld.**
- **REA Property Seeker 2025 (n=2,051 buyers): price is used by 76% and called *most important* by 61%** — against address 25%, features 22%, suburb 21%, **photos 13%**, floorplans 12%.
- **Fields' own measurement (n=205 live listings, Robina/Varsity Lakes/Burleigh Waters, 10 Aug 2026): 79% state no estimate of value.** 42.4% show only an "Offers Over" floor, 36.6% no number at all, 19.5% a single price, and exactly **one listing in 205** showed a genuine range.

**Analysis.** That last statistic is the commercial opportunity, and it is Fields' own. Four in five local listings give the buyer nothing to judge, in a market where price is the single most-used piece of information and where three-quarters of buyers say they skip listings without one. A campaign arguing that the price field is a reach decision is not arguing against the market's practice — it is arguing against a practice the market's own buyers say drives them away.

One caveat REA also reports and Fields should not ignore: buyers complain that listings **well out of their price range appear in search results due to inaccurate prices**. The bracket cuts both ways. Being visible to the wrong bracket is a cost too.

### A mechanism that is often conflated and should not be

**Repetto & Solís (2020)**, *Journal of the European Economic Association* 18(6) — 349,476 Swedish apartment sales 2010–15, 27,173 with bidder-level data — find that an asking price set **just below a round million** attracts **+0.72 more bidders and +2.7 more bids (+25% and +30%)**, producing a **3–5% higher final price**, identified by regression discontinuity.

That looks like the bracket argument, and it is not. **The authors explicitly reject the search-filter explanation.** On Hemnet, a listing priced exactly at a bracket boundary appears in *both* brackets, so round-number listings are if anything more visible. And when Hemnet replaced a manual slider with pre-set price brackets on 12 March 2011, a difference-in-differences test showed the price discontinuity was present before *and* after. Their conclusion is **left-digit inattention** — buyers reading $1,995,000 as "one-nine" rather than "two" — not search filtering.

**Do not merge the two.** Bracket reach (Merlo & Ortalo-Magné, Rightmove, Qld Reg s10) and left-digit inattention (Repetto & Solís) are separate mechanisms with separate evidence. Conflating them makes the claim easier to attack.

### Related proof that markets cluster at price thresholds

Transaction-tax "notch" studies are the hardest evidence that price thresholds bend real behaviour:

- **Best & Kleven (2018)**, *Review of Economic Studies* 85(1) — every UK property transaction 2004–12, ~10 million. At the £250,000 and £500,000 stamp duty notches, excess bunching is **1.85× and 1.64×** the counterfactual density, with a **£25,000 hole above each notch**. The average house-price response is **£10,000, twice the size of the tax jump**.
- **Kopczuk & Munroe (2015)**, *AEJ: Economic Policy* 7(2) — NYC's $1,000,000 mansion-tax notch: *"about $20,000 worth of transactions shift to the threshold in response to the $10,000 tax."* Critically, on 71,875 Manhattan listings they find the bunching **in asking prices** — the distortion is present the moment the property is advertised.

**Verdict on Link 2: strongly supported, and this is where the campaign should live.**

## Link 3 — Therefore, price at defensible value

The first two links support a conclusion about *not overpricing*. They do not support *underpricing*, and this is where the strategic angle needs correcting.

### The arithmetic that settles it

Chain Han & Strange's bidder elasticity to Genesove & Han's price-per-bidder effect. Genesove & Han's 2.4% per doubling implies a price elasticity with respect to bidder count of ln(1.024)/ln(2) = **0.034**.

| Case | 10% cut in asking price buys | Which is worth |
|---|---|---|
| Typical home, normal market | +2.4% bidders | **+0.08%** |
| Typical home, bust, quality-controlled | +4.0% bidders | **+0.13%** |
| Atypical home, 90th percentile — best case in the entire literature | +69% bidders | **+1.79%** |

**You give up 10% of the anchor to buy back between 0.08% and 1.8%.** Even the most favourable case in the published literature loses badly.

*(This calculation combines two studies with different samples and is illustrative, not a published finding. But the direction is not in doubt, and it is corroborated directly by the three Nordic studies in §1.3, which measured the outcome rather than inferring it.)*

### Why the anchoring counter-argument doesn't rescue the other side either

**Bucchianeri & Minson (2013)**, *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 89, >14,000 listings across Delaware, New Jersey and Pennsylvania 2005–09, is the paper usually cited to justify listing high. Overpricing by 10–20% raises the sale price by **$117 to $163** — about **0.05–0.07%**.

> ⚠ **Correction to earlier Fields documents.** This figure has circulated internally in a form that could be read as $117,000–$163,000. It is **$117 to $163**. An error of roughly 1,000×. The anchoring effect is statistically significant and economically negligible.

The authors' load-bearing caveat: the result holds *"controlling for how long one waits to sell"* — i.e. it is the price effect with the time cost removed, which is not a choice a real seller gets to make.

### So both extremes fail, for the same reason

Neither channel is big. What is big is the transaction-probability effect, and that is asymmetric: **strongly concave.** Guren's estimates show overpricing destroys sale probability quickly while underpricing barely improves it, and Zillow's 2016 analysis found the same shape — homes selling ~10% above list took about the same time as homes selling at list, while homes selling 10% below list spent **5× as long** on market. **There is a large penalty for overpricing and no speed bonus for underpricing.**

**Verdict on Link 3: the anti-overpricing half is strongly supported. The pro-underpricing half is contradicted by the best evidence and is illegal in Australia.**

# Part 3 — The counter-case, unsoftened

Anyone challenging this campaign will reach for these. They should be understood before publication, not after.

## 3.1 The Nordic trilogy

Detailed in §1.3. Three studies, three countries, 58,617 transactions combined, all finding that strategic underpricing lowers the final price. The Gothenburg study is the most damaging to the "attract with a low price" thesis because **86% of its transactions sold above list, averaging around 10% above** — the bidding war is not hypothetical in that market, it is the norm, and the price outcome is still worse. The Norwegian paper models the full causal chain and finds the mechanism fires and the outcome is still negative.

**A methodological objection worth knowing, in fairness to the other side.** These studies construct "degree of underpricing" as (list − model-predicted value) / predicted value. The list price contains information about true value that a hedonic model misses, so the underpricing variable absorbs the model's own prediction error, and regressing sale price on it partly regresses price on price. The tell is Kopsch et al.'s R² leaping from 0.80 to 0.97 when the variable is added; the authors concede it *"does contain information about estimated prices."* Han & Strange's design — outcome measured as **number of bidders** rather than price — is immune to this and is the cleanest test in the literature.

But three independent teams reaching the same sign in three countries is not dismissible on one objection.

## 3.2 The Australian auction premium is a fraction of what the industry claims

**Cortés & Singh (2026), *Review of Finance*** — over 480,000 residential transactions, NSW and Victoria, 2007–2019:

- **Successful auction premium: +0.7%** (roughly $2,000–$2,500)
- **Properties that sold after failing at auction: −1.3%** (roughly $9,000–$10,000), with stigma lasting about **10 months**
- About **one in five auctions ends without a sale**
- **Net expected advantage of choosing auction, accounting for failure risk: +0.3%**

This is directly relevant because auction is the purest form of "let competition set the price." Even where competition is maximally visible and structurally enforced, the measured Australian premium is under 1%.

**And the sign of the auction effect is not settled anywhere in the world.** Ashenfelter & Genesove (1992) found identical New Jersey condos sold face-to-face for **13% less** than at auction; Mayer (1998) found auction **discounts** of 0–9% in 1980s Los Angeles and 9–21% in late-1980s Dallas; Quan (2002) and Chow et al. (2014) found premiums; Gan (2013) found discounts. Han & Strange's survey calls the results **"disparate"** — and adds a line worth keeping: *"a seller does not typically decide between auction and sequential search. The decision is made by the market."*

## 3.3 The Australian above-asking data measures guide-setting, not competition

**PropRadar, 19,764 matched sales with both sold price and original asking price, February–April 2026, all states:**

| Method | Mean gap | Median gap | % above asking |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Auction** (n=3,636) | **+3.84%** | +3.06% | **65%** |
| **Private treaty** (n=12,508) | **−0.70%** | −1.41% | **36%** |
| National | +0.57% | −0.41% | ~43% |
| Queensland | **+2.44%** | +1.88% | **68%** |

Two warnings. **Victoria is 65% of the sample**, so the "national" figure is largely a Victorian number. And in Australia an auction "asking price" is a **price guide** — a marketing device with a statutory floor, not a seller's stated price. "65% of auctions sold above asking" substantially measures how guides are set, not how much competition there was.

The same pattern appears internationally. **The Guardian (2024)** found the final sale price exceeded the agent's price guide in **92% of sales**, but exceeded the automated estimate in only **44%**. The gap between those two numbers is the size of the marketing convention.

**This is a genuine tension with the campaign's second pillar.** If the listing price is supposed to *signal the value we believe the home holds*, then evidence that guides are routinely set below achievable value is evidence that the industry does the opposite. Fields can turn that into a differentiator — but must not cite "% sold above guide" as proof that competition works, because it is largely proof that guides are set low.

## 3.4 Winning a bidding war is bad for the buyer

**Choi, Nowak, Smith & Tchistyi (2025 working draft)** — CoreLogic MLS, deeds and HMDA data, **14.2 million transactions, 136 counties, 30 states, 2000–2018**. Bidding-war winners earn **1.3pp lower annualised unlevered returns** on a sample mean of 5.1pp — **10.5pp lower total return over a 6.3-year hold, equivalent to overpaying by 8.2pp** — and are **1.9pp more likely to default**. Effects concentrate among *"socioeconomically vulnerable homebuyers."*

Not an argument against the campaign, but a constraint on its tone. Fields' editorial position is buyer-first. Celebrating bidding wars as a seller win sits awkwardly against evidence that the winner systematically overpays. The honest framing is that competition reveals what the market will bear — not that it extracts more than a home is worth.

## 3.5 The incentive explanation for why agents underprice anyway

**Levitt & Syverson (2008)**, *Review of Economics and Statistics* 90(4): homes owned by agents themselves sell for **3.7% more** and stay on market **9.5 days longer**. Agents capture a sliver of marginal price and the full commission on a fast sale. The Gothenburg authors reached the same conclusion independently, using the word **"self-serving"**.

This is the strongest available support for the campaign's underlying premise — that the seller and the person advising them on price want different things — and it is the reason a seller needs an independent, defensible number.

## 3.6 A note on the "signal value" pillar

The angle's second pillar — that the listing price *signals the value we believe the home holds* — is supported, but by a narrower literature than expected.

**Genesove & Hansen (2025)**, on ~4 million Australian sales, estimate that **list prices reflect only seller values**, while auction prices reflect buyers' and negotiated prices sit between. Buyers incorporate about **60% of a common market shock within a quarter and 95% within three**; sellers manage **under 15% and under 40%**. So the list price is genuinely a seller's signal — and buyers know it is, and discount it accordingly. It signals *what the seller believes*, which is only persuasive if the seller can show their working.

**Northcraft & Neale (1987)**, *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 39(1) — the classic anchoring study, run on a real Tucson property with a full MLS comparables packet — found a listing-price anchor passed through at **48% for amateurs and 41% for experienced agents** (ω² = 0.22–0.40). The memorable part: *"the decision checklists and descriptions of the expert subjects flatly denied their use of listing price."* Only **14.3%** and **8%** of experts listed it among their top three considerations. **Agents are anchored by the list price and do not know it.**

**Cardella & Seiler (2016)** is weaker than the book implies. It is a laboratory experiment with **132 subjects in 66 pairs**, and the price manipulation is only **±0.7%**. It finds a high-precise list price yields the highest final price and the largest seller surplus, and that "just below" pricing yields the **lowest** (ANOVA p=.012). Effects attenuate with negotiating experience. And **Beracha & Seiler (2014)** reaches the opposite conclusion on just-below pricing from field data. Treat precise pricing as a plausible, cheap, low-risk refinement — not as a proven several-percent lever.

> ⚠ **Correction:** the book cites Cardella & Seiler to *Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics* 52(4), 434–461. The paper is in the ***Journal of Economic Psychology* 52:71–90**. The book's citation is wrong.

Three shipped Fields artefacts give three incompatible answers to where the listing price should sit:

| Artefact | Where it puts the listing price |
|---|---|
| **Before You List**, Ch 4 | **Inside** the valuation range, near the top |

**The evidence in this document resolves it.** The Playbook's *"higher starting prices produce higher final sale prices in private treaty"* is technically true and economically trivial — it is the Bucchianeri & Minson $117–$163 effect. # Part 5 — Conclusions

**1. The campaign line is defensible, and the strongest version of it is about reach, not price.** The listing price determines who sees the home. That claim is supported by peer-reviewed viewing-arrival data, a published bracket test, Queensland's own regulation, and REA's own buyer research. It is the part of the argument no one can attack.

**2. "Not your expected selling price" should be read as "not your hoped-for price."** In private treaty the list price is *close* to the achievable price — Fields' own median gap is −2.5%, and 48% of sold homes went below their first ask. The line works as a corrective to aspiration. It fails if it implies the two numbers are loosely coupled.

**3. Drop, or heavily qualify, the "multiple people bidding" pillar.** Not because competition doesn't matter — it is the only route above list, and Merlo & Ortalo-Magné prove that categorically — but because Fields has no data on it, the measured magnitudes are small, Australian bidder counts are falling, and the tactic most associated with it (underpricing) is contradicted by 58,617 transactions across three countries and is illegal here.

**4. The real argument is transaction probability, and it is a better argument.** "Price it wrong and you might not sell at all" is supported by effects an order of magnitude larger than anything in the price-anchoring debate: −5.6% sale probability per 1% of overpricing, ~50% less likely to sell within 60 days at 12% over, 63% versus 32% sold, and ~30% of listings never selling at all with the unsold ones priced 8.1% above fair value. It is also more honest, more useful to the reader, and impossible to attack.

**5. The commercial opening is real and it is Fields' own.** Four in five local listings publish no estimate of value, in a market where 76% of buyers use price as their primary filter, 61% call it the most important factor, and 72% skip listings without one. That gap is the product.

**6. One structural warning.** The appraisal template's list-low/target-high framing should be reviewed against Queensland bait-advertising rules and the Form 6 minimum-price requirement before this campaign draws attention to how Fields sets prices.

## The message the evidence will carry

> Most buyers never reject your price. They never see it. Three-quarters of buyers skip a listing that doesn't show one, and the price you choose decides which searches you appear in at all. Set it above what the comparables support and you don't get a lower offer — you get no offer, from an audience that was never shown your home. Homes only sell above their asking price when two buyers want them at the same time, and that only happens if both of them saw it.

Every clause above is sourced. None of it recommends underpricing, promises a premium, or tells the reader what to do.
