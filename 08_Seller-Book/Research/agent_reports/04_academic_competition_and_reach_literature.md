## VERDICT ON YOUR THREE-STEP ARGUMENT

| Step | Status |
|---|---|
| **1. Bidder count is the dominant price driver** | **Strongly supported in theory, weakly supported in magnitude.** Bulow–Klemperer is exact and unambiguous. But the measured price value of an extra bidder in housing is small: doubling bidders ≈ **+2.4%**; one extra offer ≈ **+0.64%**. |
| **2. Listing price governs how many buyers see it** | **Supported, and this is the strongest link in your chain.** Peer-reviewed viewings-arrival evidence, bracket-crossing evidence, and Queensland legislation that literally names the mechanism. |
| **3. Therefore price at defensible value; inflating destroys competition** | **Supported for the anti-overpricing half. NOT supported for any "price below value" half — that is contradicted by three large studies in three countries and is illegal in Australia.** |

**The version of the argument that survives contact with the evidence is: price *at* defensible value. Not below it.** Every study that tests deliberate underpricing finds it produces a *lower* final price even when it successfully manufactures a bidding war. Reframe accordingly — see §H.

## A. BIDDER / OFFER COUNT → SALE PRICE

### A1. Bulow, J. & Klemperer, P. (1996), "Auctions Versus Negotiations," *American Economic Review* 86(1):180–194 ★VERIFIED (full JSTOR text)

**Corollary (p.189), verbatim:** *"With independent signals and risk-neutral bidders, an absolute English auction with N + 1 bidders is more profitable in expectation than any negotiation with N bidders."*

**Theorem 1 (p.187):** revenue from an absolute English auction with N+1 bidders exceeds an English auction with N bidders followed by an optimal take-it-or-leave-it offer, if either (i) values are private, or (ii) signals are affiliated.

**The exact conditions — these matter and are routinely dropped:**
- **(A.1) Downward-sloping marginal revenue** — the standard auction regularity condition.
- **(A.2) "Serious bidders":** vⱼ(T) ≥ 0 ∀j,T — every extra bidder must value the asset **at least as much as the seller does**. A tyre-kicker does not count.
- **(A.3) Symmetry** — symmetric value functions, symmetric signal distributions, symmetric strategies.
- **No reserve price** in the (N+1)-bidder auction. Bidders risk-neutral. Seller in the comparison case has **full** bargaining power and commitment.
- The authors note the corollary is **conservative**: real sellers cannot commit as firmly, nor compute the optimal reserve, as the benchmark assumes.

**The worked example (Section I, pp.182–183) — use this, it is the cleanest number in the literature:** seller value 0, buyer values ~U[0,1].
- **One bidder + optimal take-it-or-leave-it price of 0.5 → expected revenue 0.25.**
- **Two bidders, absolute English auction, no reserve → expected revenue 1/3 ≈ 0.333.**
- **One extra serious bidder is worth +33% versus perfect monopoly bargaining power.**

**Author's own strategic conclusion (p.182), verbatim:** *"a seller should generally focus on maximizing the number of bidders, and should refuse to bargain with bidders who wish to preempt the auction process."* And (p.181): *"No amount of bargaining power is as valuable to the seller as attracting one extra bidder."*

**Companion:** Bulow & Klemperer (2009), "Why Do Sellers (Usually) Prefer Auctions?", *AER* 99(4):1544–1575 — auctions are *less* efficient than sequential search but generate higher revenue **because** the inefficiency (outcome dispersion) draws entry. Exception: when buyers cannot make credible pre-emptive jump bids, sequential can beat auction.

⚠ **Scope limit:** this is a theorem about corporate/asset sales under stated assumptions. It is not a housing result. Cite it as the principle, not as measurement.

### A2. Hungria-Gunnelin, R. (2013), "Impact of Number of Bidders on Sale Price of Auctioned Condominium Apartments in Stockholm," *International Real Estate Review* 16(3):274–295 ★VERIFIED (full PDF)

- **n = 512** inner-city Stockholm condominium sales, **Jan–Nov 2010**, e-bud auction platform. Log-linear hedonic + spatial error/spatial lag models.
- **Bidder coefficient ≈ 0.038–0.039 (z = 5.27–5.47); bidder-squared ≈ −0.0018 (z = −2.6 to −2.8).**
- **Abstract, verbatim:** *"The average price per square meter paid by every extra bidder has an increasing but decelerating growth, starting with an approximate 4 percent increase when going from one to two bidders."*
- ⚠ **Author's own caveat:** no controls for building age, maintenance or refurbishment. *"It may be that high-quality apartments on average attract more bidders."* Direction of bias unknown.

### A3. Repetto, L. & Solís, A. (2020), "The Price of Inattention: Evidence from the Swedish Housing Market," *JEEA* 18(6):3261–3304 ★VERIFIED (full working-paper PDF)

**This is the single best causal link running asking price → bidder count → sale price.**

- **n = 349,476** Swedish apartment sales, **2010–2015** (Mäklarstatistik, ~90% of broker sales); **27,173** with bidder-level auction data. Mean asking 1.514m SEK; mean final **10.4% above asking**; mean **2.7 bidders**.
- **Asking price just below a round million → +0.72 more bidders and +2.7 more bids** = **+25% bidders, +30% bids**.
- **→ 3–5% higher final price**, regression-discontinuity identified.
- Asking-price distribution bunches below round millions with a **hole at and just above**; final-price distribution reverses. Around 5m SEK, **~7× more apartments transact at 5.00–5.19m than at 4.80–4.99m**.
- ⚠ **CRITICAL — the authors reject the search-filter explanation.** (i) On Hemnet an apartment listed *exactly at* a bracket boundary appears in **both** brackets, so round-number listings are if anything *more* visible. (ii) Natural experiment: Hemnet replaced a manual slider with **pre-set price brackets on 12 March 2011**; difference-in-differences (Appendix Table 18) shows the price discontinuity was present **before and after**. Their conclusion: **left-digit inattention, not brackets.** Do not merge the two mechanisms.

### A4. Genesove, D. & Han, L. (2012b), "Measuring the Thinness of Real Estate Markets," working paper — reported in Han & Strange (2015) Handbook p.34 ★VERIFIED (quote read in the Handbook PDF)

Verbatim: *"controlling for observed and unobserved heterogeneity among housing units, **doubling the number of bidders increases the sales price by 2.4%**. This effect is statistically significant."* Large North American metro, 2005–2009 survey. Spread of bidders' valuations for the same house = **4–5% of home value**. ⚠ **Working paper — not peer-reviewed.**

### A5. Merlo, A. & Ortalo-Magné, F. (2004), *Journal of Urban Economics* 56(2):192–216 ★VERIFIED (full CESifo WP 778 text)

- **n = 780** complete transaction histories, Halifax Estate Agencies, 3 Greater London branches + 1 South Yorkshire, **June 1995 – April 1998**. Every listing-price change and **every offer** recorded.
- Mean **2.26 offers** and **1.44 matches** per transaction; sale/list **95.9%**; 11 weeks to sale; 23.2% had a price change.
- **Table 21 (sale price, £):** **NMATCH coefficient = +£430 (SE 153)** — each additional buyer who makes an offer adds **£430** to sale price, on a mean of £66,964 = **+0.64% per additional competing buyer**. WTSALE = **−£72/week** (SE 13). ILISTP = 0.942 (SE 0.007).
- ★ **The purest statement of your mechanism in a private-treaty market (p.6, verbatim):** *"we detect the occurrence of about 30 de facto auctions out of 780 transactions… such de facto auctions account for **all** instances in the data where the sale price is above the listing price."* **In a no-auction market, selling above list happens only when two or more buyers bid simultaneously. Full stop.**

### A6. Han, L. & Strange, W.C. (2014), "Bidding Wars for Houses," *Real Estate Economics* 42(1):1–32 ★VERIFIED (full WP text)

- **92,700** NAR micro survey responses aggregated to **334 MSAs**, 15 surveys **1987–2010**. Response rates never exceed 19%.
- Bidding-war share: **~3.5% in 1986** (buyer 2.94%, seller 4.80%); 3–4% through 1995; **12.46% (2003) – 15.52% (2005)**; **13.78% in 2003–06 vs 8.70% in 2007–10**. Metro peaks: DC ~29.1%, LA ~26%, Las Vegas ~23%, Norfolk 22.64%, Houston 11.43%, New York 8.62%.
- ★ **Bidding-war premium over list: 10.01% (2003–06 boom) and 17.89% (2007–10 bust).**
- ★ **Their key inference (p.12), verbatim:** *"The bust is identified by a decrease in sales price. That bidding wars showed a higher premium during the bust must therefore require that **sellers have chosen lower list prices**."*
- Time on market, seller sample: bidding-war sales **5.84 weeks (boom) / 9.46 (bust)** vs below-list **11.84 / 16.20**. Boom medians: 2 weeks vs 6; 75th percentile 6 vs 15 weeks.
- ⚠ **This paper estimates no causal price effect and does not test whether low list prices cause bidding wars.** The authors call the underprice→war→high-price idea **"folklore"** (Handbook p.33, verbatim: *"popular discussions of bidding wars, with folklore suggesting that a low listing price to bring people to the table can result in a high sales price as bidders throw caution to the wind"*).

### A7. Australia — bidder count → price is **unmeasured**

- **Genesove & Hansen state outright: *"We have no data on the number of bidders at individual auctions."*** That is a ~4-million-transaction census of Sydney + Melbourne, 1993–2016. If the best-resourced study of Australian housing auctions could not get bidder counts, **the honest position is that this relationship has never been estimated in Australia.**
- **Ray White** (the only ongoing AU bidder series, >64,000 campaigns since 2022, ~1 in 4 of all Australian auctions): registered/active bidders **8.4 / 4.1 (Sep 2021 peak)** → **4.7 / 3.1 (Jun 2025)** → **4.0 / 2.7 (2025 YTD)** → **~3.1 / 2.2 (2026)**. Ray White NSW/ACT July 2026: **2.7 registered, −44% YoY**, 68% clearance, **53% of single-bidder auctions still sold under the hammer**. ⚠ 2026 weeklies unverified to raywhite.com (site returned empty bodies).
- **Domain, 29 May 2026** ★VERIFIED: Brisbane ~2.5 registered bidders (down from 5 earlier in the year); national ~2 active (vs ~3 a year prior); open-home attendance 2.1 groups vs 3.5.
- ⚠ **Nerida Conisbee's "fewer bidders ⇒ falling prices" claim is contradicted by her own Sept 2024 data** — Sydney and Melbourne both had fewer active bidders *and* +0.2% price growth.

## B. SEARCH-AND-MATCHING: HOW BUYER TRAFFIC BECOMES PRICE

### B1. Genesove, D. & Han, L. (2012), "Search and matching in the housing market," *JUE* 72(1):31–45 ★VERIFIED (full text)

- NAR buyer/seller surveys aggregated to MSA-year, **1987–2007/08**. Buyer sample **n = 2,372** MSA-years; seller **1,894**; joint **1,636**.
- ★ **First estimate of the housing matching function: elasticity of the seller contact hazard w.r.t. the buyer-seller ratio = 0.84** (constant returns assumed) — i.e. **near-proportional**: more buyers per seller means proportionally more contacts for the seller.
- Weighted-average median seller TOM 7.3 weeks; buyer TOM 8.2 weeks; **homes visited per buyer 9.9** (mean 9.96, SD 4.34).
- Semi-elasticity of seller TOM to income growth ≈ **7**; to population growth ≈ **14.5**. Short-run NHVIS elasticities −4.5 (population), −1.2 (income).
- ⚠ Identifies **demand shocks** → fewer visits per buyer. **Not** a list-price elasticity.

### B2. Han, L. & Strange, W.C. (2015), "The Microstructure of Housing Markets: Search, Bargaining, and Brokerage," *Handbook of Regional and Urban Economics* Vol 5B, ch.13, pp.813–886 ★VERIFIED (full text)

The definitive survey. Key verbatim passages:
- On the empirical result: ***"a lower asking price increases the number of bidders on a house (a subset of the number of visitors)"***; *"asking price has a stronger negative relationship with search activity in a bust than in a boom."*
- On the state of theory — **explicitly open**: *"how can one rationalize in a fully specified equilibrium model how asking price can direct search even though it is neither a posted price nor a ceiling?"*
- On the empirical constraint: *"data sources used in empirical research nearly always provide data on outcomes… rather than on the search process itself."*
- On the auction literature: results are ***"disparate"*** (Lusht premium, Quan premium, Mayer discount, Chow conditional).
- ★ On agency: ***"a seller does not typically decide between auction and sequential search. The decision is made by the market."***

### B3. Genesove, D. & Hansen, J. (2025), "Auctions and Negotiations in Housing Price Dynamics," *Review of Economics and Statistics* 107(4):1074–1085 ★VERIFIED (full 2019 draft)

**The best Australian macro-evidence, and directly relevant to why the list price is *not* a market signal.**
- **Census of all Sydney + Melbourne sales 1993:Q1–2016:Q4, ~4 million transactions** (price recorded for all but 0.2%); attributes for 35%. Sydney + Melbourne ≈ 40% of Australian transaction volume.
- ★ **Estimated weights: auction prices weight buyer values more heavily than seller values; negotiated prices are close to an equally weighted average of buyer and seller values; *list prices only reflect seller values*.**
- Theory statement, verbatim: *"Absent seller reserves, auction prices are solely determined by the distribution of buyers' values; even with seller reserves, theory predicts the weight on the seller's value to approach zero as the number of bidders increases, for private values."* Their calibration puts the threshold at **~six bidders**.
- Buyers incorporate **~60% of a common shock within a quarter, 95% within three**; sellers **<15%** and **<40%** respectively.
- Footnote 5: *"'Negotiated' prices that follow (informal) bidding wars are best interpreted as auctions that have been misclassified as negotiations"* — i.e. they **understate** the auction–negotiation gap.

### B4. Merlo, Ortalo-Magné & Rust (2015), "The Home Selling Problem: Theory and Evidence," *International Economic Review* 56(2):457–484 — ⚠ **contradicts the elasticity premise**

Verbatim from the working paper: *"the estimated relationship between the list price and the expected rate of arrival of potential buyers is **relatively inelastic**. More precisely, relatively small adjustments in the list price hardly affect the expected sale probability while impacting the expected sale price."* A menu cost of **<0.006% of house value** (~£12 on £200,000) explains observed list-price stickiness — precisely *because* the arrival response is weak.

⚠ **This directly conflicts with Vandenbergh's −6.7% elasticity (§C2). Flagging the conflict rather than resolving it: the two papers use different countries, decades, and outcome variables (offers vs portal clicks).**

## C. LISTING PRICE → BUYER TRAFFIC / REACH

### C1. Merlo & Ortalo-Magné (2004) — the canonical viewings result ★VERIFIED

- **Viewings sub-sample n = 199** properties, Greater London. **Mean 9.54 viewings per sale** (median 7, max 51); **1.74 viewings per week on market**.
- **Table 18, Poisson on viewings per week, exposure-adjusted:** initial listing price coefficient **−0.395 (SE 0.154)**, significant at 5%. Pseudo-R² 0.17.
- ★ **Authors' conclusion, verbatim:** *"a lower listing price increases the arrival rate of viewings, which in turn increases the arrival rate of offers. Thus, **the listing price affects the arrival of offers indirectly, by affecting the arrival of viewings**."*
- Viewing arrival decays **monotonically** from listing with no discrete drop after week 1–2 → **there is no stock of waiting buyers; interest is a flow.**
- ★★ **The bracket test (§4.1, Table 19) — the strongest peer-reviewed support for your step 2:** replacing a generic price-change dummy with SAMESEG vs LOWERSEG: **a price cut that moves the property into a lower £5,000 segment significantly raises the probability of first-offer arrival; a cut that leaves it in the same segment has no effect.** Robust to including the size of the reduction (itself insignificant), and holds in Poisson regressions on the arrival rate of offers *and* viewings. **About half of all initial listing prices in the sample sit within £50 of a £5,000 segment boundary.**
- Their stated mechanism, verbatim: *"potential buyers restrict their search to properties whose listing price is below a certain amount… The marginal cost (in terms of a decrease of the arrival rate of potential buyers) of raising her listing price up to the nearest mass point is zero."*
- ⚠ Same paper: **no** relation between viewings and probability of a *sale agreement*. Traffic predicts offers, not completions.

### C2. Vandenbergh, J. (2024), "Seller and search behavior in the Belgian housing market," *Journal of Housing Economics* 64:101997 — abstract-verified only

Immoweb.be, **daily ad tracking July 2022 – July 2023**, Poisson with **property fixed effects** (identified off within-listing price changes). **A 1% increase in listing price reduces search activity for that property by 6.7% (sales) / 6.6% (rentals).** Elasticity **rises with the number of competing listings in the segment** — the reach penalty is worse in a well-supplied market. ⚠ Full text paywalled; listing count not verified.

### C3. Guren, A.M. (2018), "House Price Momentum and Strategic Complementarity," *JPE* 126(3):1172–1218 ★VERIFIED (author's PDF)

- Altos Research weekly MLS × DataQuick; **SF Bay Area, LA, San Diego, Apr 2008 – Feb 2013**. **663,976 listings → 480,258 transactions**; IV sample 416,373 → 310,758. First-stage joint F = 206.
- **IV, Table 2:** **+1% list price → −2.7pp probability of sale within 13 weeks** (base 48pp) = **−5.6% relative**. **+5% list price → −21.5pp = −45%.**
- ★ **Demand is strongly concave: overpricing destroys sale probability fast; underpricing barely helps.**
- Guren's own first-listed mechanism: *"buyers may avoid visiting homes that appear to be overpriced."*
- ⚠ OLS is badly biased by unobserved quality — do not use the OLS shape.

### C4. Han & Strange (2016), "What is the role of the asking price for a house?", *JUE* 93:115–130 ★VERIFIED (full 14-Aug-2014 working paper)

**The only direct measurement of asking price → number of bidders.**
- Survey of homebuyers in a large North American metro; **3,193 valid interviews (1,722 mail + 1,467 phone), 19.2% response**, matched to **89,891 MLS transactions, 2006–2009**. **Over one third of surveyed buyers reported facing competing bidders.**
- **Table 4 (log-linear, bidders on asking price):**
  - Col. 6, district + period + attribute controls: **−0.22** → **10% lower ask = +2.2% bidders**
  - With tax-assessment controls (proxy for unobserved quality): **−0.40** → **10% lower ask = +4% bidders**
- **Table 5:** −0.24 with crisis interaction −0.16 → **+2.4% in normal times, +4% in busts**. Atypicality interaction −0.018 → **+7.8% at the 10th percentile of atypicality, +69% at the 90th**.
- ⚠⚠ **The single most important honesty point in this whole brief: the raw relationship is POSITIVE.** Columns 1–3 (no district dummies) give **+0.08, +0.09, +0.14** — higher asking price, *more* bidders. **It only flips negative once you control for location.** The authors call their estimate a **lower bound** (bidders proxy visitors; measurement error; residual unobserved quality). But anyone can run the naive regression and get the opposite sign.
- **Theory caveat from their own conclusion, verbatim:** *"A lower asking price is shown to encourage more potential buyers to visit, **but only up to a point**. Past this bound, a lower asking price leads to more bidding wars, and buyer recognition of this means that more cannot be encouraged to search."*
- ⚠ A **sign typo** on p.3 of the working paper prose ("a lower asking price reduces the number of bidders") contradicts its own tables, the published abstract, and the Handbook. The tables are correct.

### C10. Transaction-tax notch bunching — hard proof that markets cluster at price thresholds

- **Best, M.C. & Kleven, H.J. (2018), *Review of Economic Studies* 85(1):157–193** ★VERIFIED (Sept 2016 WP). Universe of UK property transactions **2004–2012, ~10m**. At the lower notch, effect on average transacted value **£3,500–£5,000 = 4–5× the jump**.
- **Kopczuk, W. & Munroe, D. (2015), *AEJ: Economic Policy* 7(2):214–257** ★VERIFIED. NYC **380,629** taxable sales 2003–2011; NY State 1,172,708; NJ 1,703,260. Crucially, on **71,875 REBNY Manhattan listings (2003–2010)** they find bunching **in asking prices** (median initial ask **$899,000**) — the distortion is present when the property is *first advertised*.

## E. THE COST OF AN INFLATED ASKING PRICE, IN LOST BUYERS

Ranked by strength:

1. **Merlo & Ortalo-Magné (2004):** listing price coefficient **−0.395 (SE 0.154)** on the weekly viewing arrival rate; and a cut into a **lower price segment** raises first-offer probability while a same-segment cut does **nothing**. *Peer-reviewed, primary-verified.*
2. **Han & Strange (2016):** asking-price elasticity of **bidder count −0.22 to −0.40** (−0.24 normal / −0.40 bust; up to −0.69 for highly atypical homes). *Peer-reviewed. Small. Raw sign is positive before location controls.*
3. **Guren (2018):** **+1% list price → −2.7pp (−5.6% relative) probability of sale in 13 weeks; +5% → −21.5pp (−45%)**, IV-identified, concave. *Peer-reviewed, primary-verified.*
4. **Vandenbergh (2024):** **−6.7% portal search activity per +1% list price**, property fixed effects. *Peer-reviewed, abstract-verified. Conflicts with Merlo/Ortalo-Magné/Rust's "relatively inelastic".*
5. **Zillow (2015):** homes priced **≥12% above estimated market value are ~50% less likely to sell within 60 days**; first-week page views separate a 12% from a 36% 60-day sale probability. *Industry, correlational, no controls.*
6. **Seiler & Siebert (2025), *Real Estate Economics*, DOI 10.1111/1540-6229.70024:** **~30% of US listings never sell**, and unsold listings are priced **8.1% above fair market value**; overpriced listings raise neighbours' list prices by **$39,086 (5.3%)** and sale prices by **$35,688 (5%)**. **The real cost of overpricing is not a price haircut — it is failure to transact at all.**
7. **Rightmove (2019/2023):** correctly priced from day one → **vendor 40% more likely to sell through that agent**; properties needing a reduction are **>10% less likely to find a buyer**; **63% vs 32%** sold, **21 vs 47 days** (>300,000 listings, 2020). *Industry, no published methodology.*
8. **Redfin (2019):** **3.4× more views on day 1 than on the day of a price drop**; a price cut buys **one day** of elevated views. **You get one launch. Reach is a flow, not a stock.**
9. **Realtor.com (11 June 2026, US):** homes closing in **week 4** sell **1.8pp above** the monthly average for comparables; **week 18** closings **1.3pp below** — a **>3pp spread**. Price-reduction peak shifted from week 3 (2021) to **week 6** (2026).

### F1. Three large peer-reviewed studies, three countries, all find underpricing *lowers* the final price — including where it demonstrably *does* create bidding wars

**⚠ My own methodological objection to F1, which you should also know:** DOP is constructed as (list − model-predicted value)/predicted value. The list price contains information about true value the hedonic/ML model misses, so DOP absorbs the model's prediction error and regressing sale price on DOP partly regresses price on price. **The tell is Kopsch et al.'s R² jumping from 0.80 to 0.97 on adding DOP** — the authors concede DOP *"does contain information about estimated prices."* Han & Strange's design (outcome = **number of bidders**, not price) is immune to this and is the cleanest test in the literature. But three independent teams reaching the same sign in three countries is not dismissible on one objection.

### F2. Australia: the auction premium is ~1/15th of what the industry advertises

**Cortés, K.R. & Singh, M. (2026), "How Perception Affects House Prices: Evidence from Failed Auctions," *Review of Finance*.** **Over 480,000 residential transactions, NSW + VIC, Jan 2007 – Dec 2019.**
- **Successful auction premium: +0.7% (≈$2,000–$2,500).**
- **Properties that sold after failing at auction: −1.3% (≈$9,000–$10,000)** vs the private-treaty counterfactual; **stigma lasts ~10 months**. ~1 in 5 auctions end without a sale.
- ★ **Net expected advantage of choosing auction, accounting for failure risk: +0.3%.**

Compare: **Frino, Lepone, Mollica & Vassallo (2010)**, *AABFJ* 4(3) ★VERIFIED (full PDF, RP Data, five capitals, **>536,000 transactions, Jan 2005 – Jun 2009**): `ListType` coefficients for houses — Melbourne 0.1223 (t=12.37), Sydney 0.0153 (2.39), Brisbane 0.0678 (7.16), Perth 0.1778 (9.15), Adelaide 0.2425 (37.31); auction premium survives a two-stage Heckman self-selection correction (all λ significant). **For units, significant only in Perth and Adelaide.** Median TOM: auction ~25 days vs private treaty ~37. ⚠ **Do not quote those percentages as "the auction premium"** — the model includes `ListType × SSD` interactions (−0.23 to +0.26), so each coefficient is the effect in that city's reference sub-division only. **Lusht (1996)**, *Real Estate Economics* 24(4):517–530 — Melbourne, **163 auction vs 58 private-treaty sales, Jan 1988–Mar 1989**, premium **AUD $19,326 (~6.5–8%)**; sample predates the dummy-bidding ban.

**The sign of the auction effect is not settled anywhere.** Ashenfelter & Genesove (1992) *AER* 82(2):501–505: 83 NJ condos, 2,348 registered bidders — identical units later sold face-to-face for **13% less**. **Mayer (1998)** *Real Estate Economics* 26(1):41–66 ★VERIFIED (Boston Fed WP 93-1): auction **DISCOUNTS** of **0–9% (LA, mid-1980s boom)** and **9–21% (Dallas, late-1980s bust)**. Quan (2002): premium. Chow et al. (2014): premium larger for homogeneous properties and in booms. **Gan (2013)** *REE* 41(2):347–383: positive auction *discounts*.

| Case | 10% cut in asking price → | → price gain via competition |
|---|---|---|
| Typical house, normal market | +2.4% bidders | **+0.08%** |
| Typical house, bust, tax-controlled | +4% bidders | **+0.13%** |

### F5. Why agents recommend underpricing anyway

**Levitt, S. & Syverson, C. (2008), "Market Distortions When Agents Are Better Informed," *Review of Economics and Statistics* 90(4):599–611.** **Homes owned by agents themselves sell for 3.7% more and stay on the market 9.5 days longer.** Agents capture a sliver of marginal price and the full commission on a fast sale. Hungria-Gunnelin et al. reach the same conclusion independently ("self-serving").

### F6. The Australian legal wall

**Underquoting is illegal, and it is illegal in every jurisdiction that has studied it at scale.**
- **Victoria:** Estate Agents Act 1980 (Vic) **s47C** — *"You must not market a property for sale at a lower price than your estimated selling price."* Max **200 penalty units = $41,820** at the 2026–27 rate ($209.10/unit). Advertised price may not fall below an estimate derived from the **three most comparable properties** (s47C(2)); s47A requires a written estimate before appointment.
- **Queensland:** the governing concept is **bait advertising** — *"Giving buyers a false impression about the price a seller will accept is known as 'bait advertising'"* (Qld Government, Property advertising). An **"offers over" price must reflect the vendor's genuine acceptance threshold**; setting one the vendor will not accept **is false advertising**. Minimum selling price is recorded on the **Form 6**. POA 2014 **s212**: false representations, max **540 penalty units**.
- **Australian Consumer Law:** misleading conduct on price — corporations up to **$100m / 3× benefit / 30% of turnover**, whichever is greater; individuals up to **$2.5m** per breach.
- **NSW:** Property and Stock Agents Act 2002. Enforcement datapoint: a NSW Fair Trading blitz found **57% of 87 agents inspected non-compliant** with underquoting rules. ⚠ Section numbers, commission-forfeiture and the reported $110,000 penalty **not verified to primary legislation.**
- **Norway:** *lokkepris* illegal under the Marketing Act for over a decade (Finanstilsynet fines to NOK 2m individuals / NOK 5m or 5% of turnover for firms). **Sweden:** *lockpriser* actionable via Fastighetsmäklarinspektionen; the Board of Supervision of Estate Agents *"considers the use of underpricing a serious problem and not lawful"* (Hungria-Gunnelin 2013, p.276).

### F7. Other contradictions worth knowing

- **Repetto & Solís explicitly reject the search-bracket mechanism** for the just-below premium (§A3). Bracket-reach (Merlo, Rightmove) and left-digit inattention are **two distinct mechanisms** — do not merge them into one claim.
- **Merlo & Ortalo-Magné** find **no** relation between viewings and probability of a *sale agreement*.
- **Zillow (2016):** no speed bonus for underpricing, only a penalty for overpricing.
- **Han & Strange's own raw regression is positive** — higher asking price, more bidders — until district fixed effects are added.
- **Pilat, C. & Leishman, C. (2025)**, *IJHMA* 18(7):66–86, **~55,000 South Australian private-treaty transactions 2018–2024** — extreme undercuts sold in a median **88 days vs 125**, and **>60% sold above initial list price**. ⚠ **It models agent strategy choice and TOM. It does NOT estimate the effect of underpricing on final sale price.** Its stated contribution is explaining *why agents underprice*, not that it pays the seller. ## H. THE VERSION OF THE MESSAGE THE EVIDENCE WILL CARRY

**Do not say:** "price low to start a bidding war." It is contradicted by n=11,658 (Sweden), n=31,671 (Iceland) and n=15,288 (Norway); it is called **folklore** by the authors of the field's standard survey; and in Australia it is **illegal** under Vic s47C, Qld bait-advertising/s212 and ACL s18/s30.

**The claims that survive scrutiny, in descending order of defensibility:**

1. **"Above-list sales happen only when two or more buyers bid at once."** Merlo & Ortalo-Magné: **all 30 above-list sales out of 780** were de facto auctions. Peer-reviewed, private-treaty, no auction market required.
2. **"Competition, not negotiation, sets the price."** Bulow & Klemperer's exact result plus Genesove & Hansen's Australian estimate that **list prices reflect only seller values while auction prices reflect buyers'** — 4 million Sydney/Melbourne sales.
3. **"Your price decides who ever sees the home."** Merlo & Ortalo-Magné's bracket test (a cut *into a lower band* moves offers; a cut *within* a band does nothing), plus **Queensland's own Regulation s10** conceding the portal filters listings into price brackets, plus REA's **72% of buyers skip a no-price listing.**
4. **"Overpricing costs you buyers, and you only get one launch."** Guren's **+1% → −5.6% sale probability**; Zillow's **≥12% over estimate → ~50% less likely to sell in 60 days**; Redfin's **3.4× day-one views, and a price cut buys exactly one day back.**
5. **"The real cost of overpricing is not a discount — it is not selling."** Seiler & Siebert: **~30% of US listings never sell, and unsold listings are priced 8.1% above fair market value.**

**The honest synthesis:** under every credible estimate, *both* price channels — anchoring upward and competition downward — are small (0.05% to ~2%). **The large, robust, unanimous effects are on probability of sale and time on market.** That is the claim to build the message on, and it happens to be the one that is both legal and true.
