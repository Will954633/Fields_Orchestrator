# Is There a Proven Way to Get a Higher Price for Your Home?

**A synopsis of all research, evidence and commentary on that question in *Before You List* (Will Simpson, Fields Real Estate, v4, April 2026, ~24,500 words)**

Source read: `output/seller_book_draft_v4.md` (1,615 lines) — verified against the published `08_Seller-Book/Latest_Copy_26th-May-2026/Fields_book_digital.pdf`; text matches.

**Structure:** §1–§7 are the book as written — every claim, figure and citation it makes on this question. **§8 is what Fields' own audit files have since found**, including four headline claims that have been revised downward and one figure with no underlying analysis. Read §8 before reusing any Fields-sourced number from §1–§7.

---

## 1. The book's answer, in its own words

The book never claims a single silver bullet. Its thesis is a **mechanism**, stated twice and then unpacked across eight chapters:

> "The highest sale price comes from the highest number of emotionally invested buyers competing at the same time. Everything in this book exists to create that outcome."
> — *Houses Sell Themselves*

> "When multiple buyers want the same property, they stop negotiating against the seller and start negotiating against each other… The seller's role is not to negotiate harder — it is to create the conditions where buyers compete."
> — Ch 4, *The Principle Behind All of This*

Everything the book presents as a price lever is justified as a way of either (a) increasing the number of qualified buyers who see the property, or (b) concentrating them into the same narrow window, or (c) raising each one's emotional investment before they make an offer. Marketing is framed identically: "The goal is to concentrate as many qualified buyers as possible into the same narrow window of time" (Ch 7).

The framing on premium is explicit and worth quoting because it defines what "higher price" means in the book:

> "The valuation tells you what the market considers a fair price. A premium sale price sits above that figure… The goal is not to accept the valuation as the ceiling. It is to use it as the foundation from which a higher price can be built." — Ch 1

The claimed size of the whole opportunity, stated up front: **$50,000–$120,000** on a typical southern Gold Coast home, described as the gap between "an adequate campaign and an excellent one." Elsewhere the same gap is given as **$50,000–$100,000** for discount-listed homes vs. properly campaigned equivalents. (These two ranges are stated within seven paragraphs of each other and are not reconciled.)

## 2. The levers the book says are proven — graded by evidence strength

### Tier A — External, peer-reviewed, large-sample

#### A1. Price accurately on day one (the single best-evidenced lever)

This is the most heavily cited claim in the book, supported by six independent studies spanning 25 years:

| Study | Sample | Finding as stated in the book |
|---|---|---|
| **Taylor (1999)**, *Review of Economic Studies* 66(3), 555–578 | "thousands of transactions" | Properties listed >10% above market value took **2–5× longer** to sell. Overpricing >10% "universally destructive"; establishes the stigma effect |
| **Knight (2002)**, *J. Real Estate Finance & Economics* 24(1-2), 93–119 | — | Overpriced homes that later sold after reductions achieved **lower final prices** than homes correctly priced from the start. The reduction itself became a negative signal |
| **Anglin, Rutherford & Springer (2003)**, *JREFE* 26(1), 95–111 | — | Sellers overpricing by >10% were significantly more likely to take **multiple** reductions, each eroding confidence — "self-reinforcing downward spiral" |
| **Khezr (2015)**, *Applied Economics* 47(29), 3049–3060 | 25,000+ Sydney sales | Overpriced homes take longer to sell and ultimately sell for less |
| **Zillow Research (2019)** | 25,000 sales | Homes overpriced by **12%+ were ~50% less likely to sell within 60 days**; homes lingering >2 months sell for **5% less** |
| **Nikiforou et al. (2022)** | 538 transactions | Optimal degree of overpricing in transparent markets is **~1.5%** — i.e. a small, not large, premium |

**Stated mechanism:** a buyer in the $1.25M–$1.4M Robina range has 15–20 options on a Saturday and time to inspect four or five. An overpriced listing "is invisible to its natural buyer pool and uncompetitive in the pool it's been placed into."

**The book's own worked case (Ch 4 opening):** a Federal Place, Robina home, comparables $1,280,000–$1,320,000, listed $1,450,000. Six weeks of nothing → reduced to $1,380,000 at week 6 → sold **$1,290,000 after 97 days**. Bottom of the comparable range. The book estimates the overpricing cost **$20,000–$30,000** against a correct day-one price.

**The core distinction the book draws:** *"The asking price is a marketing tool. The sale price is the outcome. They are not the same thing."*

#### A2. Don't underprice either (private treaty ≠ auction)

- **Bucchianeri & Minson (2013)**, *J. Economic Behavior & Organization* 89, 76–92 — **14,000 transactions**. Quoted directly: *"Pricing the house too low may result in a quick sale but at a price lower than might have been achieved with a more selective pool of buyers."*
- Mechanism given: in private treaty there is no public forum where buyers compete in real time. A buyer seeing an attractive price offers *at or below* it. "The anchor works in the buyer's favour, not the seller's."
- Stated exception: extreme undersupply — dismissed as "temporary and unpredictable… like planning your retirement around winning the lottery."
- **Conclusion:** "Not high. Not low. Right."

> **Resolved by the underlying research file.** Bucchianeri & Minson's headline finding is an *anchoring* result — higher list prices do anchor higher sale prices — which looks like it contradicts A1. The Fields Positioning Playbook v5.0 resolves it: the anchoring benefit is real but **tiny — overpricing by 10–20% yields only 0.05–0.07% additional sale price, which does not come close to compensating for the time-on-market penalty.** The book uses the conclusion without showing this working.

> **The number that actually answers the question.** **Nikiforou, Dimopoulos & Sivitanides (2022)**, *Journal of European Real Estate Research* — 538 transactions, Cyprus — is the most precise "how much above" figure in the entire evidence base: in **transparent markets where sold prices are published (i.e. Australia), the optimal Degree of Overpricing is ~1.5%**, and each 1% increase in DOP raises the probability of selling within 30 days by 1.23%. The book lists this study in its references but never uses the 1.5% figure in the text.

#### A3. Precise pricing above a round number

**Cardella & Seiler (2016)**, *J. Real Estate Finance and Economics* 52(4), 434–461 — the book's most operationally specific research claim. Four conditions, all of which must be met:

1. **Precise, not round** — $1,315,000, not $1,300,000. Round signals approximation; precise signals knowledge.
2. **Above the round number, not below** — $1,315,000, not $1,285,000. A precise price *below* a round number "reads as weakness."
3. **Small gap** — $1,315,000 works, $1,387,000 doesn't. Precision must feel deliberate, not arbitrary.
4. **The property must present at the upper end of its comparable range** — precise pricing above round only works when marketing materials are professional and first impression supports a premium.

**Claimed effect when all four are met:** buyers anchor more strongly to the precise number, negotiate less aggressively, and final sale prices are higher — "the equivalent of several percent of the sale price."

Fields' corroboration is stated qualitatively only: Robina properties listed at precise-above-round prices "show a measurably tighter spread between listing price and sale price." **No Fields figure is given** for this.

The Positioning Playbook v5.0 calls Cardella & Seiler "**the single most actionable academic finding in this entire playbook**" and notes the effect **persists even among real estate professionals — expertise does not eliminate the anchoring bias**. Their four experimental conditions were Rounded $200,000 / Just Below $199,000 / High Precise $201,326 / Low Precise $198,674.

> ⚠ Two things the book does not surface. **(a) The citation looks wrong** — the book gives *Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics* 52(4), 434–461; the underlying research file places this paper in the *Journal of Economic Psychology*. Verify before reuse. **(b) A conflicting peer-reviewed result exists.** **Beracha & Seiler (2014)**, *JREFE*, found "just below" pricing ($999,000) attracts the *largest* buyer-negotiated discount but, because it embeds a higher starting overprice, nets **+2.5–3% higher sale prices** than round pricing. The playbook flags this as directly conflicting with Cardella & Seiler and resolves it as "format vs position." The book presents only one side.

#### A4. Method of sale — auction has no proven price advantage (in this market)

- **University of Sydney (multiple authors), with the Reserve Bank — 1.2 million residential sales.** Finding: **no statistically significant difference in sale price between auction and private treaty**, controlling for property type, location and market conditions. The book calls this "the largest study ever conducted" on the question and uses it to directly contradict the standard agent claim that auction "creates competition which drives the price up."

  > ⚠ **Now identified.** The underlying source is **Wright, D. (2010), "Heterogeneous Asset Pricing: An Examination of the Australian Residential Real Estate Market", PhD dissertation, Discipline of Finance, University of Sydney** — a single-author doctoral thesis, not a multi-author RBA partnership. The "Reserve Bank" association appears to come from Wright *citing* an RBA estimate (that ~65% of average Australian household wealth sits in the home), not from RBA co-authorship. The **1.2 million** sample figure appears only in an internal note (`DATA_SOURCES_NOTE.md`), **not in the reference file that catalogues the thesis**, and is not among the 16 page-referenced quotes extracted from it. The book's single strongest structural claim should not be republished in its current form until the sample size and the auction/private-treaty result are read directly out of the thesis.
- **Cost asymmetry:** GC auction campaigns typically $5,000–$10,000 marketing vs. $2,000–$5,000 private treaty. No price benefit + $3,000–$5,000 extra cost + a smaller buyer pool.
- **72% of buyers scroll past listings with no displayed price guide** — attributed to REA Group (2014) internal platform data, referenced in Queensland Parliament *Property Occupations Act 2014* review submissions.
- **Queensland-specific amplifier:** QLD law prohibits price guides at auction (unlike NSW). Buyers see "Auction" and nothing. Also removes the agent's ability to advocate: *"The agent can advocate for the seller's price because there is a price to advocate for."*
- **Behavioural underpinning — ambiguity aversion:**
  - **Ellsberg (1961)**, *QJE* 75(4), 643–669 — foundational ambiguity aversion paper
  - **Fox & Tversky (1995)**, *QJE* 110(3), 585–603 — aversion increases as stakes rise; property is the highest-stakes purchase most people make
  - **Gneezy, List & Wu (2006)**, *QJE* 121(4), 1283–1309 — uncertain prospects valued *below the worst guaranteed outcome*
  - **Frisch & Baron (1988)**, *J. Behavioral Decision Making* 1(3), 149–157 — people avoid options where information is missing
- **Local mix:** of 2,786 currently listed southern GC properties, **8% auction / 92% private treaty** (Fields analysis). Appendix B adds that GC auctions are "below 2% of total sales."
- **When auction *can* work** (the book's own counterweight): genuinely unique properties with no comparables; properties with exceptional emotional appeal ("perhaps one in a hundred listings"); deceased estates and court-ordered sales.
- **Appendix B — auction clearance rates are not a usable signal.** Deakin University research identifies four structural flaws: (1) selective agent-submitted reporting → systematic upward bias; (2) post-auction private sales counted as "sold at auction"; (3) withdrawn properties excluded; (4) self-selected sample tells you nothing about the private-treaty market where most homes sell.

#### A5. Agent incentives are structurally misaligned with yours

**Levitt & Syverson (2008)**, *Review of Economics and Statistics* 90(4), 599–611 — 98,000 transactions (per References; Ch 4 says "thousands… in the Chicago market"). When agents sell **their own** homes they hold out for **3.7% higher prices** and keep them on market **~10 days longer** (References say 9.5 days).

The arithmetic the book runs: 3.7% of $1,400,000 = **$51,800**. To the agent on 2.5% commission, that extra $52,000 is worth ~**$1,300** — not worth ten more days of open homes and follow-ups. To the seller it is worth $52,000.

> "This isn't an indictment of every agent… But the structural incentive is real."

**The practical instruction derived from it:** price on comparable-sales data, not on an agent's recommendation alone — "any agent who resists that verification is telling you something about their confidence in their own number."

Supporting industry source: **Consumer Action Law Centre (Stephanie Tonkin)** — agents have an incentive to tell owners what they want to hear in order to win the listing. The book names this the "buy the listing" playbook: inflate the estimate to win, then pressure the seller down once it fails to attract offers.

#### A6. Virtual tours — a real but shrinking effect

**Soleymanian & Qian (2024)**, NBER Working Paper No. 33204 — **75,000 home sales, Los Angeles**. Virtual tours increase sale prices by **~1%**, but the differential advantage has narrowed as tours moved from novelty to expectation.

The book's honest reading: *"Virtual tours are now table-stakes: not having one hurts more than having one helps."*

#### A7. Speed and the first-two-week window

**Lusht & Rossini (2010)** — 4,500+ sales. The **majority of homes sell within the first two weeks; 10% sell in nine days or fewer.**

The book converts this into its strongest campaign-management claim:

> "The first seven to ten days of a campaign are the highest-leverage period in the entire selling process… The first-week audience arrives all at once." — Ch 7

And into a counter-intuitive seller instruction (Ch 4, *Early Offers*): **an early offer is a signal of correct pricing, not of underpricing.** The book describes the common failure: seller gets a day-10 offer, rejects it believing they priced too low, no higher offer materialises, the property sits, takes a reduction, and sells below the rejected offer. *"The seller's instinct to protect themselves against undervaluing their home actually produced the outcome they feared most."*

### Tier B — Industry / platform data (real numbers, thin sourcing)

#### B1. Professional photography

- **118% more online views** for listings with professional photography — sourced only to "Various (buyer behaviour studies)."
- **Virtual tours: 77 seconds of engagement vs 44 seconds for static images** (a 75% increase) — same unattributed source.
- The book's structural argument (Ch 5, Layer 1): a buyer decides to click or scroll in **~3 seconds** from thumbnail, price and address alone. "If the lead image doesn't stop them, the description is never read… The $45,000 kitchen renovation is invisible."

#### B2. Portal tier and portal presence

**PropTrack / realestate.com.au:**

| Metric | Premiere Plus vs Standard |
|---|---|
| Email enquiries | 2.6× more |
| Listing views | 2.9× more |
| Search appearances | 2.1× more |
| Speed of sale | 10 days faster |

- **REA receives 3.6× more traffic than Domain.**
- **PropTrack (Brisbane): properties sold off-market sell for 3.6% less** than those listed on REA — ~$50,000 on a $1,400,000 home.

#### B3. Reaching passive buyers

- **Active vs passive buyer split** (source stated as "Research consistently shows"): strong market 60–70% active / 30–40% passive; softer market 40–50% / 50–60%. **At least 30% of the buyer pool is passive in any market**, and the majority is passive in a soft one.
- The financial argument: *"Missing one motivated buyer means one less person competing… One less competitor can mean $20,000, $50,000, or more."*
- **Genroe (October 2023)** — 6.9M+ Australian Facebook users aged 45+; book updates this to **7.5–8 million** using 2026 Meta ad-reach data. Australia's under-16 social media ban (Dec 2025) further concentrates the adult demographic.
- Four claimed advantages of social over portal-only: geographic reach beyond the buyer's chosen suburb; algorithmic favouring of strong visual content; cost efficiency per 1,000 relevant people; cross-generational sharing.

#### B4. Vendor-paid marketing — an incentive argument, not an empirical one

The book's worked scenario ($1,200,000 property, 2.5% commission, extra $4,000 of marketing generating one additional competing buyer and a $60,000 higher price):

| | Cost | Gain | Net |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agent pays the extra $4,000 | −$4,000 | +$1,500 commission | **−$2,500** |
| Seller pays the extra $4,000 | −$4,000 marketing − $1,500 commission | +$60,000 sale price | **+$54,500** |

> "When marketing costs are left to agents, they will rationally spend the minimum. The homeowner is the party with the most to gain."

Recommended total budget: **$6,000–$12,000** for a $1.2M–$1.8M southern GC property, allocated across photography ($800–$1,500), video ($500–$1,000), REA Premiere Plus ($1,500–$4,000), targeted social ($800–$2,000), signage/print ($500–$900), staging if needed ($2,000–$5,000).

> ⚠ Note: Figure 16's caption states the vendor's net gain from upgrading marketing is **~$191,000** against the agent's ~$1,000. That figure is not derivable from the worked example in the text, which gives $54,500. The chart and the prose disagree.

### Tier C — Fields Estate proprietary research

> ⚠ **Read §8 before quoting anything in this tier.** Four of the seven studies below have since been formally audited and revised downward, and one supporting figure in the book was found to have no underlying analysis at all. The findings are reproduced here as the book states them.

| Study | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|
| **Domain Valuation Accuracy Study (2026)** | 1,689 automated estimates vs actual sale prices | **89% of properties were overvalued** by the online estimate. Median error 34% under $750,000, declining to 8% above $2,000,000 |
| **Comparable-Sales Valuation Engine (2026)** | Backtested vs 1,689 Domain estimates | Fields' valuations "accurate within the stated range **97% of the time** on Southern Gold Coast houses" |
| **Agent Volume vs Price Premium (2026)** | 1,475 GC sales | Agents selling **3–5 properties/year achieved 9.6% higher prices** than agents selling 20+/year — ~**$96,000** on a $1,000,000 property |
| **Seasonal Timing Analysis (2026)** | 13,585 GC sales | Best-price month: Robina **November**, Varsity Lakes **April**, Burleigh Waters **March**. Best-to-worst month variation **3–8%** = $42,000–$112,000 on a $1.4M sale |
| **Positioning Research, Playbook v5.0 (2026)** | 60+ studies, 14 academic papers, 2,153 sold properties | Underpins the three-layer framework and the renovation ROI table |
| **Facebook Advertising Performance (2026)** | — | Property-specific content generates **4× engagement** vs generic market content; informational beats aspirational **5:1** |
| **Price-driver analysis** | 931 verified southern GC sales | Floor area is the dominant driver: **r = 0.68–0.79**, explaining **46–62%** of price variation within each suburb |

#### C1. The anchoring problem the estimate creates

The 89% overvaluation figure is not presented as an accuracy complaint but as a **price-destroying anchor**:

> "It's anchored you to a number that is $180,000 above reality. Every agent who walks through your door now has to overcome that anchor. The ones who tell you the truth sound pessimistic. The ones who confirm the fantasy sound optimistic. Guess who gets hired."

The stated consequence, which the book calls invariable: *"The property lists too high, sits on the market for six or eight weeks, takes a price reduction, and eventually sells at or below where the comparables said it would."*

#### C2. What actually drives value (931 sales)

- **Floor area** — strongest driver in every suburb analysed (r = 0.68–0.79)
- **Bedrooms** — an additional bedroom worth **$255,000–$607,000** depending on suburb. Burleigh Waters 3→4 bed is the highest measured premium at **$607,000**; Robina ~$255,000. *Explicitly not a recommendation to build one* — "construction costs almost never deliver a positive return in the short term"
- **Corner lots** — sell at approximately a **14% discount** to mid-block in Robina and Burleigh Waters. On $1,400,000 that is $196,000
- **Pools** — relationship to price/sqm is **0.6–3.7%**; "statistically, this is not significant. The confidence intervals overlap zero in most analyses." Pools correlate with larger blocks and newer builds — "along for the ride, not driving it"

#### C3. The agent-volume finding — the book's single largest claimed lever

**9.6% premium for low-volume agents.** The book's causal explanation is time, not talent: an agent with five listings can invest more per buyer than one with twenty-five. That time buys three capabilities:

1. **Deep buyer knowledge** — including the buyer's *stretch budget* ("the price they'd pay if they truly believed this was the one"), not their stated budget
2. **Becoming the buyer's trusted source** — built by telling buyers uncomfortable truths, which converts into influence over willingness to commit and stretch
3. **Better buyer–home matching** — "Better-matched buyers pay higher prices"

The illustrating anecdote: a buyer whose top priority was watching his children ride bikes to school bought before the first open home, ignoring a dated kitchen and original bathrooms. "The property sold above valuation."

**Negotiation** is presented as a consequence of the above, not a separate skill: *"The strongest negotiating position a seller can be in is having more than one buyer who believes this is their home."* Four agent behaviours listed — never negotiate with a single buyer in isolation when other interest exists; use time to create urgency without pressure; control information flow (buyers know competition exists, not who or how much); **help each buyer reach their emotional peak at the same time so the best offers land in the same window.**

**The screening question the book recommends:** not "are you a good negotiator?" but *"walk me through how you managed the last property where you had multiple interested buyers."*

**The commission counter-argument** ($1,200,000 property):

| | Commission | Sale Price | Net to Seller |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agent A (1.5%, sells at valuation) | $18,000 | $1,200,000 | $1,182,000 |
| Agent B (2.5%, 5% premium) | $30,000 | $1,260,000 | $1,230,000 |
| **Difference** | +$12,000 | +$60,000 | **+$48,000** |

> "The $12,000 saving cost $48,000."

Note the premium assumed here is **5%**, not the 9.6% the study produced — the book is conservative in its own worked example. Commission on the GC is stated as 1.5–2.75%, averaging **2.56%**.

**Five agent-selection indicators:** (1) a valuation backed by specific comparable sales with adjustments explained — "an agent who arrives without printed comparable sales analysis is not prepared"; (2) listens more than talks; (3) transparent about risks; (4) can explain the marketing strategy in detail with a budget breakdown; (5) can demonstrate live buyer relationships in your price bracket.

#### C4. The three-layer positioning framework (Ch 5 — "the three things that actually move the price")

Built from 60 academic studies, 14 peer-reviewed papers, 2,153 sold properties. "Get all three right, and you create the conditions for a premium sale. Get even one wrong, and the other two can't compensate."

**Layer 1 — What buyers see first.** The 3-second scroll decision. Highest-ROI spending lives here:
- **Fresh paint** — sells ~**25% faster**, offers up to **5% higher**. On $1,400,000 that is $70,000, for a $3,000–$8,000 cost
- **Landscaping** — up to **20% increase in perceived value**. The book is careful: *"Not actual value — perceived value. But in property, perceived value becomes actual value the moment a second buyer makes an offer."* Cost $500–$2,000
- **Professional photography** — "non-negotiable"; twilight, wide-angle, drone for neighbourhood context
- **Virtual tours** — the Soleymanian & Qian ~1% (see A6)

**Layer 2 — What buyers feel.** Narrative positioning, mental simulation, and the endowment effect.
- The worked contrast: *"4-bedroom family home with pool and outdoor entertaining area"* vs *"The north-facing backyard catches afternoon light until 6pm in winter. From the living room, the spotted gums frame a view of the hinterland that most visitors don't expect."*
- Mechanism: specific, vivid description activates **mental simulation** → the buyer mentally takes ownership → **endowment effect** → *"Their willingness to pay increases. Their sensitivity to price decreases."*
- *"The first could describe hundreds of properties. The second can only describe one. The specificity is what creates credibility, and credibility is what creates trust, and trust is what creates willingness to pay a premium."*
- **What backfires — manufactured urgency.** Fields' Burleigh Waters analysis: listings with urgency-style opening lines ("Don't miss out!", "Once in a lifetime opportunity!") averaged **33 days on market vs 20.5 days** for factual, data-driven openings — **61% longer**. Genuine, verifiable scarcity is fine ("one of only two four-bedroom homes with a pool currently listed in Robina under $1,400,000"); manufactured scarcity damages positioning because "buyers in these suburbs are experienced and information-rich"

**Layer 3 — What buyers compare.** Competitive positioning against the live competitive set, not the historical market.
- "A feature that would be unremarkable when fifteen competing properties are listed might be genuinely scarce when there are five."
- **Honest trade-offs as a pricing tool** — the book's most distinctive commentary. A 540sqm Robina block is ~200sqm below the suburb average; that gap *is* roughly $220,000 the buyer isn't spending. A busier road is why the price is $80,000–$120,000 below the cul-de-sac comparable. *"They aren't flaws. They are the reason the property is at this price point, and the buyer already knows it."*
- *"A buyer who can see exactly what they're trading and exactly what they're saving feels in control — and a buyer in control is a buyer who makes an offer."*

#### C5. The emotional peak (Ch 4)

Presented as the timing mechanism that everything else serves:

> "To secure the best possible price — or even exceed expectations — an agent must possess the expertise to artfully elevate buyer emotions to a peak and then skillfully extract their optimal offer. Timing is crucial."

> "The emotional peak cannot be sustained. It will drop, at which point the buyer begins to second-guess their decision. Offers must be obtained at the emotional peak — not before it (when the buyer hasn't committed enough to stretch) and not after (when doubt has set in and the willingness to pay has declined)."

The role assigned to visual content is *sustaining* the peak between inspections: buyers return to the listing repeatedly and each revisit reinforces the connection. *"A buyer who returns to professional twilight photography and a detailed video walkthrough deepens their emotional investment with every viewing. A buyer who returns to dark, blurry photos has nothing to sustain their imagination."*

Source is Fields positioning research (Figure 9), not external literature.

#### C6. Timing — real but the weakest of the three

- 13,585 GC sales; best-to-worst month spread **3–8%** ($42,000–$112,000 on $1.4M)
- Absorption rates as of April 2026 — Robina ~1.8 months of inventory, Varsity Lakes ~1.3, Burleigh Waters ~1.6; all "firmly in seller-favourable territory" (<3 months creates competitive conditions, <2 months creates buyer urgency)
- **Competitive set, not suburb inventory:** "If there are 55 listings in Robina but only six of them are four-bedroom homes priced between $1,300,000 and $1,500,000, your competitive set is six, not fifty-five. The other forty-nine listings are noise."
- **The book explicitly demotes this lever:** *"The strongest sale combines three things: a correct price on day one, marketing that reaches the right buyers, and — where possible — a listing month that the data favours. The first two matter more than the third, but the third is free."*
- And explicitly disclaims prediction: *"These patterns reflect historical averages… past seasonal performance does not guarantee future results."* / *"It's not a crystal ball — it's a rearview mirror."*

#### C7. Price brackets and portal visibility

A purely mechanical lever: a home at $1,510,000 is invisible to a buyer searching $1,250,000–$1,500,000; at $1,495,000 it appears. "In most cases, being at the top of the bracket that contains your natural buyer pool is the stronger position" — a $1,485,000 home is the premium option in its bracket rather than the cheapest and "least desirable" option in the bracket above.

## 3. What the book says does **NOT** reliably raise the price

This is a substantial part of the answer and the book treats it as equally evidenced.

| Item | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Full kitchen renovation** | **~57% ROI** — a $40,000 kitchen recovers ~$22,800 in sale price |
| **Full bathroom renovation** | **~75% ROI** — a $20,000 bathroom recovers ~$15,000 |
| **Pool installation** | **0.6–3.7%** price/sqm effect; not statistically significant. "A $50,000 pool adds negligible value" |
| **Renovation generally** | "Renovating before sale can help your home sell faster and with less buyer resistance, but the data suggests it rarely adds dollar-for-dollar value in the sale price" |
| **Auction (in QLD)** | No price advantage across 1.2M sales; higher cost; 72% smaller top-of-funnel |
| **Underpricing to start a bidding war** | Fails in private treaty (Bucchianeri & Minson, 14,000 transactions) |
| **Choosing the highest-volume agent** | 9.6% *worse* than 3–5/year agents |
| **Minimising commission** | The $12,000 saving cost $48,000 |
| **Manufactured urgency in copy** | 61% longer on market |
| **Auction clearance rates as a timing signal** | Structurally unreliable (Deakin) |
| **Watching the RBA cash rate** | A *lagging* indicator — moves ~12 months *after* GC prices turn (Appendix C) |

**The book's own worked example of renovation disappointment:** two same-street Robina homes, one with a $65,000 renovation, sells for $30,000 more. *"They invested $65,000 to recover $30,000 at the point of sale. The remaining $35,000 was the cost of enjoying the renovation while they lived there — which is perfectly valid, but shouldn't be confused with an investment return."*

**The suburb exception, and the book's own caveat on it.** In Burleigh Waters (median $1,710,000) fully renovated properties achieve ~**14% higher price/sqm** than original condition. In Robina ($1,400,000) and Varsity Lakes ($1,224,000), renovated homes showed *lower* $/sqm than originals. Two caveats the book states itself:
- *"This finding may partly reflect a floor-area effect. Larger homes tend to have lower price per square metre… and larger homes are also more likely to be renovated. Without controlling for floor area within size brackets, some of the apparent renovation discount… may be explained by renovated homes being larger, not by renovation itself reducing value. Further analysis is underway."*
- Even where renovation pays, it costs time: Burleigh Waters renovated homes take **~3× longer to sell** than original-condition ones, because the higher asking price narrows the buyer pool.

**Appendix C — what actually drives GC prices at the macro level.** **Abelson, Joyeux, Milunovich & Chung (2005)**, *Economic Record* 81(s1), S96–S103: **r = 0.940** correlation between Australian wages and property prices, income elasticity **1.71×** (every 1% wage rise → ~1.7% price rise). Fields' local confirmation: the QLD Wage Price Index leads GC median house prices by **3–4 months** consistently across a decade. Migration adds ~**15,300 residents/year**. The seller instruction: *"The seller who waits for the RBA to signal the right moment is reading the rearview mirror."*

## 5. So — is there a proven way?

Reading the whole book against its own evidence *and* against the internal audits in §8, the honest answer is:

**The proven findings are almost entirely negative — things that reliably cost you money. The positive levers are either small, narrow, or internally unverified.**

- **Robustly proven, multiple independent peer-reviewed studies, hundreds of thousands of transactions:** overpricing beyond ~5–10% on day one *reduces* your final price and multiplies time on market by 2–5×; a price reduction mid-campaign leaves you worse off than pricing correctly from the start; underpricing to start a bidding war does not work in a private-treaty market; auction confers no measurable price advantage in a market like Queensland's while costing more and shrinking the top of the funnel; your agent's incentives are structurally misaligned with maximising your price by roughly 3.7%.
- **Proven and positive, but small and narrow:** precise-above-round list pricing with all four Cardella & Seiler conditions met (though a conflicting peer-reviewed result exists); an optimal overprice of **~1.5%**, not 10% (Nikiforou et al.); virtual tours **~1%**, and shrinking; listing into the first-two-week window when the active-buyer audience arrives all at once.
- **Internally measured but audited downward or unverified:** the 9.6% agent-volume premium (**now ~4.1–4.5%** — see §8.1), the 97% valuation-range accuracy claim (**internal figure is 56.8%** — §8.3), the 3–8% seasonal spread (**the 13,585-sale sample is not reproducible; canonical peak is November +3.3%** — §8.2), the 89% online-estimate overvaluation (**benchmark contaminated** — §8.3), and every days-on-market claim in the book (**0.8% native DOM coverage; the DOM model's cross-validated R² is 0.006** — §8.4).
- **Explicitly disproven as price levers:** renovation as an investment (57% / 75% recovery), pools (0.6–3.7%, not significant), kitchen finishes (p > 0.1), condition score (near-zero correlation), paying less commission — and, from the peer-reviewed literature the book does not cite, **staging** (null effect on willingness to pay, Lane et al.).

**The most defensible framing available.** The evidence does not support "here is how to get more." It supports: *the range of outcomes between a well-run and a badly-run campaign is far wider than any fee you are negotiating over.* The Fields commission-conversation rewrite states this range as **+3% to +5% for strong process, −5% to −8% for poor process, against a commission spread of −1.25%.** That is a claim about avoiding a large downside, not about capturing a proven premium — and it is the one version of the book's argument that survives every check.

**The counter-evidence the book omits entirely.** A separate body of peer-reviewed work finds agents deliver **zero to negative** price premium net of commission, and add value in *liquidity* instead — speed and certainty of sale. Hendel, Nevo & Ortalo-Magné (2009) found FSBO pre-commission prices no lower than MLS; Bernheim & Meer (2013), using Stanford faculty housing with identical MLS access, found **broker use reduced sale prices by 5.9–7.7%**; Barwick & Pathak (2015), across 650,000 Massachusetts listings, found flat-fee homes sold 1–4% less but were **10 percentage points less likely to sell at all**. The internal synthesis is blunt: *"agent value is in liquidity (certainty + speed of sale), not price."* None of this appears in the book, which argues the opposite in Chapter 6. (Caveats: all US except one Dutch study, mostly 15+ years old, and Australian commissions of 2–3% are well below the US-era 5–6% those studies assume.)

The book's own summary sentence is the fairest statement of its position:

> "Not certainty of outcome. Certainty that the process was right." — Ch 8, *Will's View*

And its framing of what the seller actually controls:

> "The sellers who feel best about that day are the ones who made informed decisions. Not because they got the highest possible price — no one knows what the highest possible price was. But because they understood the data, they chose their agent deliberately, they marketed broadly, and they evaluated offers against evidence rather than emotion."

## 7. Evidential weak points a critical reader will find

5. **Bucchianeri & Minson (2013) is an anchoring paper**, used here only for its underpricing implication, alongside six studies arguing that anchoring high destroys value. The tension is unaddressed.

## 8. What has since been audited — corrections that post-date the book

Everything in this section comes from Fields' own internal audit files, not from external criticism. It is included because it directly changes the answer to the question the book is being asked.

### 8.1 The 9.6% agent premium — the book's largest single claimed lever — does not survive robustness checks

`08_Seller-Book/agent_analysis/` replicates the published figure exactly (headline gap 9.49 pp, n = 1,464 sales / 444 agents) and then breaks it four ways:

| Check | Result |
|---|---|
| **Detrending** (subtract each year's median ratio) | Gap falls to **4.5 pp**; on *medians* it nearly vanishes — 3–5 bucket +1.81 pp, 20+ bucket −0.04 pp |
| **Restrict to 2025+** | Gap falls to **4.15 pp** (98.7% vs 94.6%) |
| **Temporal mix** | This is the confound. The metric is `sale_price / domain_valuation_at_listing`, and Domain's valuations drifted from **−34% (2021) to +5% (2026)**. The 20+ bucket's median sale date is **2024-08-28** vs 2025-01 to 2025-05 for every other bucket. METHODOLOGY.md: *"The drift is **not a real change in agent performance**"* |
| **Top-bucket composition** | The 20+ bucket is **9 agents**, of whom 3 (means 0.766, 0.762, 0.769) drag it down, all with median year 2023. Bucket median 90.2% vs mean 86.3%. *"fragile to outliers… would shift materially if any of these three agents were missing"* |

Three further corrections:

- **The buckets are lifetime totals over a ~4.5-year window, not "per year."** METHODOLOGY.md states plainly: *"the seller book Chapter 6 draft refers to these buckets as 'per year', which is incorrect."* The book says "per year" in the headline claim, in Figure 12's caption, and in the chapter heading.
- **Figure 13's commission table is fabricated.** `COMMISSION_CONVERSATION_REPLACEMENT.md` exists to remove *"the **fabricated** four-tier commission/premium table (Figure 13, currently labelled 'Source: Fields Estate, 2026' but **with no underlying analysis**)"* and its +0%/+3%/+5%/+7% scheme.
- **The 9.6 pp headline was knowingly retained in this print run.** The decision log records: *"Print version of the book keeps the existing chapter claim (Will's decision — already at print, not rewritable now)."* The replacement text stops *amplifying* it but does not correct it. Next edition should rewrite Chapter 6.

Official position, verbatim: *"**The pattern direction holds. The magnitude was inflated by Domain valuation temporal drift and 2–3 outlier agents in the 20+ bucket.**"* Defensible restatement: *"agents with 3–5 lifetime listings achieved higher average sale-to-Domain-valuation ratios than agents with 20+ lifetime listings"* — consistent with Levitt & Syverson (2008).

### 8.2 The seasonal analysis — sample not reproducible, magnitudes overstated

`Market_Data/README.md` (2026-06-02) is now the source of truth: **18,978 matched sales, 2010–2025 excluding COVID years 2019–20**, median sale price by month vs each year's annual average:

`Jan −1.4 · Feb −1.4 · Mar +0.9 · Apr −0.3 · May −0.5 · Jun +1.6 · Jul −0.0 · Aug +2.3 · Sep +2.5 · Oct +2.6 · **Nov +3.3** · Dec +2.8`

- Peak is **November (+3.3%)** — the book's Robina peak month is right, the magnitude is not. The book's stated **3–8%** best-to-worst spread ($42,000–$112,000) is roughly **4.7 percentage points** on this dataset.
- *"The article **overstated** the effect (it claimed December +6.05% and a −3.83% January; the real, COVID-excluded figures are +2.81% and −1.37%). **None of the old numbers were reproducible from a committed script.**"* Six files were deleted as untrustworthy.
- `seasonality/RUN_SUMMARY.md`: *"**The article's 13,585-sale sample is not reproducible from the current DB… The article's exact extraction is unknown.**"* — that is the sample cited in the book and in Reference 25.
- The effect is measured on **sale month, not listing month** — which is the decision a seller actually makes.
- Standing editorial rule: *"these are historical patterns, never predictions or advice… **not a recommendation to list in a given month**."*

### 8.3 The valuation accuracy claims

- **"Accurate within the stated range 97% of the time"** (Ch 1) is contradicted by the internal measurement: *"our own range contains the sale price only **56.8%** of the time, and `high` (56.0%) is **indistinguishable** from `medium` (57.5%) — the confidence label is non-discriminating."*
- **The 89% Domain-overvaluation finding rests on a contaminated benchmark.** `domain_valuation_at_listing` is misnamed — it is snapshotted at sold-*detection*, and **703/766 (91.8%) were captured on or after the sale date, median +266 days**. Domain ingests sold prices, so it was being scored with hindsight while Fields was held out-of-sample. The clean subset is n=21. Verdict: *"**We have no valid Fields-vs-Domain comparison in either direction.**"* On a separate 708-property measurement the platform's MAE was **10.3% — better than ours**. The supported angle is **calibration, not accuracy**.
- **Never claim more accurate than an agent appraisal.** Against a random agent 3-comp valuation, Fields wins **exactly 50.0%** of the time — median absolute error **10.0% vs 9.6%**. A dead heat.
- **What does hold:** *determinacy*. The median spread between the best and worst defensible agent valuation of the same home is **32.9% of its value (median $469,000)**, and exceeds 20% on 77% of homes. And adjusting comparables narrows the quoted range — median **38.8% narrowing across n=512**, narrowing at all in **91.0%** of cases.

### 8.4 Every days-on-market claim in the book rests on almost no data

From `phase_0_summary.md` for the 2,153-property positioning corpus:

- **`time_on_market_days` coverage: 17 / 2,153 = 0.8%.** `first_listed_date`: 14 / 2,153 = 0.7%.
- The DOM model built on it has **cross-validated R² = 0.006** — effectively zero predictive validity.
- The hedonic price model is respectable by comparison: Ridge on log(price), n=1,662, **CV R² = 0.594 ± 0.127, MAE $202,119 (13.3% MAPE)** — which is itself worth knowing when the book quotes floor-area and bedroom effects.

This affects: the **33 vs 20.5 days** manufactured-urgency finding, the **"3× longer to sell"** Burleigh Waters renovation claim, the **25% faster** paint claim, and the **10 days faster** Premiere Plus figure.

The corpus's own conclusion on speed is more useful than any of them: *"**The honest answer: price drives speed, not quality.** … Higher condition/presentation scores = **SLOWER** sales (r = +0.13). The only controllable speed lever is pricing. Everything else is noise."*

### 8.5 One published case study misrepresented a struggling campaign as a win

Logged as an accuracy incident directly relevant to "how to get a higher price": the article *"$1,700,000 in Robina: How 16 Collingwood Avenue Beat the Suburb Median by 11.8%"* described a home that first asked **$1,949,000**, was cut to $1,749,000, and sold at **$1,700,000 after 127 days** — and called the reduced price "the guide." The fix logged: *"How It Sold must read `price_history`, not just `listing_price`"* — otherwise any reduced campaign reads as a win. (Context: sold homes that cut their price ran a median **68 days vs 37** for those that didn't.)

### 8.6 Consolidated do-not-claim register

Compiled from the audit files; these override anything in the book:

1. Do not re-amplify **9.6 pp** without caveats — the honest number is **~4.1–4.5 pp**.
2. Do not call the agent buckets "**per year**" — they are lifetime-in-dataset (~4.5 years).
3. Do not use the **+0%/+3%/+5%/+7% commission-tier table** — fabricated, no underlying analysis.
4. Do not claim "**agents always add value**" or "higher commission → higher price."
5. Do not claim Fields is **more accurate than an agent appraisal** — dead heat, 10.0% vs 9.6%.
6. Do not claim Fields is **more accurate than a portal/Domain** — benchmark contaminated; no valid comparison exists in either direction.
7. Do not claim **staging adds 1–10%** — peer-reviewed evidence (Lane et al.) is a null on willingness to pay.
8. Do not frame seasonality as **advice or a forecast**, and do not reuse the old December +6.05% / January −3.83% figures.
9. Do not claim **underpricing creates bidding wars** in private treaty.
10. Do not claim **renovation, kitchen finishes, pools or condition** raise $/sqm in Robina or Varsity Lakes.
11. Do not treat any **days-on-market** claim from the 2,153 corpus as robust.
12. Never say "We have not found another agency or portal that publishes theirs" (logged never-say; a contradiction between two internal documents on this is unresolved, pending Will).
