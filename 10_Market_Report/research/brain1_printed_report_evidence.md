# Printed Market Reports as a Listing-Winning Instrument

## 1. Two Distinct Instruments, Not One

The corpus describes two genuinely different printed artefacts that get conflated under "market report." They serve different jobs, are distributed differently, and the practitioners who swear by each are largely different people.

**A. The substantial, personalised report (CMA / suburb report / market wrap)** — a weightier document built around a specific home or a specific farm, positioned as a credibility weapon.

**B. The short, glossy quarterly review** — an eight-page recurring booklet mailed to a whole database on a fixed cadence, positioned as a *nurture rhythm* instrument, not a one-shot closer.

The corpus does **not** contain a head-to-head A/B test of the two lengths. What it contains is two coherent, separately-evidenced schools of practice. I'll treat the contrast honestly on that basis rather than manufacture a comparison the units don't make.

---

## 2. The Substantial Report — "The Secret Weapon"

The strongest claim for the large, personalised report comes from **u0048**, which frames the CMA as the single most powerful area-working tool an agent has:

> "This is probably the greatest tool that you have in working an area. It's literally like the secret weapon." (**u0048**)

Two design principles make it work, both in **u0048**:

- **Personalisation is the mechanism, not a nicety.** "The power of this is when they get the report, they're going to open it up. There's a picture of their house on the covering letter." (**u0048**) The report is opened *because* it is visibly about the recipient's own home.
- **It compounds into incumbency.** "You will have no competition after a period of time." (**u0048**)

**u0202** extends the "substantial = authority" logic to the suburb report specifically, and — critically — makes the strongest **readership** claim in the corpus:

> "It's going to stamp your authority in the marketplace... Because it's not just junk type mail, people are going to read this document." (**u0202**)

The distinction being drawn is *report vs. flyer*: the substance is what earns it the status of "a document" rather than "junk mail," and that status is what (the practitioner asserts) drives it being read rather than binned.

**Distribution of the substantial report** is deliberately *slow and high-touch*:
- **u0048** specifies a three-day delivery timeline (built, not instant), and pairs it with the personalised covering letter.
- **u2384** ties it to a single-suburb database discipline: "I just focused on one particular suburb and then I just built up a database from there," with explicit "quarterly market report distribution" as the cadence.
- **u2436** frames the same instrument as market-positioning: "I'd like you to become the Google in your marketplace... when they need to list, you're already there," alongside "monthly market data reports."

**Downside / obstacle (stated in-corpus):** the substantial report is *effortful and slow*. **u0051** places it in a demanding rhythm — "The rhythm is very important" — that requires weekly letterbox + monthly letter + quarterly high-effort materials in parallel. The report only pays off inside that full cadence; as a standalone artefact it is not claimed to convert.

## 3. The Short Quarterly Review — Sonja Treloar's Eight-Page Booklet

The clearest, most concrete practitioner account of a *specific length* is Sonja Treloar's, and it is unambiguous about the eight-page format:

> "We have four phone calls a year... So the first thing we do is actually send out a four quarter market reviews and that's an eight page, beautiful glossy booklet." (**u0449**)

Key facts from **u0449**:
- **Length recommended in practice: eight pages.** This is the corpus's only explicit page-count recommendation for a recurring market review, and it is *short and glossy*, not substantial.
- **Distribution:** posted to a database of **3,264 contacts**, four times a year, each mailing paired with a phone call — a print-plus-call cadence, not print alone.
- **Achievement claimed:** "**50% of sales have come out of my database**" (echoed in **u0645**: "50% of my sales have come out of my database"). The eight-pager is the nurture spine of a database that produces half of all revenue.

The **follow-up call script** is preserved in **u0955** and reveals what the booklet is *for*:

> "Sonja just wants me to give you a call one to see if you got the quarter market review and two is there anything Sonja or her team can help or support you through this time." (**u0955**)

Two things follow from that script:
1. **The booklet is a permission-based reason to call.** Its primary function is to *earn the phone contact*, not to close on its own — "60% of the decision is made before the listing presentation" (**u5850349667**, **u0302**, **u0293**), and the quarterly booklet is one of the pre-decision touchpoints.
2. **It is calibrated to a cold-market reality.** The same body of coaching notes that "only 4% of Australians [are] in real estate mode" at any time (**u0955**), which is the explicit rationale for a *light, repeatable* format over a heavy one: you cannot mail a 40-page document to 3,264 people four times a year, but you can mail eight glossy pages.

**Corroborating short-format practice** appears across other sources, all pointing the same way — *short + frequent beats long + rare*:
- **u2063**: work a patch of ~200,000, using "quarterly market reports as information (not self-promotion)" — the report is *information-giving*, deliberately not a brochure.
- **u2384** and **u2436** (above) both fold quarterly/monthly reports into a frequency system rather than a single showpiece.

## 4. The Length Question — What Practitioners Actually Recommend

Putting the two schools side by side, the corpus's actual recommendations resolve less around *page count* and more around *format-fit-to-job*:

| Dimension | Substantial report (CMA / suburb report) | Short quarterly review |
|---|---|---|
| Explicit length in corpus | Not page-specified; positioned as "a document," weighty enough to escape "junk mail" (**u0202**) | **Eight pages**, "beautiful glossy booklet" (**u0449**) |
| Job | Establish authority / incumbency on a specific home or farm (**u0048**, **u0202**) | Sustain database nurture rhythm; earn the quarterly call (**u0449**, **u0955**) |
| Distribution | Slow, personalised, 3-day build, own-home photo on cover (**u0048**); single-suburb DB (**u2384**) | Posted to full DB (3,264), 4×/yr, each paired with a call (**u0449**) |
| Frequency | Biannual/quarterly inside a weekly-monthly-quarterly rhythm (**u0051**) | Strictly quarterly (**u0449**) |
| Claimed outcome | "No competition after a period" (**u0048**); "stamp your authority" (**u0202**) | 50% of all sales from the database it feeds (**u0449**, **u0645**) |

**The honest synthesis:** no unit says "eight pages is better than forty" or vice-versa. What the corpus *does* recommend, repeatedly and from every source, is a **frequency principle that outranks length**:

> "You are better off doing 5,000 letter box drops a week to the same people each week than just doing one person once in a month... frequency builds trust." (**u0199**)

> "The branding, the marketing is not about innovation... It's about old stuff again and again and again." (**u1009**)

> "They still work. But they work in combination, not in isolation. They work with consistency, predictable, repeatable." (**u1174**)

So the practitioner recommendation, drawn across sources, is: **make the report short enough that you can afford to send it often**, because the trust is built by the cadence, not the page-count. The eight-page booklet exists *because* it is sustainable at quarterly frequency to thousands of people; the substantial CMA is reserved for the individual home where personalisation, not volume, is the lever.

## 5. Evidence That Recipients Actually Read Them

This is where the corpus is **thin and mostly assertional** — I want to be precise about what is evidence versus what is a practitioner's belief.

**What the corpus asserts (belief, not measurement):**
- **u0202**: "people are going to read this document" — the strongest readership claim, but it is the agent's assertion, resting on the *not-junk-mail* distinction, not on any open-rate or engagement data.
- **u0048**: "they're going to open it up. There's a picture of their house on the covering letter" — again a mechanism-of-attention claim (own-home photo drives the open), asserted, not measured.

**What the corpus offers as *behavioural* evidence (weak but real):**
- The **response to the follow-up call** in **u0955** is the closest thing to read-evidence: the call opens by asking "if you got the quarter market review," and the whole model rests on that contact being warm enough to sustain a database that yields 50% of sales (**u0449**, **u0645**). If recipients were binning it unread, the paired-call nurture wouldn't produce that conversion — but this is *inferred* from the outcome, not a direct readership measurement.
- **k01086** supplies the only *external/general* evidence on print engagement, and it is about credibility perception rather than this specific artefact: "The printed word is still perceived as more credible to many people than anything on the web," alongside "64% of B2B marketers create original content for customer retention." That supports *why* print is chosen but does not measure whether these particular reports are read.

**A cross-cutting caution the corpus itself raises — the engagement-decay window.** **u2267**, though about pre-listing kits rather than quarterly reports, is the one unit that quantifies *consumption timing* of printed materials: "after 14 days... it tapers off very quickly." If that decay generalises, it argues *for* the frequent-short model (re-触 the reader before attention lapses) and *against* assuming a substantial report is read cover-to-cover over time.

**Plainly stated gap:** the corpus contains **no direct readership measurement** — no open rate, no response-attribution study, no read-through data — for either report type. Every "they read it" claim is either the producing agent's assertion (**u0202**, **u0048**) or an inference from downstream sales (**u0449**). I will not dress that up as harder than it is.

## 6. Where Reports Sit Among Competing Listing-Winning Methods

To keep the reports in proportion: a large body of the corpus argues that **reports are one touchpoint among many, and rarely the decisive one.**

- The decision is substantially pre-made: "60% of the decision is made before the listing presentation" (**u5850349667**, **u0302**, **u0293**, **u0299**) — reports are pre-decision *inputs*, not closers.
- Multi-touch, not single-artefact, is the repeated frame: "I touched them 50 times... The collectiveness of them" (**u0139**); "80% of sales are made from the fifth to the 12th contact" (**u1229**).
- Some top practitioners explicitly **de-emphasise** printed reports in favour of relationships, referrals, and video: "Listings are won and lost at dinner parties" (**u0695**); "A third-party referral is far better than a hundred cams calls" (**u1142**); "market wrap videos to me are absolutely critical" (**u2317**). Jackson Wilkey's first listing came from a *mailer* (**k01416**) but his winning *pitch* was drone video + Facebook reach, "not print reports" (**k01415**).

So the substantial CMA and the eight-page quarterly are best understood as **the print rails of a multi-channel farm**, valuable for authority and rhythm respectively — not as the mechanism that, alone, wins the listing.

## 7. Insight Layer — Core Principles and Extensions

**Principle 1 — Length is a function of frequency, not of authority.**
The corpus's implicit rule: *substance where you personalise to one home; brevity where you repeat to thousands.* The eight-page booklet is short **because** it's quarterly-to-3,264; the CMA is heavy **because** it's one-home-at-a-time. Anyone asking "how long should my report be?" is asking the wrong question — the right question is "how often can I afford to send it, and to how many?" (**u0449**, **u0199**, **u0048**).

**Principle 2 — The personalised open is the whole game.** "There's a picture of their house on the covering letter" (**u0048**) is the difference between a read document and junk mail (**u0202**). Personalisation, not production value, drives the open.

**Principle 3 — The report's real job is to earn the next contact.** Sonja's booklet exists to license the quarterly call (**u0955**); the CMA exists to make the agent the incumbent so the next conversation is uncontested (**u0048**, **u1128**). The artefact is a *permission device*, not a persuasion device.

**Extensions — and which are un-copyable for Fields:**

- **Copyable now:** A *short, glossy, high-frequency* suburb review posted quarterly, paired with a call — Sonja's exact model (**u0449**) — is directly replicable and matches Fields' existing quarterly report pipeline.
- **Un-copyable by legacy agents, native to Fields:** The corpus's personalisation ceiling is "a picture of their house on the cover" (**u0048**). Fields' data platform can put the *reader's own home's comparable range, floor-area-driven valuation, and days-on-market* on the cover — "Floor area is the strongest driver of price in every suburb we've analysed" (**k02044**) — turning **u0048**'s covering-letter trick into a genuinely per-home report at database scale. Legacy agents cannot personalise 3,264 booklets to each home's own data; Fields can. This is the "data is the weapon" moat (**k02895267819**... i.e. **i2895267819**) applied to the exact instrument the coaches call the "secret weapon."
- **Un-copyable measurement extension:** the corpus's biggest gap is that *nobody measures whether reports are read* (§5). Fields' platform can close it — tokenised report links, per-home open/engagement tracking, and read-through attribution — converting the coaches' *assertion* "people are going to read this document" (**u0202**) into an actual measured funnel. The **u2267** "14-day taper" becomes something Fields can observe rather than assume, and feed back into cadence.

**Sharpened method for Fields:** *Short-and-frequent (Sonja's eight-page quarterly, **u0449**) as the database rail, each booklet auto-personalised to the recipient home's own comparable data (Fields moat over **u0048**), delivered with a tokenised link so readership is measured rather than assumed (closes the **u0202**/**u2267** evidence gap), each mailing licensing a call (**u0955**) — with the heavy personalised CMA reserved for the specific home being worked toward a listing.*
