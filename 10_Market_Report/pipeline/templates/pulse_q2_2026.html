The quarter · Q2 2026
The quarter

A loud sale in a quiet market

On a Friday afternoon in April, on a lakefront street in Robina, bidding closed on a single-level home with twenty-two metres of water frontage. The hammer fell at $2,410,000 — more than double what the house last changed hands for in 2020. It was one of the loudest moments of the quarter. Most of the quarter was quiet.

That contrast is the story of Q2 2026. Judged by the trailing-twelve-month median — the measure that doesn't lurch on a handful of sales — prices in all three suburbs held or rose. Judged by how many homes actually sold, the quarter was among the thinnest in years: turnover ran roughly half its year-earlier level, listings built up, and homes took longer to find a buyer. With the RBA cash rate at 4.35% after three rises this year, and a tax change announced mid-quarter, the pressure landed on activity, not price.

When this report last read the market, at the close of Q1 2026, the Conviction Index sat at 100.1 — right on the line between balanced and cooling. A quarter later it reads 94.4. The softening the volume figures hinted at then has now shown up in the composite. Nothing here suggests prices falling; it suggests a market catching its breath.

What firmed

  • Trailing-12-month median still rising in all three suburbs
  • Wages (+3.4%) and household spending — the best leading indicators — both still climbing
  • Burleigh Waters' average time-to-sell fell sharply (60.8 → 42.5 days)

What softened

  • Single-quarter sales down 44–56% on a year earlier
  • Listings building; days-on-market lengthening in Robina and Varsity Lakes
  • The Conviction Index below its 100 baseline (94.4)

What to watch

  • The 1 July 2027 negative-gearing & CGT changes (page 5)
  • The 11 August RBA decision; Q2 CPI lands 29 July
  • Whether sellers meet the market, or buyers return first
The Conviction Index since 2017 (composite in bold). It has eased to 94.4 — its first move below the 100 baseline since early 2025.

The question for the second half of 2026: which gives first — sellers adjusting to thinner demand, or buyers returning as the rate cycle settles? The data doesn't answer it yet. It tells us where the tension sits.

The pattern is consistent: where a single quarter shows a price fall — Burleigh Waters −7.3%, Robina −3.8% — it does so on a thin sample, against a rolling line that is still climbing. Burleigh Waters' trailing median is not merely rising but accelerating. What has changed is not the price of a home that sells; it is how many homes are selling, and how long they take.

Rolling prices up, single quarters wobbling — which is the signal? On the weight of transactions, the trailing line. The wobble is what a thin quarter looks like.

What activity did — the real story

Fewer sales, slower sales

Quarterly house sales, deduplicated. Q2 2026 ran below each suburb's five-year average and well below a year earlier.
Median days-on-market. Robina and Varsity Lakes have slowed; Burleigh Waters sits near its long-run median.
Reading the days-on-market curve
Time-on-market is the market's patience gauge. A settled home's clock runs longest in its first weeks; interest is highest early, then eases. When the median stretches — Robina from 24 to 38 days over the year — it means more homes are sitting past that early window before a buyer commits. Prices can hold while this happens: it is buyers taking longer to decide, not sellers dropping their price.

When homes take longer to sell but prices hold, someone is being patient. The data doesn't yet say who blinks — the seller who trims, or the buyer who returns.

Policy · Q2 2026
The rule change beneath the numbers

A tax reform enters the calculus

On Budget night, 12 May 2026, the Federal Government announced the biggest change to property tax settings in a generation. It doesn't take effect until 1 July 2027 — but it landed inside this quarter, and it is already part of how buyers and sellers are thinking.

12 May 2026Announced — Budget night
Q2 2026 — nowAnnounced, not yet binding
1 July 2027Changes take effect

Negative gearing

From 1 July 2027, investors who buy established homes after 12 May 2026 can no longer offset rental losses against their wages. Properties owned before Budget night are fully grandfathered, and new builds stay fully negatively gearable — a deliberate nudge toward new supply.

Capital gains tax

The 50% CGT discount is replaced by cost-base indexation plus a 30% minimum tax on net gains, for gains accruing from 1 July 2027. Gains built up before that date keep the old treatment.

The Commonwealth Bank estimates the package leaves established house prices about 3% lower than they otherwise would have been, with a smaller effect on rents. The southern Gold Coast's $700,000–$1,300,000 mid-market has drawn steady investor interest, so this is the segment most exposed — while first-home buyers are being pushed the other way: the 5% deposit guarantee had its income and place caps removed in October 2025, and the Help to Buy shared-equity scheme (QLD price cap $1,000,000) now reaches all three suburbs.

What the data shows — and what it can't. In the two months after the announcement, volumes fell and listings rose across all three suburbs. But three rate rises and the tax announcement landed in the very same window, so the slowdown can't be pinned on any single cause — and because the change doesn't bind until 2027, its fuller effect on established-property demand is still ahead, not in these prints. We've shown the overlap rather than pretend one explanation.

Why markets go quiet

When rates rise and the rules change at once, buyers and sellers both wait — not because anyone is being foolish, but because uncertainty has a real cost, and waiting is how a market absorbs it. Thinner volume is what caution looks like in the data. It is a normal phase of a cycle, not a verdict on value.

For a market where investors have been active buyers, the question isn't whether the 2027 change matters — it's how much of it has already been priced in.

What this report won't tell you

It won't tell you what your home is worth — that takes the specifics of your street and your house, not a suburb median. It won't predict where prices go next; we report indicators and use conditional language, never forecasts. And it can't fully separate the effect of three rate rises from the 12 May tax announcement — both landed in the same three months, so we've shown you the overlap rather than pick a story.
