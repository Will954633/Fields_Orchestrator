The quarter

A loud sale in a quiet market

On a Friday afternoon in April, on a lakefront street in Robina, bidding closed on a home with twenty-two metres of water frontage. The hammer fell at $2,410,000 — more than double what it last changed hands for in 2020. It was one of the loudest moments of the quarter. Most of the quarter was quiet.

Judged by the trailing-twelve-month median — the measure that doesn't lurch on a handful of sales — prices in all three suburbs held or rose. Judged by how many homes actually sold, the quarter was among the thinnest in years: turnover ran roughly half its year-earlier level, listings built up, and homes took longer to find a buyer. With the cash rate at 4.35% after three rises, and a tax change announced mid-quarter, the pressure landed on activity, not price.

When this report last read the market, at the close of Q1, the Conviction Index sat at 100.1 — right on the line between balanced and cooling. A quarter later it reads 94.4. Nothing here suggests prices falling; it suggests a market catching its breath.

What firmed

  • Trailing-12-month median still rising in all three suburbs
  • Wages (+3.4%) and household spending — the best leading indicators — both climbing
  • Burleigh Waters' average time-to-sell fell sharply (60.8 → 42.5 days)

What softened

  • Single-quarter sales down 44–56% on a year earlier
  • Listings building; days-on-market lengthening in Robina and Varsity Lakes
  • The Conviction Index below its 100 baseline

What to watch

  • The 1 July 2027 negative-gearing & CGT changes (p.5)
  • The 11 August RBA decision; Q2 CPI lands 29 July
  • Whether sellers meet the market, or buyers return first

What activity did — the real story

Fewer sales, slower sales

Quarterly house sales, deduplicated. Q2 2026 ran below each suburb's five-year average and well below a year earlier.
Median days-on-market. Robina and Varsity Lakes have slowed; Burleigh Waters sits near its long-run median.
Reading the days-on-market curve
Time-on-market is the market's patience gauge. A home's clock runs longest in its first weeks; interest is highest early, then eases. When the median stretches — Robina from 24 to 38 days — more homes sit past that early window before a buyer commits. Prices can hold while this happens: buyers taking longer to decide, not sellers dropping their price.

Turnover ran roughly half its year-earlier level in Robina and Varsity Lakes, and down 44% in Burleigh Waters. Fewer homes traded, and the ones that did took longer — yet cleared at firm prices.

When homes take longer to sell but prices hold, someone is being patient. The data doesn't yet say who blinks.

The rule change beneath the numbers

A tax reform enters the calculus

On Budget night, 12 May 2026, the Federal Government announced the biggest change to property tax settings in a generation. It doesn't take effect until 1 July 2027 — but it landed inside this quarter, and it is already part of how buyers and sellers are thinking.

12 May 2026Announced — Budget night
Q2 2026 — nowAnnounced, not yet binding
1 July 2027Changes take effect

Negative gearing

From 1 July 2027, investors who buy established homes after 12 May 2026 can no longer offset rental losses against wages. Homes owned before Budget night are grandfathered, and new builds stay fully negatively gearable.

Capital gains tax

The 50% CGT discount is replaced by cost-base indexation plus a 30% minimum tax on net gains, for gains accruing from 1 July 2027. Earlier gains keep the old treatment.

The Commonwealth Bank estimates the package leaves established house prices about 3% lower than they otherwise would have been. The southern Gold Coast's $700,000–$1,300,000 mid-market has drawn steady investor interest, so it is the segment most exposed — while first-home buyers are pushed the other way: the 5% deposit guarantee had its caps removed in October 2025, and Help to Buy (QLD cap $1,000,000) now reaches all three suburbs.

What the data shows — and can't. In the two months after the announcement, volumes fell and listings rose. But three rate rises and the tax news landed in the same window, so the slowdown can't be pinned on one cause — and because the change doesn't bind until 2027, its fuller effect is still ahead. We've shown the overlap rather than pick a story.

Why markets go quiet

When rates rise and the rules change at once, buyers and sellers both wait — not because anyone is being foolish, but because uncertainty has a real cost, and waiting is how a market absorbs it. Thinner volume is what caution looks like in the data — a normal phase of a cycle, not a verdict on value.

Why this report exists

About the author

Fields Estate started with a story about vulnerability — an agent who underquoted my own home by 17%, and a property recommended to us at $1,100,000–$1,200,000 that sold for $1,400,000.

That experience became the reason Fields exists. I built it to give homeowners something I didn't have: data. Not opinions, not the reassuring-but-vague "the market is strong" that agents offer regardless of conditions — actual, verifiable, local analysis that turns a vulnerable seller into an informed one.

Fields analyses sales, market trends and agent performance across the southern Gold Coast, and maintains comparable-sales valuations in Robina, Varsity Lakes and Burleigh Waters. The information asymmetry between agents and homeowners is the single biggest risk to a fair sale price. Reducing it is what Fields does.

I live on the southern Gold Coast. I walk these streets. I photograph these suburbs. I study these markets because they are my community — and the people selling their homes deserve better than what the industry currently provides.
