How to read this report.

Three minutes here will save you fifteen later. Plain-English explanations of the four numbers we lean on most. The technical names are in brackets — for the readers who want them.

The Fields Conviction Index (94.5 this issue)

One number we publish every quarter. We've designed it so that 100 means "a typical southern Gold Coast quarter pre-COVID". Anything above 100 means tighter than that — sellers in charge. Below 100 means looser — buyers in charge. This issue: 94.5.

We build it from three measurable things: prices, sales volume, and how long homes take to sell. Each is converted to a standard score against five years of its own history, then weighted and combined.

Formal: a weighted composite of indexed median price (40%/50%), latest-quarter sales-volume z-score (20%/25%), and inverse days-on-market z-score (20%/25%), rebased Q1 2020 = 100.

The rolling 12-month median

The middle sale price across the last twelve months. We prefer it to a single-quarter median because twelve months gives us a sample of 80-200 sales per suburb; a single quarter gives us 20-50. One $5M sale moves a small sample dramatically; the 12-month series barely flinches.

When we report a "year-on-year" growth number, this is what we compare to the same number a year ago.

Formal: median of all arms-length house transactions over the trailing 12 months, recomputed monthly.

The composite

When we say "the southern Gold Coast composite", we mean Robina, Burleigh Waters and Varsity Lakes combined, weighted by how many transactions each suburb produced. So Robina pulls more weight than Varsity Lakes (it has more sales), but no suburb is left out.

The composite is for the broad picture. The suburb pages are where individual stories live.

Formal: transaction-weighted aggregate of the three core suburbs.

Sample size and what we don't trust

Every chart in this report cites how many sales went into it. Below 30 sales in a quarter, we flag the number as small-sample and don't make strong claims off it. The rolling 12-month series is what we trust at suburb level; the single-quarter prints are colour and texture only.

We also flag when our data sources disagree, and we don't resolve disagreements by picking a winner — we report the gap and explain it.

Formal: minimum N≥30 for quarterly reporting, N≥100 for confidence-banded statistics.

The gap between the headline and your street.

If you have read this month that the property market is falling, you are not wrong — nationally. Sydney and Melbourne values slipped again in June. Listings across the country are at a six-year high. More than one auction in five is now being withdrawn before it runs. And a tax change most people have not had the time to understand is now law. It is a genuinely unsettling picture, and the anxiety it is producing is reasonable.

But you do not live in the national market. On the southern Gold Coast, house prices are up between 10.9% and 15.5% on a year ago. Vendors here are discounting between 0.4% and 2.3% off their asking price — a fraction of the 3.6% now typical across the capital cities. Homes are still selling inside four months of listing. The fear is real. It is just, for the most part, happening somewhere else.

That does not mean nothing has changed here. Fewer homes are selling, and the ones that do are taking longer — median time on the market in Robina has stretched from twenty-five days to thirty-eight over the year. And from 1 July 2027, the way investors are taxed on established homes changes materially. This issue is about holding both of those truths at once: a local market still firmly on the front foot, cooling at the edges, under a national cloud that has not — yet — reached the coast.

What you're hearing.

A fair account of the national slowdown — because the honest place to start is with the news that is worrying people, not around it.

The national headlines are not wrong. Through the middle of 2026 the two largest markets have gone backwards: Sydney dwelling values fell 1.2% in June and now sit almost 4% below their January peak; Melbourne fell 1.0%. The number of homes for sale nationally has climbed to a six-year high — up 7.7% on a year ago — handing buyers the kind of choice they have not had in years.

The auction market tells the bluntest version of the story. The national clearance rate has fallen to around 43%, its weakest May result since 2020, and more than one auction in five is now withdrawn before it runs — in Sydney and Melbourne, closer to two in five. The tax change everyone's talking about.

On Budget night the Federal Government announced the biggest change to property tax in a generation. It is now law, it begins on 1 July 2027 — and it is the one piece of the national story that will, eventually, reach the coast.

Two settings that have shaped Australian property investment for decades are being wound back. Both were announced on 12 May 2026, both are now legislated, and both begin on 1 July 2027.

Negative gearingCapital gains tax
From 1 July 2027, an investor who buys an established home after 7:30pm on 12 May 2026 can no longer offset rental losses against their wages. Losses can still be carried forward against future rental income or the eventual capital gain. The 50% discount on assets held more than a year is replaced by cost-base indexation plus a 30% minimum tax on net gains — for gains that accrue from 1 July 2027. Gains built up before that date keep the old treatment.

Two carve-outs matter. Homes already owned at Budget night are fully grandfathered — nothing changes for them until they are sold. And new builds stay fully negatively gearable, with the 50% discount intact — a deliberate nudge to steer investors toward adding supply rather than competing for existing homes.

What it means for your suburb.

The reform is national, but it does not land evenly. It falls hardest on exactly the kind of stock the southern Gold Coast has plenty of — and it pushes first-home buyers the other way.

The Commonwealth Bank estimates the package leaves established dwelling values around 3% lower than they otherwise would have been, and has trimmed its national price-growth forecast for the year from 5% to 3%. That is a headwind, not a cliff — and it is still eighteen months away.

Where it concentrates locally is the $700,000 to $1,300,000 mid-market — the price band where investors have been most active across Robina, Burleigh Waters and Varsity Lakes. From 2027, an established home in that band becomes a less efficient investment than it is today, while a new build in the same band keeps every advantage. Over time, that is likely to tilt investor demand toward new stock, and to leave more established homes for owner-occupiers and first-home buyers.

And first-home buyers are being pushed forward, hard. The 5% deposit guarantee had its income and place caps removed in October 2025; the Help to Buy shared-equity scheme, with a Queensland price cap of $1,000,000, now reaches all three of our suburbs. For an owner wondering who will buy their home in the years ahead, the answer is shifting — not disappearing.

What the data shows — and what it can't

Three rate rises and this announcement landed in the same few months, so the quarter's slowdown cannot be pinned on any single cause. Because the change does not bind until 2027, its fuller effect on established-property demand is still ahead — not in the numbers you are reading today. When rates rise and the rules change at once, buyers and sellers both wait; thinner volume is what caution looks like in the data, not a verdict on value.

94.5 — the index slipped below the line.

The Conviction Index is our single gauge of how firm the market is — it blends price, sales volume and how fast homes sell into one number, where 100 is a typical, balanced quarter. At 94.5 it has dropped decisively below that line — its softest reading in seven years. It reads like a warning. Look closely and it is mostly telling you one thing: fewer homes are changing hands.

94.5 sits below the five-year baseline of 100, in the lower part of its historical range. It is now the lowest the index has read since 2019. That sounds ominous until you look at what happened last time: the composite spent 2018 and 2019 in the low 90s — bottoming at 90 in late 2018 — then climbed back above 100 and held there for six years. Even the 2022 rate shock never pushed it below 100; at its post-COVID peak it reached 118. So this is a real cooling — the softest reading in seven years — but in this index, soft has meant quiet, not falling: closer to "quiet" than to "weak".

Here is the apparent contradiction the index resolves. The number is below baseline — yet every suburb in this report is still, on the ground, a seller's market: homes are clearing in under four months and vendors are conceding less than 2.5% on price. Both are true. The index fell because of momentum — sales volumes thinned and homes took longer to sell. The balance of supply and demand, measured by how quickly the available stock is being absorbed, still sits firmly in sellers' favour. The index measures the market's pace; absorption measures its balance. This quarter, the pace eased while the balance held.

The Standoff Tilts

Last quarter we called this a standoff — prices firm, momentum just below the line, neither side breaking. A quarter on, the activity side has moved hard, and prices have not followed. The lines did not reconverge. The gap widened.

When the price line and the conviction line separate

The two lines tracked closely through the first three quarters of 2023 — both staying within ~1.5 points of one another. They began separating in Q4 2023, when the indexed median pulled ahead of the Conviction Index by seven points. The gap widened progressively: ~18 points by Q4 2024, ~30 points by Q3 2025, peaking near 43 points in Q4 2025. Q1 2026 closed near the 100 baseline; Q2 2026 then broke sharply lower, the Conviction Index falling to 94.5 — its softest reading in seven years — while the indexed median held near its high. The gap between the two lines is now close to its widest.

The data behind the standoff:

  1. Sales volume. 85 transactions across the three suburbs combined in Q2 2026, against a five-year same-quarter average of approximately 128 — a 34% shortfall, and the sharpest single signal in this report. Robina fell furthest (30 sales versus a 54.2 average, 44% below). Varsity Lakes ran thin at 15. Burleigh Waters was the exception — volume recovered to 40, near its 46.2 average.
  2. Prices. Medians by suburb: Robina $1,450,000 (+10.9% year-on-year), Burleigh Waters $1,710,000 (+12.2%), Varsity Lakes $1,350,000 (+15.5%). Across the three combined, growth runs at approximately +12.9% — roughly double the 5-year average annual growth of 6.7%.
  3. Time-to-sell (days-on-market). 33 days median across the three suburbs, lifted from a 5-year median of roughly 24 days. Robina lengthened most, to 37.5 days; Burleigh Waters and Varsity Lakes sit closer to their historical norms.
  4. The Fields Conviction Index. 94.5 for the three combined — the softest reading since 2019. Robina at 89.6, Burleigh Waters at 94.8; Varsity Lakes the only suburb above the 100 baseline at 103.3.

In Issue 01 we called this a standoff and said the data could not yet tell us which side would break. One quarter on, it has told us something: the activity side moved first — volume fell hard while price held. What it has not told us is whether price follows. Two scenarios resolve it. In the first, stock-on-market keeps shrinking while transaction volume recovers — buyers absorbing the scarce listings; historically that combination has preceded renewed price acceleration. In the second, volume recovers because sellers capitulate on price — the same pickup in sales, but with prices easing rather than accelerating. This quarter did not decide it. It did rule out complacency.

Between now and the next edition we watch four numbers: how much stock sits on market (stock-on-market), what share of asking price vendors actually achieve (sale-to-list ratio), what share of listings getpulled within 60 days, and how lenders respond to the new APRA debt-to-income lending cap. Each will be reported next quarter.

LEADING INDICATORS The Fields Quarterly · Q2 2026

What moves before prices move.

Most market commentary mistakes confirmation for prediction. By the time interest-rate cuts have moved house prices, the market has already moved — for between three and twelve months. The indicators that genuinely lead prices are different from the ones the news leads with.

This report's underlying methodology leans on Australian property economics — particularly Abelson,Joyeux & Knight (2005), which formally established the correlations and lead-times below for the Australian housing market. Where else where in this report we say "the data is consistent with…", this is the framework we are leaning on.

The strongest forward-looking indicator we know of is wage growth. Wages have led residential propertyprices in Australia by 3 to 4 months with a correlation of 0.94 — the kind of statistical relationship thatdoesn't show up by accident. Household spending follows almost in real time (correlation 0.91). Interestrates and credit growth, by contrast, lag prices — by 12 months and 3.5 months respectively. They confirm; they do not predict.

It is the question underneath every headline you have read this quarter. We cannot tell you what to do — and we won't. But the data can frame the decision honestly.

METHODOLOGY The Fields Quarterly · Q2 2026

How every chart in this report is made.

A first-class chapter, not an appendix. Trust scales with disclosure; we publish what we measure and how we measure it.

The Fields Conviction Index

A composite of three components, each z-scored against its own rolling 5-year history, then weighted and rebased so that Q1 2020 = 100. The components are: indexed median price (50% weight), latest-quarter sales volume (25%), and inverse days-on-market (25%) where DOM data is available. Where DOM data isnot available (pre-Q2 2023), the index is computed on the two-component basis (67% price, 33% volume).The four-component FCI specified in the methodology document — adding sale-to-list ratio and stock-on-market — will be live from Issue 02 once historical backfill is complete.

Indexed prices

Hedonic regression at suburb level. Each transaction generates an attribute-adjusted index point; the index is the time-series of those points smoothed to quarterly. Methodology cross-validated against the Cotality Hedonic Home Value Index at the SA2 level.

Comparable-sales valuations

Per property: 3-8 comparables matched on distance ≤500m, recency ≤6 months, beds/baths, floor area±15%, land size ±20%, condition, position, view. Each comp adjusted; weighted mean used; 90% confidence interval is 1.645 × weighted standard deviation. Properties listing ≥$2,500,000 flagged as directional_only — comp scarcity widens the CI; shown as a range only.

Days-on-market

First-list date to unconditional contract. Excludes properties withdrawn within 14 days. Excludes family transfers under $200,000 and probate sales without arms-length pricing.

Sales volume

Houses only, deduplicated across three sources (Domain, REA, on-property), taking max per quarter to avoid double-counting. The in-progress current quarter is excluded from this report.

Sample sizes

Any quarter with fewer than 30 transactions for a suburb is flagged. Charts cite the underlying N. Confidence intervals widen with sample size; we report N every time.

Where we differ from Cotality, Domain, PropTrack and SQM

Each source answers a slightly different question. Cotality models attribute-adjusted hedonic prices onsettled sales; we use the same approach at suburb level and reconcile against their published series.Domain reports stratified medians on settled sales; we use these as a postcode-level sanity check. PropTrack and SQM track asking prices on currently listed stock; we treat asking-price indices as a negotiability signal rather than as price-trend evidence. The four-source reconciliation chart is in scope for Issue 02.

GLOSSARY The Fields Quarterly · Q2 2026

The terms we use, defined.

A one-glance reference for the technical terms in this report. The plain-English meaning is given first; the formalname is in italics.

The Fields Conviction Index (FCI)
One number, published every quarter, that captures the underlying strength of the southern Gold Coast property market. 100 is a typical pre-COVID baseline; above 115 is tight; below 85 favours buyers. Formal: a transaction-weighted weighted composite of three components, each z-scored against five years of history.
Composite
Robina, Burleigh Waters and Varsity Lakes combined into one number, weighted by how many transactions each suburb produced. Formal: transaction-weighted aggregate.
Indexed price
The price level after adjusting for the mix of houses sold each quarter, so a quarter heavy with prestige sales does not fake a price boom. Formal: hedonic regression-based price index, the same statistical method CoreLogic / Cotality use.
Rolling 12-month median
The middle sale price across the most recent twelve months, recomputed monthly. We trust it more than a single-quarter median because the sample size is larger. Formal: trailing-twelve-month median, recomputed at month-end.
Z-score
A measure of how unusual a reading is against its own history. A z-score of +1 means "one standard deviation above the five-year average" — uncommon but not rare. +2 means strongly above; −2 means strongly below. Formal: standardised score, (current value − mean) ÷ standard deviation.
Sale-to-list ratio
The final sale price as a share of the property's first listed price. 100% means the home sold at ask; 95% means it sold 5% below; 102% means it sold above. Formal: median (sale ÷ first list) across closed transactions.
Days-on-market
How long a home took to sell, measured from first listing to unconditional contract. We report the median across each quarter so a few outliers do not distort the figure. Formal: list-to-unconditional median, private-treaty + auction combined.
Sample size (N)
How many transactions a number is based on. Below 30, we flag the figure as small-sample and do not make strong claims off it. Formal: transaction count for the relevant period and segment.
